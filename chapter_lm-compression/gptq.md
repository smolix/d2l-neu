**Idea:** Rounding a layer's weights is a least-squares problem whose Hessian is the input covariance, and rounding one column at a time while compensating the rest with that Hessian loses far less than rounding all at once.
**Opening:** The reader has 11.2's calibrated grids and knows that round-to-nearest at three bits is much worse than at four. The opening cell takes a row of six weights with a known input covariance, rounds it to a 2-bit grid three ways (nearest, one column at a time with compensation, and exhaustive search over all grid points) and prints the three reconstruction losses: the compensated rounding is far below nearest and close to the exhaustive optimum. The question is where the compensation comes from and whether it scales to a 6,144-column layer, and the closing question is whether a better local loss is a better model.
**Teaches:**
- The layer-wise objective `‖X Wᵀ − X Ŵᵀ‖²` with `H = XᵀX` shared by every output row, its conditioning (`κ(XᵀX) = κ(X)²`), rank deficiency when calibration tokens are fewer than input channels, and damping as a ridge that pulls the solution toward nearest rounding.
- The Optimal Brain Surgeon step: fixing one weight to a grid point costs `Δ²/[H_F⁻¹]_qq` and the remaining free weights move along a column of `H_F⁻¹`; the Schur-complement update that removes the fixed index.
- GPTQ's three changes: a fixed column order so one Cholesky factor of `H⁻¹` supplies every update, lazy block updates that turn rank-one updates into matrix products, and the dampening constant; act-order as the one-difference variant and what it does to kernel layouts.
- Calibration as part of the method: size, domain and the "true sequential" propagation of quantization through the block; the random-token control.
- The local loss as a proxy: per-layer loss ratios predict perplexity only loosely; the single-site swap that tests which layers matter.
**Derivation:** The Optimal Brain Surgeon update from a quadratic with one equality constraint (Lagrangian, closed form), then the GPTQ column sweep: with the columns processed in a fixed order the needed entries of the successive inverses are the rows of one upper Cholesky factor of `H⁻¹`, so a single factorization replaces a sequence of inverses. Smallest case: the 2 by 2 weight row against every grid point, drawn with the loss ellipse of `H`.

**Writer notes:** Accumulate `H` in float32 from BF16 inputs with a running normalization (`d2l.accumulate_hessian`); BF16 accumulation overflows or loses the small eigenvalues. Collecting `H` from the unquantized model's activations (`sequential=False`) is a legal variant with a different, usually worse, result; the notebook runs the sequential variant and names the other. Act-order with groups breaks the contiguous-group layout fast kernels expect unless static groups or a `g_idx` are supported; say so where the packed checkpoint is written. Zero "pruned" entries explicitly after a sweep (rounding leaves `1e-18`). The per-column loop is slow in eager PyTorch (6,144 iterations for `down_proj`): lazy blocks of 128 keep it within budget; fall back to `torch.compile` on the inner loop or to the `FAST` flag (first 8 blocks) with the full result committed. The largest Hessian (`down_proj`, 6144 squared, 151 MB in float32) fits; keep it on the host and move it for the Cholesky if 10 GiB is exceeded. Reference implementation and tolerance: brute force on rows of at most eight weights (exact comparison of losses; GPTQ's loss must be at most that of nearest rounding and is allowed to exceed the exhaustive optimum); optionally the IST-DASLab `gptq` reference line by line as the convention oracle (symmetric against asymmetric grids, zero-point rounding, group-scale rule); the published `Qwen/Qwen3-1.7B-GPTQ-Int8` configuration is read only for its settings table `tab_lm_gptq_defaults`. The WikiText-2 train against test ablation is an in-domain effect, not generalization; say so. Do not re-teach: conditioning, Cholesky, 11.2's rescaling.
