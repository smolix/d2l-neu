**Idea:** Removing weights removes work only when the pattern of removal is one the hardware or the dense kernel can skip, so the same reconstruction machinery that chose codes in 11.3 chooses zeros here, and the patterns are judged by measured speed.
**Opening:** The reader has 11.3's column sweep and the fact that half of a layer's weights are small. The opening cell sets half of Qwen3-1.7B's weights to zero by magnitude, prints the perplexity (ruined), then the decode tokens per second (unchanged, because a zero in a dense BF16 matrix costs the same bytes). The question is which weights can go and which patterns of zeros a kernel can skip, and the closing question is what recovers a model that has lost whole layers.
**Teaches:**
- Four granularities of removal (unstructured, 2:4 semi-structured, channel or neuron, whole block) and what each changes in bytes and arithmetic; why a sparse mask alone changes nothing on a dense kernel.
- Saliency: magnitude; Wanda's `|w|·‖x_j‖` with a diagonal Hessian and no update, compared per output row; SparseGPT as 11.3's sweep with a zero grid and mask selection per block (restated, not re-derived); why per-row comparison matters.
- The 2:4 pattern: two nonzeros per four consecutive inputs, 9 bits per BF16 weight once metadata is counted (a byte ratio of 0.5625), doubled throughput on sparse tensor cores for prefill and large batches, and little at batch-1 decode; conversion with `torch.sparse` and what the backend reports.
- Structured width and depth pruning (neuron importance from the SwiGLU product, block influence from the residual stream's cosine change) and recovery by distillation from the original model (`d2l.kd_loss`, 9.1); the only variants that speed everything up without special kernels; Minitron as the scale reference.
- The chapter's closing table of every compressed checkpoint (quality with intervals, bytes, measured speed) and the decision table for a deployment target.
**Derivation:** The cost of removal with and without compensation: for positive definite `H`, `[H⁻¹]_jj ≥ 1/H_jj` with equality only when `e_j` is an eigenvector, so zeroing weight `j` with no update costs `w_j² H_jj` (Wanda's score up to the row's activation norms) and with optimal compensation `w_j²/[H⁻¹]_jj`, which is never more; the factor `H_jj [H⁻¹]_jj` is large when the input channel is correlated with others, which compensation exploits and Wanda cannot. Smallest case: a 2 by 2 `H` with correlation 0.9. Second, short: the 2:4 byte ratio and the bounds it puts on decode (1.78 times) and prefill (2 times) speedups.
