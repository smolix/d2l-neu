**Idea:** The attention output moves by at most twice the dropped attention mass times the largest value norm, so a policy that keeps the entries that future queries will attend to can drop or skip the rest, and its failures appear on tasks a single needle never tests.
**Opening:** The reader has 12.6's four-bit cache, which cuts bytes by four and no further. The opening cell shows, for one layer of Qwen3-4B over a 4,096-token prompt, that most of the attention mass of the last 32 queries falls on a few hundred entries, and that those entries are nearly the same for the next 32 queries. The question is whether the rest can be dropped, and the closing question is what happens when a second, different question arrives over the same context.
**Teaches:**
- The error bound for dropping a set of entries and why keeping the largest-mass entries minimizes it; the persistence hypothesis (accumulated past attention predicts future attention, H2O) and the observation-window hypothesis (the last queries preview what the answer needs, SnapKV with pooling); sinks plus a recent window as the training-free baseline (restated from `subsec_kv-cache-sinks`).
- Implementation under GQA: scores aggregated over the query heads of a group; kept columns keep their original positions and rotations (eviction never renumbers; `kv_len` against `pos`); the explicit-attention path needed to read probabilities that FlashAttention never materializes, and its prefill cost.
- Reading less without storing less: query-dependent page selection (Quest) keeps every byte and reads only the pages whose key bounds can score highly, so a later question still finds its evidence; the one difference from eviction and what it costs per query.
- Measuring capture before accuracy: the fraction of the future queries' attention mass that a kept set captures, per policy and budget, against an oracle that knows the future, and against a different question over the same context.
- Evaluation beyond a needle: single and multi-key needles, variable tracking, aggregation over the whole context, one- and two-hop QA, and a second question over a compressed context; why a needle-only ranking of methods can be wrong.
**Derivation:** The eviction bound. With kept set `K`, dropped set `E` of attention mass `m`, and `ô` the renormalized output over `K`, `‖o − ô‖ ≤ m · max_{t ∈ E} ‖v_t − ô‖ ≤ 2 m max_t ‖v_t‖`; the bound is tight when one dropped entry's value points away from the rest. Smallest case: three entries with masses `(0.6, 0.3, 0.1)` and the third dropped.

**Writer notes:** A needle test rewards whatever keeps what the question asks for and says nothing about aggregation, multi-hop or a later question; the two-question task exists to show this and must stay. The question comes last (the RULER order) except in the one labelled ablation. Question-aware compression applied before a different question arrives is the central failure and is measured, not argued. Evicting entries must not renumber positions; the new token's position is `pos`, not `kv_len`. Prefill with explicit attention for the column sums is much slower than the fused prefill; prefill once, branch many, and print the projected cost. Page selection reads per-page key bounds from the page-aware kernel of 12.2 (block size 16); the bounds are computed once at prefill and updated per appended page. Random eviction is the control that every policy must beat. Methods that need training (sparse attention with a learned indexer, hybrids) are Chapter 14; the text says once that these policies apply to any checkpoint. Do not re-teach: sinks, online softmax, 12.6's quantizer.
