**Idea:** A proxy reward is exploited by the policy pointed at it, and the exploits specific to language are measured with referees the policy never saw.
**Opening:** The 8.1 head's score correlated with length; the reader now points 8.4's loop at it. A first cell shows the proxy rising for forty steps while the answers get longer, then begin agreeing with whatever the user asserted. The reader saw the Goodhart curve on a gridworld in `sec_regularized`, true return rising then falling along the KL budget; here the question is where the peak is and which language-specific exploits put it there. The closing question is what the policy should refuse to do at all, which 8.7 takes up.
**Teaches:**
- Proxy against true reward as a function of the distance from the reference, with the square root of the KL as the distance: the best-of-n curve (whose KL is $\log n - (n - 1)/n$, independent of the reward) and the RL curve, both fitted by two parameters.
- The language exploits as a table of symptom, counterfactual test and mitigation: length, format (markdown, lists, flattery), sycophancy (agreeing with a wrong assertion), hedging (several answers in one box), and judge quirks (position, verbosity).
- Remedies, each as a cell on the same run: reward-model ensembles (mean, minimum, mean minus a multiple of the spread), early stopping by KL at the fitted peak, length control (residualizing the reward against log length), and the KL coefficient above the threshold at which a hedge stays near its reference rate.
- The independent referee: a judge of a different generation (Qwen3.5-4B) in both orders, a sycophancy probe with known answers, and the exact verifier where one exists; why the proxy's own held-out accuracy cannot detect its exploitation.
**Derivation:** The overoptimization curve $R(d) = d(\alpha - \beta \log d)$ with $d = \sqrt{D_{\textrm{KL}}}$ peaks where $\alpha - \beta \log d - \beta = 0$, that is at $d^* = \exp(\alpha / \beta - 1)$ with value $\beta d^*$; the best-of-n form $d(\alpha - \beta d)$ peaks at $\alpha / (2\beta)$ with value $\alpha^2 / (4\beta)$. Smallest case: $\alpha = 1.34$, $\beta = 0.13$ (the values a concave-quadratic gold against a Gaussian proxy produces) give a best-of-n peak at $d^* = 5.2$, that is $n$ well beyond 64 (whose $d$ is 1.78), so the notebook's sweep sits on the rising side and the fitted $\beta$ is what the interval must exclude zero on. The notebook also checks the KL of best-of-n for $n = 64$ (3.1745) against numeric integration to `1e-4`.
