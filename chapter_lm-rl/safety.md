**Idea:** A model's safety is a pair of error rates, harmful compliance and over-refusal, measured at rest and under attack with sets whose categories and rubric are public.
**Opening:** After 7.2 the model answers everything, including requests it should decline; after a fine-tune on a safety slice it also declines a cooking question because it mentions a knife. The reader has measured accuracy with intervals in Chapter 1; a refusal rate is the same kind of number, except that it only means something next to its complement and under attack. The closing question is what happens when the harmful instruction arrives inside a tool's output rather than from the user, which 10.5 takes up.
**Teaches:**
- Two sets and two rates: a should-refuse set (categories, a judge rubric and aggregate rates published; the prompts private) and a should-comply set of benign prompts with alarming surface features (public); harmful compliance and over-refusal as paired, clustered error rates with intervals, reported as a vector, never as one score.
- Three jailbreak families and why they work: role-play and persona prompts that pit helpfulness against the refusal (competing objectives), encodings, translations and few-shot patterns the safety training did not cover (mismatched generalization), and optimized adversarial suffixes transferred from public attack suites; attack success rate measured per family before and after the safety fine-tune.
- Memorization and extraction on a model whose training data is known: prefixes from the SFT training conversations against prefixes from the held-out split, verbatim-continuation rate as the extraction rate, and what it says about privacy.
- In one paragraph each: bias measurement with ambiguous and disambiguated questions, and training against written principles (a constitution that labels pairs, deliberative alignment that reasons over the rules), with the principle-labelled judge's agreement with dataset labels as the one number.
**Derivation:** none.

- Attack suites: use HarmBench's public behaviours and its transferred suffixes rather than optimizing suffixes live (no attack generation code in the book); the role-play and encoding families are template-based and committed. Attack success is judged with the same rubric; report per family with intervals, and never a single pooled rate.
- Over-refusal needs its own set: XSTest's 250 safe prompts (public) plus the book's 100; the refusal regex is a pre-filter only, the judge decides. A falling harmful-compliance rate read alone is the pitfall the section exists to prevent.
- Extraction: the SFT training set gives known membership, so the comparison is training prefixes against held-out prefixes of the same distribution; a 50-token verbatim match is the criterion (Carlini et al.); never print an extracted sample that contains personal data (the SFT mixture is public, but filter anyway).
