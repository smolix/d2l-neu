**Idea:** For decode, tensor parallelism trades bytes per GPU for a collective's latency on every layer, and expert parallelism keeps each request's cache local while pooling the batch that the experts see, so the two layouts win in different regimes of batch and load.
**Opening:** The reader has a 30-billion-parameter mixture model whose 61 GB of weights do not fit one 40 GB GPU. The opening cell prints, from the configuration and the datasheet alone, what one 8-GPU node holds under five layouts (replicas, tensor-parallel groups of 2, 4 and 8, and data-parallel attention with the experts spread over 8): weights per GPU, cache bytes per token per GPU, total cache tokens; the first layout does not fit, the others differ by about five times in cache capacity, and the 8-way tensor split stores every KV head twice. The question is which layout serves a workload best and how to know without trying all of them, and the closing question is whether prefill and decode should share a layout at all.
**Teaches:**
- Why serving differs from training: no backward pass, no optimizer state, tiny messages (a decode step moves `b × d` activations), and an objective that is a latency percentile, so the collective's latency `α` matters as much as its bandwidth; 96 all-reduces or all-to-alls per decode step of a 48-layer model.
- Tensor parallelism for decode: two all-reduces per layer, bytes per rank and in aggregate, the KV head replication when the degree exceeds the KV heads, the inefficient expert tile when an expert's width is split eight ways, and why the degree stays inside the fast link domain.
- Expert parallelism with data-parallel attention: each rank owns requests, their cache and replicated attention weights and shares the experts; dispatch and combine as all-to-alls with destination deduplication; the pooled batch the experts see; dummy steps on idle ranks.
- The decode paradox of mixtures: at moderate batch nearly every expert is touched, so weight traffic approaches the whole model; tokens per expert against the ridge (about `ridge × E/k` tokens per step), which is why wide expert parallelism exists; load imbalance (the straggler factor) and hot-expert replication (EPLB); recorded routing on real text as the only honest input.
- Capacity per layout (weights, replicated KV heads, cache tokens) and the fitted mixture step model that predicts step time from the layout and the link table; production forms (wide EP across nodes, DeepEP kernels) as the case studies the formulas extend to.
**Derivation:** Traffic and distinct experts. Under tensor parallelism of degree `t`, each rank sends `8 d (t − 1)/t` bytes per token per layer (two ring all-reduces of `n d` elements); under expert parallelism of degree `e` with `E` experts and top-`k`, the expected number of remote destinations is `(e − 1)(1 − C(E − E/e, k)/C(E, k))`, so a token sends `2(e − 1)p × 2d` bytes per layer; a step of `n` tokens touches `E(1 − (1 − k/E)^n)` experts; the busiest of `e` ranks carries about `1 + z_e √((e − 1)/(nk))` times the mean load. Smallest case: `d = 2048`, `t = 8` gives 14,336 bytes per rank and 114,688 in aggregate; `E = 128`, `k = 8`, `e = 8` gives 4.67 remote destinations and about 38 KB per token per layer, three times less than the tensor split; `n = 16` touches 82 of 128 experts; on an A100 the experts reach the ridge at about 3,210 tokens per step.

**Writer notes:** "More GPUs, lower latency" holds only while the collective latency is small against the step; at small batch the latency, not the bandwidth, is the cost. Tensor degree above the KV head count replicates the cache (4 heads here). Compare layouts on the throughput-latency plane or at equal fractions of each layout's own saturation; equal offered load hides the crossover. A mixture's decode step is not "3-billion-parameter cheap" at moderate batch. Straggler analysis with uniform routing underestimates imbalance; the recorded routing is the input and the conclusion is stated for its two text domains. Data-parallel attention ranks step together even with no requests (dummy batches). One node only: cross-node expert parallelism and the network are modelled with the hardware chapter's rows and not measured; the LMSYS and DeepSeek write-ups are read as case studies of the same formulas at 96 and more GPUs, not reproduced. The T3 plan is about 85 to 100 minutes (start 3.5 min, probe 1.5, five loads at 1.25, one repeat, per layout) and is calibrated by a dry run; drop one load per layout if over 2 hours. vLLM's expert-parallel and data-parallel flags are verified at the pinned version; SGLang is not needed for any stated claim and is cut from the node run. A100 has no FP8 tensor cores: BF16 only (the FP8 checkpoint would run weight-only through Marlin-class kernels and change the roofline). If a link row is missing, a 3-minute `torch.distributed` microbenchmark refits it with `d2l.fit_alpha_beta`. The T2 pair and the simulator path are the same code under `TIER`; the build box has no usable peer-to-peer, so no multi-GPU run happens there and the notebook says so. Do not re-teach: the mixture layer, ring all-reduce, 6.3 and 6.6's mechanisms. Library additions: `d2l.tp_ep_traffic`, `d2l.moe_routing_stats`, `d2l.expected_distinct_experts`, `d2l.MoEServeStepModel` (a subclass of 13.2's fitted model whose weight-read term counts distinct experts), `ServeStepModel.with_tp(t, link)`; artifacts `lm-serving-t3-logs` (the layout rows), `lm-serving-t2pair-logs` (the tensor rows), `lm-moe-routing-qwen3-30b-a3b`, `lm-moe-routing-olmoe`, the calibrated model JSON, the dry-run calibration.
