# Attention Circuits

Source: `chapter_attention/what-attention-computes.md`. About 5,300 words of prose, 2,050 words of exercises, 16 code cells per framework (PyTorch and JAX only; no TensorFlow or MXNet material).

## Verdict

The best of my three sections, and for most of its length at the bar. It does what the brief asks a teaching section to do and what most treatments of interpretability do not: instead of retelling the induction-head papers, it trains the model, finds the circuit, and then tries four ways to be wrong about it. Two moments are of MacKay quality. The one-block model that solves the fixed-period task perfectly and collapses to chance when the period changes is a control the reader will remember. The ablation that shows the block-1 heads "whose maps looked irrelevant" to be as necessary as the previous-token heads overturns the reading the heatmaps invited, and the text says so. The faults are at the edges. Two mechanistic explanations are asserted beyond what the section measured, and one of them is contradicted by the section's own accuracy figure. The last third (in-context regression, larger models, the debate on attention as explanation) drifts from doing to reporting, and the evidence chain is told three times. The most important fix: hold the closing third to the standard of the first two, by cutting or demoting the in-context regression subsection and by claiming for the circuit only what the four tests showed.

## What works

- The opening states the section's thesis as a problem with the tool the reader has used for three sections: "The map shows only half of a head." It returns to the same point at the end, so the section has an arc.
- "Bigrams, Skip-Trigrams, and the Limits of One Layer". Zero blocks, one block, the task one block cannot do, and why ("The key at position $j$ carries no information about its neighbor at $j - 1$."). The remark that a tied zero-block model has a symmetric Gram matrix of logits, "not an arbitrary $|\mathcal{V}| \times |\mathcal{V}|$ lookup table", is a small precise observation of the kind the reader keeps.
- The distinction drawn at the end of that subsection: "Whether gradient descent finds the circuit is a separate, empirical question". Expressibility and learnability are kept apart, and the experiment answers the second.
- "A Fixed-Offset Solution". The whole subsection, including the explanation of why the bias makes the shortcut easy, and the general statement: "a synthetic benchmark can admit a simpler solution than the mechanism it was designed to test."
- The copy-matrix control that fails to discriminate, reported honestly: "The control copies too." Many texts would have dropped the control.
- "The maps had no way to show this; only the intervention did."
- `tab_induction-evidence`, in particular its "remaining limitation" column. It is the one table in my three sections that teaches method.
- The figures `mdl-attention-induction-circuit.svg` and `mdl-attention-residual-stream.svg` are clear and carry the argument (rendered and checked).

## Structural findings

1. **The last third changes mode.** Up to "Head Ablation" every claim is something the section computed. From "Induction Heads in Larger Models" on, the section reports: what Olsson et al. found, a hand-built regression head, what von Oswald et al. found, attention sinks, Jain and Wallace against Wiegreffe and Pinter, activation patching, the indirect-object circuit, superposition. Each item is accurate as far as I know and short, but together they are about 1,300 words at equal weight, and the reader cannot tell which matter. The further-reading notebook already has "Induction Heads at Scale", "In-Context Learning as an Algorithm", "Patching and Automated Circuit Discovery" and "Superposition and Dictionary Features". Keep in the section only what closes its own argument: one paragraph on larger models, the sink example (it is the sharpest illustration of the thesis), and the evidence table.
2. **"In-Context Regression" is a second topic.** It is not a circuit found in a trained model; it is a hand-set head that evaluates an algebraic identity. Its first cell compares two orderings of the same sum and cannot fail. Its second cell does teach something (the error falls as $1/n$), but about linear regression, not about attention circuits. It also depends on :eqref:`eq_linear-attn` from the previous section. Options, in order of preference: (a) move it to an exercise of this section (the construction is a good four-part problem) and keep one sentence in the text; (b) move it to the Language Models part, where in-context learning will be a topic; (c) keep it, cut the first cell, and state the expected error $(p+1)/n$ so that the second cell tests a prediction (see CIRC-14).
3. **The evidence chain is told three times.** Once as it happens (the four subsections), once in the paragraph that begins "The evidence for the induction circuit in this section comes in levels of increasing strength" together with the table, and once in the Summary ("Attention maps located the candidate heads, the copy matrix showed that they copy, ..."). Keep the table, delete the paragraph before it (it restates the table's rows in prose), and reduce the Summary's version to one sentence.
4. **A slogan repeated.** "where it looks / what it moves" appears in the opening, in the introduction to "Evidence for the Circuit", as the first sentence of "The Copy Matrix", and again in "Attention Weights as Explanations". See CIRC-9.
5. **Length.** At 5,300 words the section is long but does not need a split: with finding 1 and 2 applied it comes to about 4,300. If a split is wanted anyway, the seam is before "In-Context Learning", but the second half could not stand alone as a section.
6. **Heading.** The title was changed to "Attention Circuits" as the writing guide proposed; the label and file name still say `what-attention-computes`, which is harmless.

## Passage-level findings

**CIRC-1** (minor, code). Imports. Quote: "import copy". A second imports cell in the PyTorch tab only. One imports cell per framework.

**CIRC-2** (moderate, explanation). Under "Residual Representations Across Layers". Quote: "Reading a head from its weights requires an exact account of what each". The subsection writes :eqref:`eq_stream-update` with $\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{OV}}^{\ell m}$ "defined below" and the attention weights $\alpha_{ij}^{\ell m}$ before either circuit has been introduced. The reader meets a symbol whose meaning is promised for later (guide 8.2). Since the next subsection defines both circuits in six lines, swap the order: start from the head the reader knows from :numref:`sec_multihead-attention` ($\mathbf{W}_o$ applied to a weighted sum of $\mathbf{W}_v \mathbf{h}_j$), name the two products, and then write the stream update. Also say that the sum over $m$ with a separate $\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{OV}}$ per head is the concatenate-then-project layer of that section rewritten; the reader has not seen multi-head attention as a sum of heads, and the step is not obvious.

**CIRC-3** (minor, explanation). Under "QK and OV Circuits". Quote: "Vectors are columns here:". The chapter until now has used row vectors ($\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_q$ in :eqref:`eq_pos-selfattn`). The switch is announced, which is good, but it costs a tab note of five lines in each framework about weight orientation. Consider staying with rows, or state the reason for the switch (the circuit products read left to right as "output from input").

**CIRC-4** (minor, focus). Under "QK and OV Circuits". Quote: "same holds for every $\mathbf{R}$ that commutes with the rotations, such as". The non-identifiability argument is worth its three sentences. The RoPE refinement is correct (I checked it) but serves nobody at this point; it is a detail for the exercise on K-composition. Cut the sentence or move it to a footnote-like parenthesis.

**CIRC-5** (minor, pedagogy). Under "QK and OV Circuits". Quote: "Both circuits are $128 \times 128$ and have rank 32, the head width.". The cell prints the shape and rank of two products of random matrices. The rank is known from the shapes; the cell exists to introduce `circuits`. That is fine, but the sentence could be dropped and the function introduced where it is first used (the copy matrix).

**CIRC-6** (minor, explanation). Under "Bigrams, Skip-Trigrams, and the Limits of One Layer". Quote: "are called *skip-trigrams*,". The skip-trigram is defined abstractly. One concrete instance from text would fix it in memory, as Elhage et al. do: for example "when `keep` appears earlier and the current token is `in`, boost `mind`". The section has a model trained on text available from the previous section and gives no example from it.

**CIRC-7** (minor, correctness of reading). Under "A Two-Block Induction Circuit". Quote: "For a long stretch of training, the later-copy curve of the two-block model". The description matches both stored figures (drop near step 1,700 to 2,000; two stages in the PyTorch run). One visible feature goes unremarked: the first-copy loss of the two-block model jumps by a few tenths of a nat exactly when the later-copy loss drops, and then decays. That bump is the cost of starting to expect repetition, which the previous paragraph explained in general; pointing at it would tie the explanation to the figure.

**CIRC-8** (minor, correctness of reading). Under "Attention Patterns of the Learned Heads". Quote: "mostly the diagonal itself, attending to their own position. In block 2,". In the stored PyTorch figure, block 1 has two heads with a line below the diagonal, one head on the diagonal, and one head (head 1) with a faint, mixed pattern concentrated on early keys, which is neither. "The others draw mostly the diagonal" is true of one of two. Since the pattern varies by run, describe what is stable ("at least one head attends to the previous token; the rest attend mostly to the current position or diffusely") and let the head scores carry the numbers.

**CIRC-9** (minor, language; pattern, 4 instances). Quotes: "A map shows where each query looks, and it"; "The attention maps show where each"; "The attention maps show where the heads look; the OV circuits show what"; "An attention map fixes the mixture $\alpha_{ij}$, but the head's effect". The same contrast opens four passages. State it in the opening and in the closing section; delete the two in between (the second of them opens a subsection with a recap of the one before, guide 7.7).

**CIRC-10** (moderate, correctness; claim beyond the evidence). Under "The Copy Matrix". Quote: "in place of $\bar{\mathbf{h}}_a$, the same count for block 2 varies widely". No cell computes the count with raw embeddings, so the reader cannot see this, and the explanation attached to it ("block 1 adds to each position a transformed copy of its own token, and block 2 has learned to read that copy") is a hypothesis that the section does not test. The same hypothesis is then used as established fact in "Head Ablation": "which block 2 reads, as the copy matrix above showed". The copy matrix showed only that $\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{OV}}$ of block 2 maps the mean stream vector of token $a$ to a boost for $a$. An alternative reading of the ablation is at least as plausible: zeroing two or three of four heads in a model with no normalization changes the scale and mean of the stream that block 2 was trained on (the table's own "remaining limitation"). Fix: print the raw-embedding count in the same cell (one more line), and write the explanation as what it is: "A likely reason is that ...; Exercise 6 tests it with mean ablation." Then delete "as the copy matrix above showed".

**CIRC-11** (major, correctness; sure of the arithmetic, the stored accuracy is read from a figure). Under "Generalization to Unseen Patterns". Quote: "circuit matches on exactly one preceding token. Disambiguating would". The text attributes the remaining errors to tokens that occur more than once in a pattern, on the grounds that the circuit matches on one token. A circuit that matches on a single token and picks among the $K$ candidate successors is right with probability $\mathbb{E}[1/K] = (1 - (1-p)^L)/(Lp)$ with $p = 1/64$ (this is the formula Exercise 8 asks for). That is 0.84 at $L = 24$ and 0.84 pooled over $L = 16, \ldots, 32$ as `eval_copy` pools. The stored models do better: the accuracy curve for period 24 sits at about 0.90 to 0.93 after the restart, and the pooled later-copy accuracy is 0.89 in both tabs even though it includes the restart position, where accuracy is near zero. So the trained model resolves some of the ambiguous cases, which a single-token match cannot do. Either it matches on more than one token (block 1 has heads beyond the previous-token ones, and RoPE gives offsets) or it uses something else; the section has not looked. Exercise 8(d) in fact asks the reader to "decide ... whether `model_two` matches on more than one token", so the text asserts what its own exercise leaves open. Fix: state the single-token ceiling (about 0.84) in the text, observe that the model exceeds it slightly, and say that repeated tokens explain most of the errors while the model evidently resolves some of them; remove "exactly one".

**CIRC-12** (minor, explanation). Under "Generalization to Unseen Patterns". Quote: "announces that repetition has begun, so no predictor can be confident of". Two consecutive sentences pull in opposite directions (no predictor can be confident; yet the low accuracy is the model's shortfall) and the resolution is in an exercise. The resolution takes one sentence: with nine pattern lengths still possible at position 24, the best predictor gives the first token probability $1/9 + (8/9)/64 = 1/8$, which is small but is the largest of all tokens, so its accuracy there would be 1. Write: "The best possible predictor is not confident there either (it gives the restart probability about 1/8), but its top guess is right, whereas `model_two`, which has no head that counts positions, guesses at chance."

**CIRC-13** (minor, correctness, accurate as far as I know). Under "Induction Heads in Larger Models". The four claims attributed to Olsson et al. (the repeated-random-token probe, abrupt formation with a visible change in the loss, in-context gain measured as the loss difference between early and late context positions, ablations in small models, heads that also complete fuzzier patterns) match my knowledge of the paper. Quote: "matching is a general algorithm, not a trick for exact repetition. In models with". The clause "so prefix matching is a general algorithm, not a trick for exact repetition" is a reflexive "not X" punchline (guide 10.3) and slightly stronger than the evidence; write "so the heads do more than complete exact repetition".

**CIRC-14** (moderate, pedagogy). Under "In-Context Regression". Quote: "The two predictions agree to rounding error. The head's weights contain no". The cell compares $\eta\,\mathbf{x}_q^\top \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y}$ with itself computed through three projections; it cannot fail, and it needs a JAX tab note about TF32 to make four digits agree. Cut it. If the subsection stays (structural finding 2), strengthen the second cell with the prediction it tests: for standard normal inputs, $\mathbb{E}\,\|(\frac{1}{n}\sum_i \mathbf{x}_i\mathbf{x}_i^\top - \mathbf{I})\mathbf{w}\|^2 = \frac{p+1}{n}\|\mathbf{w}\|^2$, so the relative error is $(p+1)/n = 9/n$: 0.9, 0.09, 0.009. The stored outputs (1.02 and 0.88; 0.081 and 0.086; 0.009) match. The text should also say what the first row means: with 10 examples for 8 unknowns, one gradient step is no better than predicting zero. "The head learns the regression from its context, and more examples teach it more" then becomes a quantitative statement. Also "learns" is generous for one step with a fixed step size; the sentence "The head's weights contain no information about $\mathbf{w}$" says it better.

**CIRC-15** (minor, correctness, accurate as far as I know). Under "In-Context Regression" and "Attention Weights as Explanations". von Oswald et al. (construction by hand; trained linear-attention models match it), Shen et al. (disputing the claim for pretrained language models), Xiao et al. (attention on the first tokens), Jain and Wallace, Wiegreffe and Pinter, Meng et al. (causal tracing by patching activations), Wang et al. (the indirect-object circuit in GPT-2 small, which includes induction heads) are described consistently with what I know. Unverified: that Gu et al. show the sink's value vector to have small norm (I believe they report this; I cannot confirm the wording). Quote: "The induction head remains the". "The induction head remains the best-established mechanism" needs its scope: best-established mechanism of in-context learning in language models.

**CIRC-16** (minor, language). Under "Attention Weights as Explanations". Quote: "attention, and the debate sharpened rather than settled. As an unqualified". "sharpened rather than settled" is a turn of phrase where a statement is wanted: what did the exchange establish? One sentence: alternative weightings found per input do not show that a model with those weights exists, so the weights are evidence but weak evidence.

**CIRC-17** (minor, dependencies). Same section. Quote: "shows why a model's sinks must be kept when its". A forward reference to chapter 11 for a consequence of sinks; not load-bearing, fine. The reference back to :numref:`sec_multihead-attention` ("two heads blend the same two values") checks out against that section.

**CIRC-18** (minor, language). Summary. Quote: "Attention weights alone explain little until the value". At 170 words in seven sentences the Summary lists every result. Three sentences would do: the two circuits; one layer against two and what the trained model learned; what each kind of evidence showed.

- `what-attention-computes-two-blocks-learn-to-look-things-up-1`: 0.47 and 0.36 nats, accuracy 0.89 and 0.88; one-block 3.50 and 3.34, accuracy 0.12 and 0.14. Prose ranges ("a few tenths of a nat", "about nine in ten", "fewer than one in five") are at the right precision.
- `what-attention-computes-the-heads-caught-in-the-act-2`: block-1 previous-token scores 0.49 and 0.38 in two heads (PyTorch), 0.95 in one head (JAX); block-2 induction scores 0.32 to 0.39 (PyTorch), 0.63 to 0.66 (JAX). The prose "more than a quarter of its attention, in many runs over half" covers both. Good use of ranges.
- `what-attention-computes-the-circuit-is-in-the-weights`: block 2 at 1.00 in both tabs; the one-block control at 0.95 (PyTorch) and 0.75 (JAX). The prose handles the control correctly. The raw-embedding variant is discussed and not printed (CIRC-10).
- `what-attention-computes-head-ablation-2`: outputs match the prose in both tabs, including "with all four removed the later-copy loss lies above even that of the one-block model" (4.36 against 3.48; 4.18 against 3.35). The JAX run has one previous-token head, so "block 1, all other heads" removes three of four heads there; the interpretation in CIRC-10 applies with more force.

- `what-attention-computes-in-context-regression`: cannot fail (CIRC-14). `-1`: matches "about tenfold".
- All experiments are small enough for a laptop (two blocks, width 128, 5,000 steps on 64-token sequences). Right scale for the phenomenon.
