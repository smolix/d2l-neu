# A GPT from Scratch

Source: `chapter_transformers/gpt.md` (lines 1 to 1291; the slides after line 1292 were not read). About 4,220 words of body prose, of which about 400 are in JAX-only tab blocks, 1,720 words of exercises, 30 code cells. Stored outputs checked: `outputs/pytorch/chapter_transformers/gpt.json` and the JAX counterpart. Figure rendered and inspected: `mdl-transformers-gpt-stack.svg`, and the stored loss curve.

## Verdict

This is the section a reader came to the part for, and it delivers most of what it promises: a model class whose `forward` is eight readable lines, honest reference levels (uniform, unigram, bigram) for the loss, a compute estimate that connects a laptop run to the largest runs, and the pleasure of loading the released GPT-2 into the class just written and watching it produce English. The numbers in the prose match the stored outputs, and the arithmetic I recomputed (parameter counts, the $6ND$ figures, the temperature example, the checkpoint sizes) is right. It is not yet at the bar, for four reasons. The training objective is never shown: no loss, no shifted targets, no loop, so "from scratch" stops at the model. There is no comparison with the recurrent network of the Basics, although the part introduction frames the whole part as the road from that network to this one. The experiment on normalization placement fails for a reason the section cannot name and that the previous section's analysis does not supply. And the central classes carry options for later sections (`num_kv_heads`, `offset`, `tables`, `ffn_factory`, a class-attribute attention) that make the code harder to hold in the head than it needs to be. The single most important fix is the comparison with the RNN on the same bytes and budget, as a third reference level beside the unigram and bigram; it is the question the reader brings, and at present the section does not let them answer it.

## What works

- **The opening** (lines 9 to 16). "Given 'the European lobste', it must predict 'r'; given 'the European lobster', it must predict a space. One sequence of 128 bytes therefore holds 128 training examples". A concrete case that produces the two requirements (no reading ahead, shared parameters) from which the design follows. Keep it word for word.
- **`GPT.forward`**. Embedding, optional positions, blocks, norm, tied head, each line commented with its shape. A reader can reproduce this from memory.
- **Bytes as tokens, with the argument** ("Bytes have two advantages here"): 1.4% of parameters in the table against almost three times the rest of the model for GPT-2's vocabulary, and a loss that is bits per byte. Both numbers check.
- **The reference levels.** Uniform 5.55, unigram 3.17, bigram 2.45, model about 1.35. This is how a loss should be presented, and the sentence "A loss in nats means little on its own" is the right introduction. The stalled post-norm run is then read against the unigram level ("the model predicts the byte frequencies of the text and ignores its input, even the current byte"), which is the sharpest observation in the section.
- **"Training Compute."** "Across these eleven orders of magnitude, the model definition hardly changes: an embedding, a stack of blocks, a causal mask, and a head." A sentence with a judgment in it, supported by four numbers that check ($2 \times 10^{14}$, $7 \times 10^{18}$, $10^{20}$, $10^{25}$).
- **"We use no dropout: a model that almost never reads the same passage twice has nothing to memorize."** and **"Shape checks alone do not validate a loaded checkpoint, because a transposed square matrix still multiplies."** Two sentences a reader could quote a week later. Protect them.
- **Reading the samples through the perplexity**: "at a typical position the model still hesitates among about four bytes, so the temperature changes the text visibly."
- **Loading GPT-2 without a model library.** It proves the class is the real architecture, and the perplexity and greedy checks are the right two.

## Structural findings

1. **No comparison with the RNN of the Basics.** `chapter_advanced/index.md` opens the part with "The Basics ended with a small recurrent network trained on *The Time Machine*" and asks what it takes to get from there to current language models. This section is where that question should get its first measured answer, and it does not mention recurrent networks at all. The Basics model reached about 2.1 to 2.4 bits per byte on its validation text (`chapter_recurrent-neural-networks/rnn-implementation.md`, lines 824 and 908); this model reaches about 1.9 bits per byte, on different text, with a different tokenizer and twenty times the parameters. A reader will put those numbers side by side, and nothing here tells them the comparison is void, nor gives them a valid one. Recommended: train `d2l.RNNLM` (and, if the authors want a fair fight, a gated variant) on the same `d2l.WikiText103` bytes with the same number of steps, a matched parameter count and the same optimizer, and report its validation loss as a third reference level after the bigram, with the time per step of both models. I cannot predict the outcome: at a context of 128 bytes a well-tuned recurrent model may come close to the transformer in loss and lose mainly in speed and in what happens at longer context. If so, say so; that is a more honest and more interesting result than silence, and it sets up chapter 12. This is an experiment for the development machine.

2. **The training objective is invisible.** The section title promises a GPT from scratch, and its second heading is "Training the Model", but the loss is never written, the pair (input window, target window shifted by one) is never shown, and the loop is "the training loop `d2l.train_steps` of :numref:`sec_adam`". The reader last saw that loop two chapters ago, in a section about Adam. Repetition is to be removed, but the objective of the model is not a detail to cite: it is what the opening paragraph motivates. Add (a) one equation, the mean over the $B \times T$ positions of $-\log p(x_{t+1} \mid x_{\le t})$, tied to the "lobster" example; (b) one small cell that draws a batch and prints the first dozen decoded bytes of `X[0]` and `Y[0]`, so the shift is seen; (c) a 5 by 5 causal mask, printed or as a figure, since in `GPT` the mask is hidden inside a fused kernel (`is_causal=True`) and never appears. These three small things are the "every step has a picture and a small concrete case" that the section lacks.

3. **The central classes are written for later sections.** `CausalAttention` has `num_kv_heads`, `kv_hiddens`, `enable_gqa`, a `qkv` method with `offset` and `tables`, and a `forward(self, X, keys=None, values=None, mask=None, tables=None)` that ignores two arguments and raises on a third. `GPT` holds the attention as a class attribute "so that a subclass can replace it" in chapter 13, and takes `num_kv_heads` and `ffn_factory`. None of this is used in the section. The essential attention is about twelve lines: one linear layer, a split into heads, an optional rotation, one attention call, one output projection. Show that version here. Add grouped key-value heads in `kv-cache.md`, where they are explained, with `d2l.add_to_class` or a subclass; if the library must have one class, present the lean one in the text and say that the saved version has two further options described later. As it stands, a reader trying to rebuild the model from memory has to work out which half of the code is the model.

4. **The normalization experiment and the initialization argument are each weaker than the text allows.** See GPT-7 and GPT-13. Both are places where the section could show a mechanism and instead shows a number and cites a paper.

5. **Cost in time and memory is missing.** The section counts FLOPs and says nothing about seconds, memory, or what hardware the reader needs. It runs one 1,000-step training and three 800-step ablations of a 4.8-million-parameter model, and loads a 124-million-parameter model. State the measured time per run on the book's GPU and an estimate for a laptop CPU (at $2 \times 10^{14}$ operations per run this is minutes on a GPU and of the order of an hour on a CPU; unverified), and say which cells a reader without a GPU should shorten.

6. **Length and a possible seam.** The section is the longest job in the chapter: build, train, test placement, sample, load a checkpoint. I do not think it must be split if the retold material goes (GPT-8, GPT-9, GPT-16, the JAX notes of GPT-6), which removes 600 to 800 words. If the authors do split, the seam is before "## Loading GPT-2": tokenizer, weight map and checks form a self-contained 620-word unit with its own exercises (6 and 8), and `kv-cache.md` depends only on that unit and on the class.

7. **Order inside "Training the Model".** "Normalization Placement in Training" reads the stalled run against the unigram level, so it belongs directly after "Loss Curves and Baselines". "Training Compute" currently sits between them and would close the training part better.

## Passage-level findings

**GPT-1** (minor, thought). Under "From Blocks to a Language Model": "Four ingredients turn a stack of transformer blocks into a language model, and each solves one part of the problem." The four bold items are the tied embedding, the mask, the positions, and "The blocks", so one of the ingredients added to the stack of blocks is the blocks. The sentence is also an announcement (guide 10.3). Write "Three things are added to the stack" and drop the second clause.

**GPT-2** (minor, explanation). Same passage: "saves $V \times d$ parameters for a vocabulary of size $V$ and helps at small scale :cite:`Press.Wolf.2017`". "Helps" what, and why? For this model the saving is 65,536 parameters, 1.4% of the total, so the saving is not the reason either. Say what the cited paper found (lower perplexity in small language models) and be candid that tying is also a constraint: exercise 7 of this very section proves that a tied head makes the logit matrix symmetric up to row scaling when there are no blocks. One clause pointing there would turn a convention into something understood.

**GPT-3** (minor, language: consistency). Same passage: "Position $t$ predicts token $t+1$ from tokens $1, \ldots, t$". The previous section and the code count from zero ("lets the query at position $t$ see the keys at positions $0, \ldots, t$"). Use one convention in both sections.

**GPT-4** (minor, explanation). Under "Positions": "so that the score of a query and a key depends on their offset". The score depends on the contents of both and on their positions only through the offset. Add "only through".

**GPT-5** (moderate, language). Before the `CausalAttention` cell: "A block passes its attention keys, values, and an optional mask, and any keyword arguments of its own call, here the RoPE tables. This module uses its input as the keys and the values, and it rejects a mask, because its own mask is always causal." The first sentence does not parse on a first reading ("its attention keys" reads as a noun phrase). Both sentences exist to excuse an interface inherited from `d2l.MultiHeadAttention` (three positional inputs and a mask) that this module does not want. If the lean module of structural finding 3 is adopted, `TransformerBlock` can call `self.attention(Y, **attn_args)` for self-attention and the paragraph disappears. Otherwise: "`TransformerBlock` calls its attention with queries, keys, values and a mask. Self-attention needs only the first, and this module applies its own causal mask, so it ignores the keys and values and refuses a mask."

**GPT-6** (moderate, pedagogy: tab prose). Four JAX-only blocks, about 400 words. Instances: "Wrapped in `jax.checkpoint`, as in `attend` below, it keeps them only during its own forward pass: the backward pass stores the queries, keys, and values and computes the attention weights again"; "not the atomic additions of an ordinary lookup, which wait on one another when a few frequent bytes fill most positions. The product splits across GPUs like any other"; "`optax.inject_hyperparams` keeps the peak rate, the step counts, the decay, and the clipping norm in the optimizer state rather than in the transformation"; and the 150-word note on the fixed-shape buffer of `generate`. The first interrupts the list of ingredients between the mask and the positions; the second sits directly under the model class and is about multi-GPU scatter-add contention. Guide section 9: tab prose is at most a sentence or two, and framework plumbing belongs in comments or in the performance chapter. The JAX reader of the book's central section gets a text that is one tenth compiler notes. Move each to a code comment of one line plus a pointer to `sec_fast_transformer`, or to that section.

**GPT-7** (moderate, thought; partly correctness). Under the `GPT` class, the initialization: "The $2L$ contributions together are then about as large as a single unscaled one, whatever the depth." and then "A forward pass through untrained stacks of increasing depth tests the argument for this scaling." Four problems.
(a) The text never says what goes wrong if the stream grows. In a pre-norm model with a final normalization, the scale of the stream does not reach the logits, and every branch reads a normalized stream. So the reader is given a rule and a reason for the rule ("the growth of the stream") but no harm that the rule prevents.
(b) The previous section said of the same growth: "This growth stabilizes the stack." The two sections now disagree in their value judgment (see `transformer-block.md`, TB-8).
(c) The cell (`gpt-from-blocks-to-a-language-model-3`) confirms arithmetic: a sum of $2L$ terms of equal size is proportional to $L$, and dividing each by $2L$ removes the proportionality. This is the kind of check the author asked to limit (decision 4), unless it teaches something more. It could: the stored output shows that even with the scaling the stream's mean square (0.0027 to 0.0033) is about seven times that of the embedding (0.0004, which the text quotes without drawing the comparison), so at initialization the blocks, not the tokens, dominate what the final layer reads. That is worth a sentence, or worth fixing the reader's picture of "the stream stays at the scale of the embedding".
(d) The paragraph ends by half withdrawing the rule: "This depth scaling is a common choice rather than a law: OLMo 2 returned to a truncated $\mathcal{N}(0, 0.02^2)$ for all parameters". Candour is welcome, but after it the reader does not know whether to use the rule.
Direction: decide what the section claims. Either show a consequence (train the six-block model, or a deeper one, with and without the scaling and report whether the loss or the stability differs; I expect little difference at six blocks) or shrink the passage to the rule, one sentence of rationale in the terms of TB-8 (each branch's first contribution should be small relative to the stream), the one-line check of the two standard deviations, and the candid sentence. Also note that the rule as coded ($1/\sqrt{2L}$) follows the common reimplementations; the GPT-2 paper says the weights of residual layers are scaled by $1/\sqrt{N}$ with $N$ the number of residual layers, and the training code was not released, so "as GPT-2 does" is the usual reading and not a verified fact. Unverified; that is my recollection of the paper.

**GPT-8** (moderate, thought: repetition). Same subsection: "The parameter count follows from the block. Attention holds four $d \times d$ matrices". This paragraph derives $4d^2$, $8d^2$ ("as two matrices of width $4d$ with GELU or three of width $8d/3$ with SwiGLU"), $12d^2$, GPT-2's 85 million and its 38.6-million table. Keep here only what is new for the whole model: 4.7 million in six blocks, 65,536 in the table, and the 31% an untied head would add to GPT-2.

**GPT-9** (moderate, thought: repetition; one inconsistency). Under "The Training Text": "The training run below makes about 8 million predictions. *The Time Machine* has about 170,000 characters, so the run would pass over the book about fifty times". `chapter_optimization/adam.md` (lines 396 to 407, `subsec_tinylm`) makes the same argument for the same corpus in nearly the same sentences: 8 million predictions, memorization of the novel, 270 million bytes, "about 3%", consecutive validation windows of 1.1 million bytes. That passage says "the 100,000 characters of *The Time Machine*" and "about 80 times"; this one says 170,000 and fifty. (Both are defensible, the training split against the whole text, but a reader who remembers the first is given a different number for the same book.) **GPT-10** (minor, thought). Under "The Training Recipe": "The training recipe is the one :numref:`chap_optimization` assembled, and each of its four parts addresses a problem met there." **GPT-11** (minor, pedagogy: code). **GPT-12** (minor, thought). Under "Training Compute": "A rough count of operations places this run among larger ones." The derivation that follows ($2N$ forward, twice that backward, $6ND$) is derived again, at greater length and with a measurement, in `scaling-laws.md` under "Six FLOPs per Parameter and Token" (lines 116 to 152). One owner. **GPT-13** (major, thought and correctness: experiment). Under "Normalization Placement in Training": "In a deep stack at initialization, this drives the tokens toward their average and leaves the query and key projections with tiny gradients." Then, after the runs: "The tiny query and key gradients at initialization are therefore at most part of the explanation." and "The third run confirms this".
(a) The first sentence restates the result of the previous section as if it applied to the model trained here. It does not. That result was obtained with Xavier weights in 32 blocks. With $\mathcal{N}(0, 0.02^2)$ weights at width 256 the collapse rate per block is $1 + \alpha^4 \approx 1.01$ (exercise 7 of the previous section), and a NumPy reimplementation of the post-norm stack confirms a token spread of 0.99 after 6 blocks and 0.98 after 32 (LayerNorm and GELU blocks; the `GPT` configuration with RMSNorm, SwiGLU and RoPE was not simulated). So at initialization this six-block post-norm model has no collapsed tokens and no starved queries. "At most part of the explanation" should read "not the explanation".
(b) The explanation offered instead is cited, not shown: "at initialization the gradients of the parameters near the output of a post-norm stack are large". No cell measures a gradient. "The third run confirms this" overstates: the third run shows that a slow warmup avoids the failure, which is consistent with the cited account and with others; it also changes two things at once (warmup and cosine decay).
(c) The striking observation, that the stalled model sits at the unigram level and so ignores even the current byte, is stated and then left. Why would a network lose the identity of its own input token? That is what the reader most wants to know, and the section hands it to exercise 4.3 ("Does the stalled run lose the distinction between its tokens, and if so, when?").
(d) The stored output says something the prose does not: with the warmup the post-norm model ends at 1.55 (1.53 in JAX), the pre-norm model at 1.54 (1.55). At this depth post-norm is not worse once it trains; what pre-norm gives is robustness to the learning-rate schedule. That is the correct and useful conclusion, and it is not drawn. The text says only that the model "leaves the unigram level and trains".
Direction: make this the section's one mechanism experiment. Record, for the stalled and the warmed-up post-norm runs, the token spread at the last block and the gradient norm of the first and last blocks over the first 100 steps (the tools exist in `signal_stats`), and plot them. Then state what was found. If the tokens collapse during the first steps, the two sections connect at last: the collapse that Xavier produces at initialization is produced here by a few large updates. If they do not, say what does happen. Close with the conclusion in (d). Keep the unigram reading; it is excellent.

The two tabs fragment the prose in four places (GPT-6); the shared prose is true for both tabs, with one near miss: "The fused attention kernels ... never store the $n \times n$ matrix of scores" is shared prose, and the JAX note immediately says that its default implementation does build that matrix.

## Exercises

Nine problems, about 40% of the body. This is a strong set and should be kept.

- Exercise 7 (tied head without blocks) is the best exercise in the two sections: a clean proof (I checked the identity and the bound $(\log 2)/3$) that tying constrains what the model can represent, followed by an experiment. The text should point to it where tying is introduced (GPT-2).
- Exercise 2 (unmasked attention) and exercise 6 (checkpoint mismatches, including an independent NumPy forward pass) teach what goes wrong in practice and how to detect it.
- Exercise 8 (bits per byte against GPT-2) asks the reader to think about a comparison across tokenizers and training data; good.
- Exercise 4.3 holds the measurement the main text needs (GPT-13); move it up and keep parts 1 and 2.
- Exercise 3 asks for five configurations, three repeats, at two learning rates: thirty training runs under a `[code]` tag. Mark it extended or cut the repeats to the two arms that differ.
- Exercise 1 repeats the parameter arithmetic a fifth time; keep part 3 (the head's share of the matrix work against its share of the parameters), which is new, and shorten parts 1 and 2 to "verify the printed total".
- To add, if the RNN baseline does not go into the main text: train `d2l.RNNLM` on the same bytes with the same budget and compare loss, time per step, and the loss as a function of position in the window.
