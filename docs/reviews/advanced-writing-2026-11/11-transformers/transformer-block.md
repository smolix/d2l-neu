# The Transformer Block

Source: `chapter_transformers/transformer-block.md` (lines 1 to 966; the slides after line 967 were not read). About 3,030 words of body prose, 1,590 words of exercises, 22 code cells (11 per framework). Stored outputs checked: `outputs/pytorch/chapter_transformers/transformer-block.json` and the JAX counterpart. Figures rendered and inspected: `mdl-transformers-block-anatomy.svg`, `mdl-transformers-norm-taxonomy.svg`, and the stored gradient plot of cell `transformer-block-signal-propagation-at-initialization`.

## Verdict

This is a good section with one first-rate idea and a tail that does not belong to it. The idea: a freshly initialized stack is "a fixed random function, so we can *measure* how it treats signals", and the measurement (token spread, stream scale, query gradient per block) shows a mechanism instead of asserting one. A reader can rebuild the block from memory afterwards, because `MiniBlock` reduces the two arrangements to four lines that differ in where `norm` is called. Against the bar the section is a strong 3 to a weak 4: the prose is clean and accurate line by line, but the central experiment is reported as a property of post-norm when it is a property of post-norm with unit-gain (Xavier) weights at depth 32, the derivation that makes the collapse quantitative sits in an exercise, two of the experiment's visible features (the shortfall from $\sqrt{33}$, the shape of the gradient curve) are left unread, and the last third of the section builds a language model one section before the section about language models in order to run a comparison that shows a difference of 0.05 nats in a model that is still improving by 0.25 nats per 200 steps. The single most important fix: state the scope of the collapse experiment in the main text (rate $1 + \alpha^4$ per block, so Xavier doubles the common component every block and the small initialization of the next section does almost nothing), because without it this section's argument for pre-norm and the next section's training experiment do not connect.

## What works

- **The opening** (lines 4 to 25). It states a problem the reader can feel ("nothing in the layer computes a nonlinear feature of what a single position holds"; a signal through 32 layers "can shrink, explode, or lose the differences between tokens") and then says what the section will measure and where. Keep it.
- **`MiniBlock`** (cell `transformer-block-two-arrangements`). "In code the two differ by two lines" is true and the class shows it. This is the passage a reader will remember the block by.
- **The design of the probe** (lines 186 to 195). The remark that after a normalization "the obvious probe $\|\mathbf{H}\|^2$ is constant by construction and has zero gradient, so we use a generic linear readout" is the kind of candid experimental detail the guide asks for (section 10.2). Keep it.
- **"Collapsed tokens starve attention of gradient."** followed by the explanation that with identical tokens "every query scores every key alike", and the contrast with $\mathbf{W}_v$ and $\mathbf{W}_o$, which still get gradients of several hundredths. The stored outputs support it in both frameworks (`W_q` 1.3e-09 against `W_v` 6.1e-02 in PyTorch, 2.7e-09 against 5.7e-02 in JAX).
- **Figure `fig_transformer-block`**. Rendered, it is clear and carries the argument: the blue stream is interrupted by `norm` boxes on the left and runs free on the right.
- **Parameter matching of SwiGLU** (width $\tfrac{8}{3}d$) is explained and then checked in one line (524,288 against 524,544).
- **Exercises 2, 5, 6 and 7** are among the best in the chapter; see below.

## Structural findings

1. **The section is one and a third sections.** Lines 632 to 756 build `CharLM`, a causal language model with embeddings, a mask, a final norm and a tied head, in order to compare GELU with SwiGLU. Every one of those ingredients is the declared subject of `gpt.md` ("Four ingredients turn a stack of transformer blocks into a language model"). The reader therefore meets the causal mask, the tied head and learned positions twice in ten pages, the first time compressed into one paragraph ("A dozen lines wrap an embedding, a stack of blocks, a final RMSNorm, and a tied output head ... around the mask"). `CharLM` is also the third toy transformer language model before the GPT (`TinyLM` in `chapter_optimization/adam.md`, `TinyCharLM` in `chapter_attention/positional-information.md`). Recommended: end this section at "Shapes and Parameters" (line 630) and move the GELU against SwiGLU comparison into `gpt.md`, where `act` is a flag of `GPT` and the model is trained with the real recipe. Exercises 5 and 8 move with it (they are about a language model, not about a block). If the authors keep `CharLM` here, give it its own `##` heading; it does not belong under "Shapes and Parameters".

2. **The collapse experiment lacks its scope, and its derivation is in the exercises.** See TB-5 to TB-9. Exercise 7 contains the two-line argument (write tokens as mean plus deviation; uniform attention adds $\mathbf{W}_o\mathbf{W}_v\boldsymbol{\mu}$ to every token; the common energy grows by $1 + \alpha^4$ per block) that turns "decays geometrically" from an observation into something the reader can derive and that tells them when it does not happen. That argument belongs in the main text in three sentences. The exercise keeps the checking and the FFN correction.

3. **"Normalizing Queries and Keys" is a survey inside a teaching section.** Its two paragraphs retell four papers and two model reports; the section neither implements QK-norm nor measures the instability, and says so ("QK-norm is not a flag of our block. It is an exercise"). The second paragraph and `fig_norm-taxonomy` list OLMo 2 and Gemma 3 placements at equal weight with the two the section studies, without saying whether the reader should care. Either teach QK-norm (a flag in the block and a ten-line demonstration that inflated query and key weights saturate the softmax with and without it; exercise 3.3 is that demonstration) or reduce the subsection to three sentences and move the taxonomy to `further-reading.md`. For a book published in 2026 or 2027 the first option is defensible: QK-norm is in current open models. What is not defensible is the present middle: 230 words and a figure for something the block does not do.

4. **Initialization is missing from the section about the block.** Three initializations are in play across the two sections and the reader is told about one of them at a time: Xavier in `signal_stats` ("the 2017 initialization"), the framework default of `d2l.MultiHeadAttention` and `nn.Linear` in `TransformerBlock` and `CharLM` (never mentioned), and $\mathcal{N}(0, 0.02^2)$ with depth scaling in `GPT`. Since the collapse rate depends on the initialization (finding 2), the block section should say which one the class ships with and why the experiment uses another. Similarly `bias=False` and `dropout=0` are defaults of `TransformerBlock` that the prose never explains; one sentence each is enough (biases are dropped in current blocks because the normalization gains and the following layers absorb them; dropout is discussed where the model is trained).

5. **No small concrete case and no picture of the collapse.** The token spread is defined by a formula and reported as three numbers per arrangement. The claim "the spread decays *geometrically*" has to be inferred from `3.6e-01 2.3e-02 9.7e-05`. Plot the spread against depth on a log axis next to the gradient (the data is already in `stats`), or give a pre-generated figure of the 64 by 64 token cosine-similarity matrix at blocks 1, 8 and 32 for both arrangements. That is the picture the Illustrated Transformer would have drawn.

6. **Two classes, one `forward`.** `MiniBlock.forward` and `TransformerBlock.forward` are the same six lines. The duplication is defensible (the first isolates the placement), but then `TransformerBlock` should be introduced as "`MiniBlock` plus options", and the hooks for later sections should be visibly secondary (TB-17).

## Passage-level findings

**TB-1** (moderate, thought: repetition). Pattern, three instances in this file plus one in the chapter introduction. Under the title: "nothing in the layer computes a nonlinear feature of what a single position holds"; under "The Feed-Forward Network": "Attention moves information between positions but computes no nonlinear feature of what a single position holds."; under "Summary": "Attention moves information between positions but computes no nonlinear feature of any one position." `index.md` (line 6) says it a fourth time in other words. Guide 7.7: once in the body, once in the summary. Open the FFN section with what the FFN does, not with the sentence from page one.

**TB-2** (minor, explanation). Under "Components of a Transformer Block": "the same two- or three-matrix MLP is applied token by token". The reader has not met a three-matrix FFN yet. Write "the same small MLP" and let the FFN section count matrices.

**TB-3** (minor, correctness). Under "Components of a Transformer Block": "The model around the blocks must supply positions, and the next section shows how." Positions were taught in `sec_positional-information`, which the previous sentence cites, and the default scheme of the next section (RoPE) acts inside each block's attention, not around the blocks. Rewrite: "Positions must be supplied separately, by one of the schemes of :numref:`sec_positional-information`; :numref:`sec_gpt` adds them."

**TB-4** (minor, language). Under "Two Arrangements": "Later in this section we upgrade both ingredients and collect every option in one configurable class; until then this short block keeps the two arrangements side by side and nothing else in view." A roadmap sentence in the middle of an argument. Cut to "This minimal block isolates the placement; `TransformerBlock` below adds the other options."

**TB-5** (major, correctness; confidence high). Under "Signal Propagation at Initialization": "give every weight matrix the Xavier initialization of the original transformer". Everything that follows (spread of $10^{-4}$, gradients of $10^{-9}$, the Summary's "a 32-block stack then collapses its tokens geometrically") is conditional on this choice and on the depth, and the text does not say so. I reimplemented the `MiniBlock` stack in NumPy (forward pass; it reproduces the stored spreads, 0.34 / 0.024 / 1.2e-4 against the stored 0.36 / 0.023 / 9.7e-5, and the pre-LN RMS). With every matrix drawn from $\mathcal{N}(0, 0.02^2)$ at $d = 256$, the initialization the next section uses, the post-LN spread after 6, 12 and 32 blocks is 0.99, 0.99 and 0.98: there is no collapse. This agrees with the section's own exercise 7: the common-to-deviation ratio grows by $1 + \alpha^4$ per block, with $\alpha^2 = \sigma^2 d$; Xavier has $\alpha = 1$ (a doubling per block), and $\sigma = 0.02$ at $d = 256$ has $\alpha^4 \approx 0.01$. Exercise 7.3 asks the reader to discover this for GPT-1, so the authors know it. It must be in the main text, because otherwise the reader concludes that post-norm stacks are untrainable at depth, which is false as stated (GPT-1 and BERT are post-norm) and which makes the next section's experiment look like a confirmation when it is not (see `gpt.md`, GPT-13). Direction: after the measurement, add the three-sentence derivation and one sentence of scope, for example: "How fast this happens depends on the size of the weights. If the value and output matrices have entries of variance $\alpha^2/d$, each attention sublayer multiplies the ratio of common to token-specific energy by about $1 + \alpha^4$. Xavier initialization is $\alpha = 1$, a doubling per block, which is why 32 blocks suffice; the small weights of :numref:`sec_gpt` have $\alpha^4 \approx 0.01$ and collapse nothing in six blocks." One more printed line of `signal_stats` with small weights would make the contrast visible.

**TB-6** (moderate, explanation). Same subsection: "At initialization attention is nearly uniform, so every attention sublayer pulls each token toward the average token. Post-LN then renormalizes, which locks in the contraction". Two problems. First, with Xavier weights and four heads of width 64 the logits of block 1 have standard deviation 1 (simulated: attention entropy 3.69 nats against $\log 64 = 4.16$), so the weights of one query vary by a factor of several; "nearly uniform" is true only once the tokens have begun to agree. What matters is that the weights are unrelated to content, so every token receives a random mixture whose expectation is the mean token. Second, "locks in the contraction" is a metaphor standing where the mechanism should be: the attention sublayer adds (nearly) the same vector to every token, so the common component grows while the differences do not, and the normalization then shrinks both by the same factor. Say that.

**TB-7** (moderate, explanation). Same subsection: "which predicts an RMS after 32 blocks of $\sqrt{33} \approx 5.7$". The stated premises (outputs uncorrelated, variances add) do not give 33; that needs the unstated premise that every block adds unit mean square. It does not. Simulated branch mean squares at block 1 are 0.04 for attention (an average over many unrelated tokens) and 0.25 for the FFN, so the first block adds 0.29; the sum per block rises to about 1.1 by block 28, as the tokens grow alike and the attention average stops cancelling. The total is 29, RMS 5.4, close to the measured 5.3. The sentence that follows, "The measured value, between 5 and 5.6, falls somewhat short; an exercise asks why", hands the gap in the argument to exercise 6. Replace the false prediction with a true statement: "If every block added a contribution of unit mean square, the RMS after 32 blocks would be $\sqrt{33} \approx 5.7$. The early blocks add less, because their attention averages many unrelated tokens, and the measured value is between 5 and 5.6." The Summary's "lets the stream grow like the square root of the depth" needs the same qualification (exercise 6.4 says in which regime it holds).

**TB-8** (moderate, thought). Same subsection: "This growth stabilizes the stack." Twenty pages later `gpt.md` introduces GPT-2's $1/\sqrt{2L}$ scaling, whose stated purpose is that the stream should not grow with depth, and tests that it does not. A reader who holds both sentences in mind is told that growth is the stabilizer and that growth is to be suppressed. The common idea is that each branch's contribution must be small relative to the stream it is added to; growth of the stream achieves that for later blocks, small writing matrices achieve it for all blocks. State the idea in those terms here, and the next section can cite it.

**TB-9** (moderate, pedagogy). Same subsection: "At block 32 the post-LN query and key projections receive gradients of order $10^{-9}$". The plot shows more than the prose reads. The post-LN curve starts near $10^{-2}$ at block 1 and falls smoothly to $10^{-9}$ at block 32; the pre-LN curve also falls, by a factor of about 30. So the lower post-LN blocks receive healthy gradients, through 30 collapsed blocks above them: this is not a vanishing gradient in depth but a local effect of collapsed inputs, and the curve tracks the spread. That is the most instructive feature of the figure and the text says nothing about it. Add two sentences, and say in one clause why the pre-LN curve declines (the branch reads a stream normalized by a growing RMS, and its tokens are also slowly contracting).

**TB-10** (minor, correctness). Under the same heading: "After a final normalization layer, the obvious probe $\|\mathbf{H}\|^2$ is constant by construction". Only the post-LN stack of `signal_stats` ends in a normalization. The pre-LN stack has none, so the readout is taken on a stream of RMS 5.3 in one arm and RMS 1 in the other. The effect on the comparison (a factor of about 5) is immaterial next to six orders of magnitude, but the sentence describes one arm only. Either add the final normalization that every pre-LN model has (and that exercise 2.3 discusses), or write "For the post-LN stack, which ends in a normalization, ...".

**TB-11** (minor, thought). Same subsection: "These are measurements at initialization, but they agree with what :citet:`xiong2020layer` find in training". Xiong and coauthors attribute the need for warmup to large gradients near the output of a post-LN stack at initialization; this section measured tiny query and key gradients at the top. These are different observations, and `gpt.md` later concedes that the second is "at most part of the explanation". Do not claim agreement here. Write what is established: the measurement shows one way a post-LN stack can start badly; the cited paper shows that post-LN needs warmup in practice; the next section tests the placement in training.

**TB-12** (moderate, correctness: experiment). Under "RMSNorm": "The two timings above therefore vary with the framework and the device. RMSNorm is preferred because it does less work at equal quality." The stored PyTorch output is `LayerNorm: 0.74 ms/call`, `RMSNorm: 0.73 ms/call`; JAX gives 0.44 against 0.29. The paragraph first explains why the benchmark shows little and then asserts the advantage the benchmark failed to show. Guide section 9: fix the experiment or remove it; do not keep it and explain what it fails to show. The citation that follows ("reducing the running time of their models by 7% to 64%") is a figure for 2019 models with other normalizers and reads oddly next to a measured 1%. Remove the timing loop and with it the forward reference to `sec_perf_model` (chapter 13; `Benchmark` is in fact already defined in Basics, `chapter_builders-guide/gpus-devices-memory.md`, line 791). Keep the one-line function. State the real reason plainly: centering turned out not to matter, so current models drop it, and the saving in time is small. The source also has a reflow defect here: line 360 ("device. RMSNorm is preferred ...") runs to about 140 characters where the file wraps at 80, a sign of a patched paragraph.

**TB-13** (minor, correctness; confidence fairly high). Under "Normalizing Queries and Keys": "*QK-norm* :cite:`Henry.Dachapally.Pawar.ea.2020` applies RMSNorm to the queries and the keys of each head right before the dot product". As I recall the paper, Henry and coauthors apply $\ell_2$ normalization to queries and keys and replace the $1/\sqrt{p}$ factor with a learned scalar; normalizing with LayerNorm or RMSNorm while keeping the scaling is the later variant used by the two studies cited next and by current open models. Attribute the idea to the 2020 paper and the form described to the later work.

**TB-14** (moderate, focus). Same subsection: "Recent open models also place norms on the other side of a sublayer." See structural finding 3. This paragraph and the figure are a list of variants. I believe both attributions are right (OLMo 2 normalizes the sublayer output and uses QK-norm; Gemma 3 normalizes before and after and uses QK-norm), but could not confirm them here; mark as unverified.

**TB-15** (moderate, correctness; confidence high). Under "The Feed-Forward Network": "One view is squashed into a soft gate. The other passes through unchanged, so wherever the gate is open it is transmitted at full strength instead of being bent by the activation." SiLU does not squash: $\mathrm{SiLU}(z) = z \cdot \mathrm{sigmoid}(z)$ is unbounded above and close to $z$ for large $z$. A gate in the sense the reader knows (a number between 0 and 1 that scales a signal) is the sigmoid of the original gated linear unit, not the SiLU of SwiGLU. In SwiGLU the "gate" can be 5 or $-0.28$, so "open", "full strength" and "passes through unchanged" do not describe it. What SwiGLU computes is a product of two linear views of the input, one of them passed through a smooth ReLU-like function: a multiplicative (roughly quadratic) feature in place of a pointwise one. The Summary repeats the error: "SwiGLU replaces the fixed activation with a learned gate". Rewrite both. A sentence on what is and is not understood would be in the voice the guide asks for: the paper that introduced it offers no explanation of why it helps.

**TB-16** (moderate, explanation: dependency). Same paragraph: "Gates in LSTMs (:numref:`sec_lstm`) use the same multiplicative interaction." `sec_lstm` is in `chapter_recurrent-modern`, chapter 12, after this chapter. The Basics only name the LSTM (`chapter_recurrent-neural-networks/bptt.md`, line 302). The comparison explains the unknown by something the reader has not read. Cut it, or turn it into a forward pointer ("we meet the same multiplicative interaction in the gates of :numref:`sec_lstm`").

**TB-17** (moderate, pedagogy). Under "A Configurable Block": "Two factory hooks, `attn_factory` and `ffn_factory`, let later sections swap whole sublayers without touching this class again: a causal attention, an attention with a smaller key--value cache, or a mixture-of-experts FFN." and the code comment "attn_args go on to the attention, e.g. the RoPE tables of d2l.GPT". This is material written for the authors' later reuse (guide 4.5), and it reaches the reader before `GPT`, RoPE tables, key-value caches or mixtures of experts mean anything. The hooks are a reasonable engineering decision; the presentation should mark them as such in one sentence ("two arguments exist only so that later sections can replace a sublayer; ignore them for now") and drop the list of future uses. The sentence "The constructor checks its flag strings, so a typo raises an error instead of silently selecting another architecture" describes two `assert` lines and can go.

**TB-18** (moderate, thought: repetition). The parameter count is derived or restated five times across the two sections. Here: "so about two thirds of the parameters of a block sit in these two matrices" (FFN section); "That makes about $12d^2$ per block, two thirds of it in the FFN." (Shapes and Parameters); "which holds about two thirds of the parameters of the block" (Summary); and the GPT-2 paragraph "Its token and position embeddings add another 39 million, which brings the model to its 124 million parameters." `gpt.md` then begins a paragraph "The parameter count follows from the block" and derives $4d^2$, $8d^2$, $12d^2$, 85 million and 38.6 million again. Two cells print parameter counts (`transformer-block-the-feed-forward-network-2` and `transformer-block-shapes-and-parameters-1`). One owner: derive the block count once here, in "Shapes and Parameters", with one cell; let `gpt.md` own the whole-model count and cite the block's.

**TB-19** (moderate, correctness: experiment). Under "Comparing GELU and SwiGLU": "The gated FFN ends a few hundredths of a nat ahead, and it stays ahead when we repeat the comparison from fresh random initializations." The stored losses are 2.48 / 2.14 / 1.88 for GELU and 2.47 / 2.08 / 1.83 for SwiGLU (the JAX tab agrees to 0.01). Both models are still improving by about 0.25 nats per 200 steps when the run stops, so a lead of 0.05 nats is about 40 steps of training. The experiment measures which variant learns faster in the first 600 steps of a two-block model of width 128 at one learning rate. The framing sentence promises more: "a comparison of the two asks whether the gate makes a better model at the same size". The statement about repeated runs is not shown by any cell and gives no range. The next sentences place the result beside Shazeer's "about 0.04 nats" at T5 scale, which invites the reader to see one finding at two scales where there is a coincidence of size. I recall the T5 figures as 1.983 for GELU and 1.944 for SwiGLU after 65k steps, which matches the 0.04; unverified here. - Exercises 6 (stream variance budget) and 7 (rate of token collapse) are excellent, and they hold the analysis the main text needs (TB-5, TB-7). Move the two-line results into the text and keep the exercises as the place where the reader checks them and accounts for the FFN.

- Exercises 5 (causal masks) and 8 (learning-rate sensitivity) are about a language model and go with `CharLM` wherever it goes. Exercise 8 is a replication of a paper at a cost of fourteen 1,000-step runs of a six-block model; it is correctly marked extended.
