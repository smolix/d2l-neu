Source: `chapter_recurrent-modern/hybrids.md` (read to the `<!-- slides -->` marker at line 562). About 2,900 words of prose, 600 words of exercises. Stored outputs read: `outputs/pytorch/chapter_recurrent-modern/hybrids.json`, the JAX file, and the stored recall plot. Figure `img/mdl-modernrnn-hybrid-cache.svg` rendered and inspected. The memory arithmetic and the proof of the Welch bound were recomputed. ## Verdict

The section asks the right closing question for the chapter (what can no fixed-size state do?) and its central experiment answers it cleanly: on associative recall with 4 to 64 pairs, two recurrent models lose pairs as the load grows, and a model with one attention layer recalls all of them. The counting bound, the cache arithmetic and the figure are correct and durable, and the opening example (a pasted table of 64 product codes, 20 of them asked for) is the best motivation of the three sections. But this is the weakest of the three as a piece of teaching. Its explanation of the recurrent models' failure, the "slots" argument with the Welch bound, is a heuristic presented as an account: the counting bound is far from binding, the slot count assumes things about the trained layer that the text does not check, and the one piece of evidence offered for it is a difference no cell prints and that lies within the quoted run-to-run range. Around the experiment, three long paragraphs report about a dozen results of runs the reader cannot see (plateaus, a reference implementation, doubled training, a second key set, four ablations). They read as a laboratory notebook, and they are where the section draws its conclusions about the delta rule and about which parts of the layer matter. The single most important fix: decide what the recall sweep shows on its own, say that, and either add cells for the two or three further claims that matter or cut them.

On the three questions. Settled: the principle (a fixed state bounds recall, a growing cache does not, and the cache is what costs memory at inference) is settled and will not date; that released models mix the two is a fact of 2024 to 2026 and the section rightly presents it as what practice does ("The answer of practice adds a small memory that grows"). Claims about recent work: Jelassi et al., Arora et al., Welch and the Jamba numbers agree with what I know; the section names no model later than Jamba (HY-13). Principle or survey: principle. The catalogue of hybrid models is in the Further Reading notebook, which is the right place.

## What works

- **The opening example** (lines 7 to 12): "A model with a fixed-size state has only what it chose to store when the tokens went by, before it knew which of them would be asked for." This states the whole difficulty in one sentence and is true without qualification. Keep verbatim.
- **Counting bits** (lines 57 to 68). The pigeonhole argument is given in full in five lines, and the reading, "the number of tokens it can recall grows in proportion to its size and not at all with the length of the context", is the durable fact.
- **Candour about the bound**: "The bound counts bits that a perfect encoding would use, and it is far from tight for the models below." Authors saying what their own argument does not explain.
- **The proof of the Welch bound** (lines 106 to 112). Four lines, correct (recomputed: trace $m$, Cauchy–Schwarz on $d$ eigenvalues, Frobenius norm as the sum of squared overlaps, giving $(m-d)/(d(m-1))$).
- **The task** (`recall_batch`, twelve lines, with a printed sample). Small, readable, the standard benchmark, and the right one.
- **The headline result**: stored accuracies of 1.00 at every load for the hybrid and for attention in both frameworks, against 0.92 and 0.74 (PyTorch) for Gated DeltaNet at 32 and 64 pairs. Large, reproducible, and exactly the phenomenon of the title.
- **Inference memory** (lines 429 to 455). $2 \cdot 8 \cdot 128 \cdot 2$ bytes is 4 KB per token and layer; 8,192 channels times 128 states in 16-bit numbers is 2 MB; the crossover is at 512 tokens; 512 MB at 128,000 tokens; 32 GB, 4 GB and 64 MB in the figure. All recomputed and correct. The Jamba figures (4 GB against 32 GB at 256,000 tokens, 4 attention layers in 32) agree with my memory of the paper. This subsection is how a systems fact should be taught: one formula, one worked number, one picture.

## Structural findings

**S1. The explanation of the recurrent curves is a heuristic, and the text leans on it as if it were established.** The argument runs: a state of $N$ numbers has $N$ "slots"; the task has 512 possible keys and the models 256 numbers; so keys must share slots; by Welch they overlap; so recall falls with load. Problems:

- The slot count assumes that a value occupies one number, read back at full precision. The layer normalizes each head's output to unit root mean square (:numref:`sec_matrix-state`, "An output norm and gate"), which discards the magnitude of a read. A read of a $4 \times 4$ head returns a column of four numbers, of which only the direction survives. Whether the trained model can then hold one four-way value per number is not obvious and is not tested.
- The count adds the states of both layers ("256 numbers of state"). Whether the second layer can store pairs of its own, independent of the first, is asserted; exercise 4 asks the reader whether "two more recurrent layers add as many slots as the section's count predicts", which concedes that the authors have not shown it.
- The evidence offered is one sentence: with 128 possible keys and doubled training, Gated DeltaNet reaches "90% or more of 64 pairs", against "80 to 90%" with 512 keys, and "the gap to the task with 512 keys is the part of the curve that the slots explain". The two ranges touch. No cell prints either. And with fewer keys than slots the theory predicts no interference at all, yet a tenth of the pairs is still lost. The data as reported speak against a sharp slot account as much as for it.
- The capacity law of :numref:`sec_matrix-state` already gives a simpler and better-supported statement: with $d_k = d_v = 4$ per head, random-key interference per stored pair is of order $1/(d_k d_v)$, so a head holds a handful of pairs; training can do better than random keys but not without limit. That, with the Welch bound as the precise form of "not without limit", is all the section needs.

Fix: present the Welch bound as what it is, a floor on the overlap of more than $d$ unit keys in $d$ dimensions that no training removes, and drop the word "slot" and the rows-as-hosts construction. State the expectation as a trend ("recall should fall as pairs are added, and should fall later for a larger state") and test the second half in a cell: the same model at width 64 (exercise 3 part 2 already proposes it). A curve that moves right when the state grows is evidence a reader can see. If the slot account is kept, it needs that cell and the 128-key run as printed output.

**S2. A dozen results that no cell prints.** In reading order: attention with rotary embeddings and no convolution learns "only after a plateau of a few thousand training steps", "some runs are still on it" (lines 188 to 190); with shorter initial timescales the recurrent models "learn the task more slowly, gated linear attention markedly so" (line 245); the gated-linear-attention plateau lasts "from a few thousand to more than ten thousand" steps (line 346); the reference implementation "sits on the same plateau for as long, in one of three runs for all 16,000 steps" (line 350); doubled training gives "about 95% of 32 pairs and 80 to 90% of 64" (line 358); the 128-key run (line 360); without the convolution gated linear attention "recalls barely more than chance" (line 372); without unit keys Gated DeltaNet "does not learn the task at all" (line 374); without the output gate "several points less" (line 375); the SiLU "costs Gated DeltaNet a few points" (line 378). The guide (section 9) says that an experiment which needs a paragraph of confounders before it can be read is a research experiment. This one needs three. The reader cannot check any of it, and several items are the authors reassuring themselves ("so the plateau belongs to the model and not to our code"). Keep at most two of these as cells, chosen for what they teach: the convolution ablation (a large effect with a clear mechanism: key and value arrive one token apart) and the wider model of S1. Move the rest to code comments, to exercises (exercise 3 already holds the doubled-training and 128-key runs), or delete them. The plateau of gated linear attention is a fact about optimization that the section cannot explain and should not narrate.

**S3. The comparison of the two recurrent layers does not isolate the write rule.** The text concludes "The delta rule pays off as the regression view of :numref:`sec_deltanet` suggests." The two layers differ in the write rule, in the form of the decay (per key coordinate against one per head), in the gate parametrization and in the chunk size; and gated linear attention has an optimization plateau of variable length that Gated DeltaNet lacks, so part of its deficit may be training that had not finished. The stated mechanism ("the corrected writes fit the stored pairs and remove much of that interference") is also not what a direct measurement shows for keys that merely overlap: with random unit keys written once, a delta rule at $\beta = 1$ recalls fewer old pairs than addition once the pairs outnumber the key dimensions (0.54 against 0.96 at 128 pairs in width 64; see the review of `deltanet.md`, S2). A trained model with learned write strengths may well do better, but the section has not shown why. Fix: after S1 of the review of `matrix-state.md` (scalar decay in both layers) the comparison changes one thing and the sentence can stand as an observation: "with the same decay, the layer that corrects recalls more". Until then, write "Gated DeltaNet recalls more than gated linear attention at every load from 8 pairs on; the two layers differ in the write rule and in the form of the decay, so this run does not say which is responsible."

**S4. The language-modeling run is not read.** Stored: transformer 3.27 (PyTorch) and 3.25 (JAX), hybrid 3.16 and 3.17, Gated DeltaNet alone 3.10. The text says "Language modeling at this scale barely registers the difference in recall". But the run shows an ordering, recurrent best and the transformer worst, by more than the difference on which :numref:`sec_matrix-state` builds its ablation conclusion, and the text passes over it. The reader will ask why the model with perfect recall is worst at language. The likely answer (128-character windows, a local task, `TinyLM` without the short convolution and with learned positions) is not given, and `TinyLM` differs from the `AA` model of the recall experiment in several ways at once. The conclusion drawn, "A comparison by perplexity alone can therefore miss what a model cannot recall", is plausible and is not supported by this run, which does not measure recall in text. Exercise 6 does: it compares the loss at positions that complete a repeated string. That is the experiment for the text. Either promote exercise 6 (one cell, three models, two numbers each) or cut the subsection to a sentence that cites the chapter's Penn Treebank table (see the review of `matrix-state.md`, S3).

**S5. The model paragraph comes before the reader needs it.** "Four Models" is one paragraph of 23 lines (169 to 192) that describes four models, justifies the convolution in front of attention, explains induction heads, reports unseen runs and justifies the head sizes. Split it: three sentences that name the four models and say that only the mixers differ; the code; then, after the results, the one design choice that matters (the convolution brings key and value together) with its ablation cell.

**S6. Order and size.** No split; 2,900 words is right, and after S2 it will be shorter. The order (limit, experiment, memory) is right. The chapter-closing paragraph (lines 460 to 470) is a second ending before the Summary; see HY-14.

## Passage-level findings

**HY-1** (minor, language). Opening. Quote: "This limit therefore matters in practice.". A flat sentence that tells the reader what the previous three sentences showed. Cut it; the paragraph ends well on "copied with one line changed".

**HY-2** (minor, correctness; certain). Under "Counting Bits". Quote: "many times the 128 bits that 64 values of 2 bits take". The state must also record which 64 of the 512 keys the values belong to: $\log_2\binom{512}{64} \approx 278$ bits, so about 406 bits in all. The conclusion stands (the state has 8,192 bits), but the count as given treats the keys as known in advance. Write "many times the roughly 400 bits needed to record which 64 of 512 keys were seen and a 2-bit value for each".

**HY-3** (moderate, explanation). Under "Slots and Overlapping Keys". Quote: "so a state of $N$ numbers has $N$ slots, and a". "Slot" is coined here and then carries the argument for forty lines, the summary and two exercises. The construction behind it ("the key's value goes into one row of the state, along a key direction orthogonal to those of the other keys assigned to that row") is hard to picture, and the text gives no example. If the idea stays, show it for one $2 \times 2$ head: four keys, two rows, two orthogonal directions, the state written out as a matrix, and a read. Better: drop it (S1).

**HY-4** (moderate, thought). Under "Slots and Overlapping Keys". Quote: "many of them do. We therefore expect the recurrent models to recall nearly". The prediction is stated before the experiment, which is good practice, but it is unfalsifiable as written: "nearly every pair when there are few" and "lose more and more" would be true of almost any limited memory for almost any reason. A prediction with a number in it is available from the section's own material, for instance the load at which the squared interference per read reaches the gap between two values, or simply "doubling the state should move the curve to the right by about a factor of two". Give one, then test it (S1).

**HY-5** (minor, correctness; from memory, fairly sure). Under "Associative Recall". Quote: "accounts for most of the gap in perplexity on natural text between attention". Agrees with my memory of Arora et al. (2024), who attribute about four fifths of the gap to associative recall. The earlier claim about Jelassi et al. **HY-6** (moderate, focus). Under "Four Models". Quote: "They learn this too, but only after a plateau of a few". Five lines on what happens in a model the section does not train (two layers of attention with rotary embeddings, forming an induction head), with a term, *induction head*, introduced in italics and never used again. It answers a question the reader has not asked. Cut to one clause, "(without the convolution, two attention layers can learn the same lookup, but more slowly and less reliably)", or cut entirely.

**HY-7** (minor, explanation). Under "Four Models". Quote: "so the size of its heads does not change the comparison". The attention baseline is not the transformer of chapter 11: it has a short convolution in front, no position encoding, and two heads. The text says so, but the section's question is "whether this one layer is enough to recall as well as a transformer", and the summary says "so does attention". Say "attention behind the same convolution" once in the results paragraph so that the reader does not carry away a claim about `TinyLM`.

## For the chapter review

- Right section in the right place: it closes the chapter on the one thing a fixed state cannot do, and its cache arithmetic ties the chapter back to :numref:`sec_kv-cache` and to the question in the chapter introduction.
- It depends on :numref:`sec_matrix-state` (the capacity law, the layer), :numref:`sec_deltanet` (the layer, the regression view), :numref:`sec_lstm` (`train_queries`, `SeqLM`, `fit_lm`), chapter 10 (NoPE, rotary embeddings, windows) and chapter 11 (the KV cache). Nothing from the appendix, and no load-bearing reference into the Language Models part.
- Pattern, strongest here: conclusions rest on runs the reader cannot see. Counting the three sections together there are about fifteen such results. The chapter needs a rule: a result is in a cell, in an exercise, or not in the book.
- Pattern: comparisons between layers that differ in more than the property under discussion (S3 here; the ablation of three parts at once in :numref:`sec_matrix-state`; `TinyLM` against the hybrid in S4).
- The capacity story is told three times with three different quantities: $(n-1)/d_k$ in :numref:`sec_matrix-state`, bits here, slots here. One quantity, the number of entries of the state $d_k d_v$, would carry all three (see MS-3 in the review of `matrix-state.md`).
- The chapter has no summary of its own beyond the recap paragraph in this section (HY-14). The regression table of :numref:`sec_deltanet`, extended by a column for what each memory costs at inference, would serve.
