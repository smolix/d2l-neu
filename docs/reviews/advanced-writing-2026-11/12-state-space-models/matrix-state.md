## Verdict

The first third of this section is at the bar. A two-dimensional memory that holds two pairs and spoils when a third arrives, then the general recurrence, then a four-line proof of the $(n-1)/d_k$ law, then a measurement that lies on the predicted lines: this is how MacKay would teach an associative memory, and it is durable material (outer-product memories are fifty years old, the duality with masked attention is exact algebra). The section loses its line after that. It has four jobs (the memory and its capacity, decay, the dual and chunked algorithms, a complete layer with four extra parts) and the last two are written for an implementer of the flash-linear-attention kernels, not for the reader who came to learn what a matrix state is. The section never shows a trained model using its memory: its one training run concludes that the "matrix memory itself does not help much on this task", and the payoff is deferred two sections. Two readings of the interference experiment are wrong as stated (the "doubling" claim contradicts the stored table by a factor of two; the "faster than in proportion" claim reads an artifact of the codebook as a property of correlated keys). The single most important fix: make the layer of this section a scalar-decay matrix memory, so that the chunked algorithm needs one mask, the overflow story and the per-coordinate machinery leave the main text, and Gated DeltaNet in the next section differs from it in the write rule alone.

On the three questions asked of these sections. Settled enough for a textbook: yes for the memory, the interference law, decay and the dual form; the chunked algorithm is settled engineering; the particular layer (rank-16 gate, exponent 1/16, width-4 convolution) is one 2024 design and will date. Claims about recent models: RetNet, Mamba-2, gated linear attention and the Mamba correspondence are stated correctly as far as I can confirm; the layer the section calls "gated linear attention" is not the layer of the cited paper (finding MS-12). Principle or survey: principle. The variant list is three sentences and the survey material sits in the Further Reading notebook, where it belongs.

## What works

- **The two-dimensional example** (lines 59 to 70). $(3, 5)$, then a third key at 45 degrees and the read of $\mathbf{k}_1$ becomes 7.95. Recomputed: $3 + 7/\sqrt 2 = 7.95$. The closing sentence, "Two dimensions hold two pairs exactly; a third key must overlap with the others, and its value leaks into theirs", is the whole section in one line. Keep verbatim.
- **The proposition and its proof** (lines 135 to 154). Correct, short, and the proof explains the $1/d_k$ ("these $d_k$ expectations sum to $\|\mathbf{k}_i\|^2 = 1$") instead of citing it. The October review asked to keep it; still right.
- **"memories are lost because other pairs interfere with them, not because they are old"** (line 160). An author judging what the formula means. This is the voice the guide asks for.
- **The dual form paragraph** (lines 294 to 300): "The two sides of the duality are two orders of computing the same products." This removes the mystery from "state space duality" in two sentences, and the restriction to linear attention (line 290) is stated.
- **The statement of the limitation** (lines 856 to 862), with `rate = 0.05` then `rate = 0.07`. It closes the section on the question the next one answers and reuses the opening example. One ending, no recap.
- **Exercises 5, 6 and 7.** Exercise 5 derives the delta-rule transition before the reader has seen it. Exercise 7 derives a capacity of $d_k/e$ pairs under decay (checked: maximizing $\ln(x d_k)/x$ gives $d_k/e$ at $x = e/d_k$). Exercise 6 is discussed under MS-6.
- **Surveys are kept out.** Three decay variants in six lines (243 to 250), each "a one-line change", is the right weight.

## Structural findings

**S1. The section's own layer should have one scalar decay per head.** The text teaches the dual form for a scalar decay (`eq_ms-semiseparable`), says Mamba-2 and "the models of the next section train this way" (line 422), and then builds its layer on a decay per key coordinate, for which "no single mask $\mathbf{L}$ multiplies $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$". That choice costs: a second code path in `chunked_linear_attention` (the `L.shape[2] == 1` branch), $d_k m^2$ masks, the fold-and-overflow paragraph (lines 436 to 449), the rank-16 gate with exponent $1/16$, `gate_scale`, and exercise 8 on sub-chunks. It also confounds the comparison the chapter cares about: Gated DeltaNet in :numref:`sec_deltanet` uses a scalar decay per head, so the two layers differ in the write rule, the form of the decay, the chunk size (16 against 32) and the gate parametrization, and :numref:`sec_hybrids` then attributes their difference in recall to the write rule. With a scalar decay here, the layer is the decayed memory the section derived, the chunked function is twelve lines with one mask, and the next section changes one method. The per-coordinate decay keeps its three sentences in "Forgetting Old Pairs" and one exercise. This needs the recall sweep re-run on the development machine; if scalar-decay linear attention then fails for an uninteresting reason, say so and keep the present layer, but state the confound in :numref:`sec_hybrids`.

**S2. No experiment in this section uses the memory.** The interference measurement uses random keys and no learning. The one training run is character-level Penn Treebank, where the layer reaches about 3.1 and the ablation shows that the convolution, SiLU and gate account for the gain over about 3.2. The text concludes honestly that the memory "does not help much on this task". So the reader has built a key-value memory and has not seen a model learn to store and look up one pair. The recall task of :numref:`sec_hybrids` is the natural experiment and is two sections away. Either bring a small version here (4 to 16 pairs, one model, one curve: does a trained layer reach the recall that the capacity law allows?) or say in the introduction that the trained test comes in :numref:`sec_hybrids` and drop the Penn Treebank runs from this section (see S3).

**S3. Penn Treebank cannot separate these models, and three sections in a row find that out.** Stored perplexities for the chapter: RNN 3.12, LSTM 2.96, GRU 3.01, minGRU 3.20, S4D 3.36, Mamba 3.00, gated linear attention 3.09, the ablated layer 3.20, Gated DeltaNet 3.10, hybrid 3.16, transformer 3.27. The plain recurrent network of Basics sits level with both matrix-memory layers. This section, :numref:`sec_deltanet` and :numref:`sec_hybrids` each train on it and each end with a sentence of the form "on this task X matters little". One consolidated table at the end of the chapter would say this once. The comparison sentence here (line 799) lists five models and omits the plain RNN at 3.1, which is the comparison that makes the point.

**S4. Repetition with :numref:`sec_attention-at-scale`, in transposed notation.** Chapter 10 already teaches linear attention as a recurrence with a matrix state ("The state acts as an associative memory, with $\phi(\mathbf{k}_t)$ the address and $\mathbf{v}_t$ the content"), cites Schlag et al. for fast weights, explains that the state is a lossy summary in which writes interfere, and implements the chunked form. This section teaches the recurrence, the unrolling and the fast-weight attribution again, with the state transposed ("the transpose of the layout in" chapter 10) and $d$ renamed $d_k$. The new content here is the worked example, the capacity law, decay, the mask $\mathbf{L}$ and the layer. Decide the owner. My recommendation: chapter 10 keeps the unrolling and the cost argument; this section opens with the two-dimensional example, states the recurrence, and says in one clause that unrolled it is the causal linear attention of `eq_linear-attn` without feature map and normalizer. The subsection "Linear Attention" (lines 94 to 114) shrinks to that clause. And one layout should be used in both chapters; the reader should not have to transpose a state between chapter 10 and chapter 12.

**S5. Not two sections, but the second half must shrink.** There is a seam at `## A Gated Linear Attention Layer`, and the file could be cut there into "the memory and how to compute it" and "the layer". I would not split: with S1 and S2 applied the layer is short. Budget after revision: about 2,800 words of prose.

## Passage-level findings

**MS-1** (minor, language). Under "Writing and Reading Pairs". Quote: "The key width $d_k$ is the $d$ of". The sentence apologizes for a notation clash with chapter 10 instead of removing it. See S4. If the clash stays, give the shape once, "$\mathbf{S}_t$ has one row per value coordinate and one column per key coordinate", and drop the remark about subscripts.

**MS-2** (moderate, explanation). Under "Interference". Quote: "interference is as large as the signal, whose squared norm is one". Twenty lines later the table shows that at $d_k = 64$ every one of 64 pairs is recovered, and 96% of 128, where the squared error is 2. The reader is told the noise equals the signal and then sees perfect recall, with no explanation. The reason is worth a sentence because it is the reason the memory is usable: the error is spread over all $d_v$ coordinates, and deciding which of 256 codebook vectors was stored needs only its component along each candidate, whose variance is $(n-1)/(d_k d_v)$. Fix: after the table, "A squared error of one does not prevent recall. The error points in a random direction, and only its component along a candidate value, of variance about $n/(d_k d_v)$, competes with the signal."

**MS-3** (major, correctness; certain). Under "Interference". Quote: "and doubling $d_k$ doubles the number of pairs held". The stored table (both frameworks) says otherwise: recall 0.96 is reached at $n = 32$ for $d_k = 32$, at $n = 128$ for $d_k = 64$, and 0.94 at $n = 512$ for $d_k = 128$. Doubling $d_k$ quadruples the pairs held at a given recall. The cause is in `matrix_memory`: the codebook lives in $\mathbb{R}^{d_k}$ (`codebook = unit(rng.standard_normal((num_values, d_k)))`), so $d_v$ doubles together with $d_k$, and by MS-2 recall depends on $n/(d_k d_v)$. I re-ran the function with $d_v$ fixed at 64: recall 0.95 at $n = 64$ ($d_k = 32$), 0.96 at 128 ($d_k = 64$), 0.97 at 256 ($d_k = 128$), so the sentence is true of $d_k$ alone and false of the cell above it. Fix, either: give the codebook its own width (`d_v=64` argument) so that the cell shows what the sentence says; or keep the cell and say what it shows, which is the better lesson and the one :numref:`sec_hybrids` needs: "the number of pairs held at a given recall grows with $d_k d_v$, the number of entries of the state".

**MS-4** (moderate, correctness; certain). Under "Interference". Quote: "it grows faster than in proportion to $n$". For zero-mean independent values and correlated keys the error is still proportional to $n - 1$: with overlap $\rho^2 + \epsilon$ it is $(n-1)(\rho^4 + (1-\rho^2)^2/d_k)$, which is 4.6 times the uncorrelated value at $\rho = 0.5$, $d_k = 64$ (measured ratio 4.1 to 4.9 for $n \le 32$). The faster growth in the plot (ratio 12.5 at $n = 512$ in my re-run) comes from the codebook's nonzero mean, a term proportional to $(n-1)(n-2)\|\bar{\mathbf{v}}\|^2$, as the section's own exercise 6 asks the reader to show. The prose presents a property of this codebook as a property of correlated keys. Fix: say what correlation does that a wider key cannot repair: "the error is about four times larger at every $n$, and the extra part, $\rho^4$ per stored pair, does not shrink as $d_k$ grows." Either center the codebook in the cell or leave the superlinear bend to exercise 6 and do not mention it.

**MS-5** (minor, language). Under "Interference". Quote: "written a moment ago: memories are lost because". Two colons in one sentence. Make the last clause its own sentence; it is the best one in the subsection.

**MS-6** (minor, pedagogy). Under "Interference". Quote: "A last curve breaks the assumptions". Good instinct, and the result (half of 64 pairs lost at $\rho = 0.5$) is the more important one for a trained model, whose keys are never random. It gets three sentences after the table and its explanation is an exercise marked hard. Move the one-line formula of MS-4 into the text; keep the codebook-mean part as the exercise.

**MS-7** (minor, thought). Under "Forgetting Old Pairs". Quote: "memory behaves as if it held about the last $1/(1-a)$ pairs". By the proposition's own argument the interference of a decayed memory is $\sum_k a^{2k}/d_k = 1/((1-a^2)d_k)$, so the effective count that enters the capacity law is $1/(1-a^2)$, about half of $1/(1-a)$ for $a$ near one. Exercise 7 uses $a^2$. One clause aligns them: "about $1/(1-a)$ pairs by weight (and the interference of about $1/(1-a^2)$)".

**MS-8** (moderate, explanation). Under "Forgetting Old Pairs". Quote: "is a matrix memory". The claim that Mamba is linear attention with other names is announced in the introduction as one of the section's results and is delivered as a paragraph of prose with five symbols and no equation. This is where a reader checks. Write the two lines: $\mathbf{S}_t = \mathbf{E}_t \odot \mathbf{S}_{t-1} + (\boldsymbol{\Delta}_t \odot \mathbf{x}_t)\,\mathbf{B}_t^\top$, $\mathbf{y}_t = \mathbf{S}_t\mathbf{C}_t$ with $E_{t,cn} = e^{\Delta_{t,c}\lambda_n}$ (this is `eq_selective_ssm` of :numref:`sec_mamba` with the channels stacked as rows; I checked the correspondence). Exercise 1 then keeps parts 2 and 3. The paragraph also deserves its own heading: under "Forgetting Old Pairs" nobody will find it.

**MS-9** (minor, correctness; unverified detail). Under "Forgetting Old Pairs". Quote: "uses an input-dependent scalar per head, as". This matches my understanding of Mamba-2 (a scalar times the identity per head) and of RetNet (a fixed decay per head). Stated correctly as far as I can confirm from memory.

**MS-10** (minor, thought). Under "Unrolling with Decay" and again under "Chunked Computation". Quote: "64 MB per head in 32-bit floats at $T = 4{,}096$". The same number is given at line 393 and again at line 582. State it once, after the timing run, where the comparison with 2 MB is made. (Both numbers are right: $4096^2 \cdot 4$ bytes, and $4096 \cdot 64 \cdot 4$ plus 64 states of $64 \cdot 64 \cdot 4$ bytes.)

**MS-11** (major, focus). Under "Chunked Computation". Quote: "exponent grows from about 20 after 2,000 training steps to between 45 and". This paragraph (lines 436 to 449) reports the authors' debugging of a different implementation on a task the reader meets two sections later ("the recall task of :numref:`sec_hybrids`"), with numbers no cell prints (20, 45 to 75, two of three runs of 32,000 steps). It is guide 4.5 material: written for the authors. The teachable content is two sentences: products of decays can be folded into queries and keys, and the fold computes $\exp(-c_s)$, which overflows once a chunk's decays multiply to less than $e^{-88}$; so compute only decays over a stretch of time, which lie in $(0, 1]$. With S1 applied the paragraph is not needed in the main text at all, since a single mask has no fold to be tempted by. If the layer keeps its per-coordinate decay, keep the two sentences and move the run statistics to a code comment or delete them.

**MS-12** (moderate, correctness; fairly sure, from memory of the paper). Under "The Layer". Quote: "We call it *gated linear attention*, after its decay gate, and build it as". The layer of Yang, Wang, Shen et al. (2024) has, as I recall it, the low-rank gate with exponent $1/16$, an output normalization and a swish output gate, and neither a short convolution nor unit-length queries and keys; those two came with DeltaNet and Gated DeltaNet. "as [the paper] and the layers that followed it do" covers this loosely. It matters because :numref:`sec_hybrids` reports that "the reference implementation of the layer" behaves like this one, and because the ablation here removes the convolution and calls the result "plain". Say which part comes from where in one sentence: "The decay gate and the normalized, gated output are those of gated linear attention; the short convolution and the unit keys are taken from the delta-rule layers of the next section, so that the two layers differ only in their memory." Someone with the papers at hand should confirm.

**MS-13** (moderate, pedagogy). Under "The Layer". Quote: "Four parts surround the memory; the experiments below and in". A bullet list of four design choices at equal weight, each with a half-sentence reason, arrives before the reader has seen the layer do anything. Two of the four (unit keys, output norm) follow from the section's own theory and can be said in the forward-pass sentence. The convolution is the one that matters (it decides the Penn Treebank result here and the recall result later) and its reason is good ("a name followed by its value, can be written as one key and one value"). Lead with that, as a judgment: "One addition matters more than the rest." The exponent $1/16$ and the rank 16 are detail that serves nobody at this level; with S1 they disappear.

**MS-14** (moderate, pedagogy). Under "Language Modeling". Quote: "How much of this comes from the parts around the memory? We remove the". The ablation removes three parts at once and exercise 9 asks the reader to separate them. The text then draws its conclusion about the memory from a difference of 0.1 in perplexity between single runs (3.09 against 3.20; the two frameworks agree to 0.01, so the difference is probably real, but the section does not say what the run-to-run spread is). If the run stays, say which part carries the gain (the convolution, presumably) and give the spread once.

**MS-15** (minor, explanation). Under "Chunked Computation". Quote: "each chunk's writes, decayed to the chunk's end, form". Step 2 of the list is the hard step and is written as a recurrence whose two coefficients are placeholders in words (the decay over chunk $c$, the writes of chunk $c$). Write it in symbols once, for the scalar case: $\mathbf{S}^{(c)} = \gamma^{(c)}\mathbf{S}^{(c-1)} + \sum_{t \in c} L_{m t}\,\mathbf{v}_t\mathbf{k}_t^\top$. The code comment then has something to point to.

## Code and experiments

- `matrix-state-interference-2`: a check of a proved proposition, of the kind the author allows "once or twice"; it is worth keeping because the recall table adds what the proposition does not say. Its prose misreads it twice (MS-3, MS-4) and omits why recall survives (MS-2). The percentages quoted (96%, 72%, 37%, half of 64) match the stored table in both frameworks; the function is seeded and NumPy only, so these digits do survive re-execution.
- `matrix-state-unrolling-with-decay-3` and `matrix-state-chunked-computation-2`: two equivalence checks, seven printed lines of `relative deviation`. They test an implementation, so they can fail, and the chunked one is worth having. The first is subsumed by the second: check the recurrence, the dual form and the chunked form in one cell and print one line per form. With S1 the "per key" rows go.
- `matrix-state-chunked-computation-3` (timing): the prose says the dual and chunked forms "take similar times, about a millisecond or less". The stored PyTorch output has the dual form at 2.45 ms against 1.0 to 1.6 ms for chunks; JAX has 0.25 against 0.13 to 0.25. So the default tab contradicts "a millisecond or less" for the dual form. More important, the run does not show why chunking exists: at $T = 4{,}096$ the dual form is fine, and the memory difference that the prose then argues from (64 MB against 2 MB) is computed by hand, not measured. Either time at a length where the dual form hurts (at $T = 32{,}768$ the mask is 4 GB) and print peak memory, or drop the timing and keep the hand count. The recurrence at 257 ms in PyTorch is mostly interpreter overhead of a Python loop (JAX's scan takes 74 ms); "because its 4,096 steps run one after another" is true but the factor is not a property of the algorithm.
- `matrix-state-language-modeling-2` and `-4`: stored 3.09 and 3.20 (PyTorch), 3.10 and 3.19 (JAX). Prose "about 3.1" and "about 3.2" is at the right precision. See S2, S3 and MS-14 for what the run does not show.
- Numbers no cell prints: the overflow statistics of MS-11; "between 45 and 75"; "two of three runs".
- The JAX tab has a second imports cell (`import random`, line 50 to 53), against the one-imports-cell rule.
- Figure `fig_ms-semiseparable`: carries its explanation well (blocks, arrows along the diagonal). The caption says "Diagonal blocks (blue)" and "below the diagonal (orange)", but every cell is shaded blue; blue and orange are the outline colours. The in-figure annotation "rank $\le d_k$" refers to a fact the text states only in exercise 2, and "(a matmul)" is jargon the text does not use. Say "outlined in blue" and "outlined in orange", and either state the rank fact in the text or drop it from the figure.

## For the chapter review

- This section is the hinge of the chapter: it turns the scalar and diagonal states of :numref:`sec_ssm` and :numref:`sec_mamba` into a matrix and claims that Mamba was one already. That claim needs its equation (MS-8).
- It overlaps chapter 10 (`sec_attention-at-scale`) on the recurrence, the fast-weight reading and chunking, with transposed notation (S4). An owner and one layout should be chosen at the level of the part.
- It assumes `linear_scan` and the timescale initialization from :numref:`sec_ssm`, `SeqLM` and `fit_lm` from :numref:`sec_lstm`. It sets up `decay_mask`, `GatedLinearAttention` and the `memory`/`decays` hooks for the next section. It needs nothing from the appendix.
- Pattern for the chapter: character-level Penn Treebank as the common benchmark separates none of the later models (S3). Every section from here on trains on it and reports that its mechanism does not matter there.
- Pattern for the chapter: results of runs that no cell performs are reported in the prose (here the overflow statistics; many more in :numref:`sec_hybrids`).
- The capacity law depends on $d_k d_v$, the size of the state (MS-3). Stated that way here, it prepares the counting argument of :numref:`sec_hybrids` far better than the present "doubling" sentence.
