# Memory and Precision

Source: `chapter_computational-performance/memory-precision.md` (lines 1 to 1200, up to the slides marker). About 7,200 words outside code, of which about 2,050 are exercises. Stored outputs read: `outputs/pytorch/.../memory-precision.json`, the allocator trace SVG, and `outputs/jax/.../memory-precision.json`.

## Verdict

The first half is close to the bar. It asks a question a reader feels (why does a billion-parameter GPT fit only three sequences on a 24 GiB card), counts the answer by hand ($16N$ bytes, about $20d$ values per token and block, $V$ values for the loss), checks the count against autograd's saved tensors (18.7 against 20), and then lets a measurement of the peak contradict the count by 77% and explains the gap. That is the book's method working, and almost every number recomputes. The second half is weaker. The mixed-precision timing is measured without the roofline prediction the chapter exists to make; the gradient-accumulation subsection spends its only cell on an identity that cannot fail while its two real claims (peak memory follows the micro-batch, time is unchanged) go unmeasured; and the closing "recipe" says the model "then trains" without the one number the chapter could supply, the time of a step. The most important fix is structural and concerns the tabs: PyTorch and JAX give different answers to the section's central questions (19 against 29 values per block, 2,800 against 2,100 tokens, a peak that is linear in one and not in the other), so 31 tab blocks carry the conclusions and several shared sentences are false for one tab, including one that contradicts a stored output ("the term $h$ hardly changes"; in PyTorch it grows from 212 to 306 KiB).

## What works

- **The opening.** "A step that does not fit in the GPU's memory does not run at all." Then 15 GiB "before the model has read a single token". A problem, a number, and a question in twelve lines.
- **The per-token tally** ("Memory per Token"): each stored tensor is named and counted in units of $d$, the total is checked against Korthikanti et al. (their $34\,sbh$ bytes in fp16 with dropout is $16d$ values without it; the citation is accurate), and the reason for the difference is given ("our feed-forward network keeps more because it has a gate").
- **The two-subtraction measurement** (`bytes_per_token`): batch 2 minus batch 1 cancels the weights, two blocks minus one isolates a block. Compact, and the idea transfers.
- **The measured peak that disagrees.** "A count is a model of what the framework stores, and a model can leave things out." The PyTorch gap of 0.38 MiB per token is two fp32 arrays of $V$ values to the kilobyte. This is the best experiment in the section because it could have failed and did.
- **The logits as the hidden term.** "196 KiB, more than a whole block", and later "The output layer and the loss now store more per token than all 18 blocks together." A reader will remember this.
- **tf32 as the fair baseline.** "The fair baseline for a speedup from mixed precision is tf32, not true fp32." A judgment, stated plainly, and correct.
- **Checkpointing is predicted before it is measured** (four forward passes against three; measured 23 to 24% in both tabs).
- **The conditions for accumulation** (equal sizes, batch normalization, clipping waits for the last micro-batch) are the details that matter in practice.
- **Exercises** 1, 3, 5, 7 and 8 are excellent (see below). Figure `fig_memory_anatomy` (rendered) is clean and matches the allocator trace.
- References into Basics all resolve to passages that hold the cited content (`subsec_memory_during_training`, `subsec_count_without_alloc`, `subsec_loss_scaling`, `subsec_activation_checkpointing`, `sec_use_gpu`).

## Structural findings

1. **Tab prose carries the section.** 31 `begin_tab` blocks before the exercises, several of eight to twelve lines, against the guide's "at most a sentence or two". The cause is real: the frameworks store different things. But the result is that the shared text cannot state a conclusion, and it sometimes states one that only PyTorch supports (MEM-6, MEM-8). Proposed rule for the revision: the shared prose states the tally and what must be true of any implementation; one short shared paragraph says that implementations differ and why (unfused normalization and SiLU in Flax, XLA recomputing cheap results); the tabs give only their numbers. Framework mechanics (`saved_tensors_hooks` reference cycles, `nnx.split`, the XLA CPU scheduler flag) move to code comments.
2. **The section is two sections.** "Memory of a Training Step" (count, budget, measured peak; about 2,300 words with code-adjacent prose) answers "where does the memory go". Mixed precision, checkpointing, accumulation and the table answer "how do we fit more". At 5,200 words of body the file is within the range, so a split is optional, but the seam is clean: after the allocator trace, before "## Mixed Precision". If split, the second file could carry the title's "Precision" honestly; at present precision is one subsection of five.
3. **"Precision" is underweight for the title, and that is acceptable only because Basics owns it.** bf16 against fp16 and loss scaling get two sentences and exercises 3 and 4; the numerical cost appears only as "rounding" in the table. `numerics.md` covers formats, master weights, loss scaling and small-update rounding, so nothing is missing from the book. One sentence should say what the reader risks numerically with bf16 here (nothing new: master weights stay fp32, the loss stays fp32) so that "cost: rounding" is not the only word on it. Quantization is not treated, by the chapter introduction's stated scope; fine.
4. **Accumulation does not do what the book promised.** `chapter_optimization/sgd.md` says ":numref:`subsec_mp-accumulation` implements this technique." The subsection checks an identity on a linear model and implements no training loop (MEM-13).
5. **The recipe has no time.** See MEM-14.
6. **Repetition** (guide 7.7), by count in this file: "one to three sequences of 1,024 tokens" three times; "half a million tokens" four times; the four numbers per parameter told in the introduction, again in "Memory per Parameter", in the figure caption and in the Summary, after two sections of Basics already measured it; the two-part split of memory stated in the introduction, at the head of "Memory of a Training Step" and again around :eqref:`eq_mp-budget`; "a fifth to a third" in the body, the table and the Summary; "running example" 16 times.

## Passage-level findings

**MEM-1** (minor, thought: repetition). "Memory of a Training Step". Anchor: "A training step holds two kinds of tensor."
This paragraph restates the third paragraph of the introduction twenty lines earlier, and "Memory per Parameter" then restates the second. Fix: cut the subsection's opening paragraph; begin "Memory per Parameter" with the dependence on the optimizer (8, 12, 16 bytes), which is the new content, and cite $16N$ in a clause.

**MEM-2** (minor, correctness: unverified). Introduction. Anchor: "language models are\ntrained on minibatches of half a million tokens or more" (grep "trained on minibatches of half a million tokens or more").
I believe the cited GPT-3 paper lists batch sizes from 0.5M to 3.2M and that the unit is tokens, but I cannot confirm the unit from memory. Check the table in the paper; the figure is used four times and anchors the accumulation example.

**MEM-3** (minor, explanation). "Memory per Token". Anchor: "The projection after the attention keeps its\nown copy of that output" (grep "own copy of that output").
A reader cannot tell why a second copy exists, or why the query, key and value projections do not also keep copies of their input. The rule that generates the whole tally is never stated: a linear layer keeps its input (for the weight gradient), an elementwise function keeps its input or its output, a product keeps both factors. State the rule in one sentence first; the tally then reads as an application and the reader can redo it for another block.

**MEM-4** (moderate, correctness: unverified and will date). "Memory per Token". Anchor: "keeps a single number per head and token,\nthe logarithm of the softmax normalizer" (grep "the logarithm of the softmax normalizer").
True of FlashAttention's backward pass. Whether `F.scaled_dot_product_attention` dispatches to such a kernel depends on the PyTorch version, the device and the arguments; on a CPU or with an explicit attention mask it stores the $T \times T$ weights. The measured 18.7 values per block supports the claim for the stored run on the RTX 4090 only. Add "on a GPU, in the configuration used here" and tell the reader that the cell's second line is the check.

**MEM-5** (moderate, explanation). After the `bytes_per_token` cell. Anchor: "A block keeps the tally plus whatever intermediate results its kernels\nkeep in addition." (grep "A block keeps the tally plus whatever intermediate results").
The shared sentence is empty, because the result differs by tab: 18.7 values in PyTorch (below the tally of 20), 29.0 in JAX (45% above it). The JAX note explains its surplus well (RMSNorm keeps four vectors, SiLU three arrays), but a JAX reader is left with a hand count that their own cell refutes, and all later budget numbers fork. Fix: say in shared prose that the tally is a lower bound for an implementation with fused normalization and activation, that PyTorch meets it and Flax's unfused layers exceed it by about half, and that this is the memory side of the fusion of :numref:`subsec_comp-fusion`. That turns an embarrassment into a connection to the previous section.

**MEM-6** (moderate, correctness). "Measured Peak Memory". Anchor: "At the peak, a token\ntherefore needs up to $8V$ bytes more than the count." (grep "therefore needs up to")
Shared prose, true for the PyTorch output (905 against 511 KiB, difference 394 KiB $= 8V$ bytes). In the JAX output the plan exceeds the count by 57 KiB (749 against 692), and the JAX note says the plan "is not linear in the number of tokens either" (0.4 MiB per token up to 2,048 tokens, 0.7 beyond) and that two effects "pull in opposite directions". So in JAX the experiment confirms neither the linear budget nor the $8V$ term; the near-agreement is coincidental and the prose says as much. This is the guide's case of an experiment that needs a paragraph of confounders. Fix options: (a) in the JAX tab measure at 4,096 and 8,192 tokens, where the plan may have settled, and report that slope; (b) keep the cell but have the shared prose claim only what both show (the peak exceeds the stored bytes, and the loss is the reason), with the $8V$ accounting in the PyTorch tab.

**MEM-7** (minor, correctness: private API). PyTorch tab before the trace. Anchor: "`torch.cuda.memory._record_memory_history()` records every".
`_record_memory_history`, `_snapshot`, the key `'device_traces'` and the action names `'alloc'` and `'free_completed'` are private and have changed before. The trace is worth having (its description matches the stored plot: start at $12N \approx 2.35$ GiB, jump at the logits, peak near 5.9 GiB at the start of the backward pass, a bump of about one gradient size in the optimizer step). Mark the cell as dependent on the pinned version in a comment, and consider moving the 20 lines of event arithmetic into a `d2l` helper so the notebook shows the plot and not the plumbing.

**MEM-8** (major, correctness: prose contradicts stored output). "Mixed Precision". Anchor: "so the term $h$ hardly changes".
PyTorch prints 212 KiB in fp32 and 306 KiB under autocast: $h$ grows by 44%, presumably because the bf16 logits ($2V$ bytes, 98 KiB) are now kept next to the fp32 log-probabilities. The PyTorch tab's own totals (2.0 to 2.2 MiB per token) use 306, so the numbers are right and the sentence is wrong. In JAX $h$ is unchanged (228 KiB), because the tied output layer computes in fp32. Fix: "The loss keeps its log-probabilities in fp32, so $h$ does not shrink; where the output layer runs in bf16, as under `torch.autocast`, its bf16 logits are stored as well and $h$ grows by half." This also strengthens the section's theme that the logits are the term to watch.

**MEM-9** (minor, explanation). "Mixed Precision". Anchor: "A block stores well over half of its fp32 bytes, not half."
The result is reported before it is explained, and the explanation is split between the shared paragraph (weights) and the tabs (residual stream and normalizations stay fp32). The tally allows a prediction: of the $20d$ values, the $4d$ around the two normalizations stay fp32 and each matrix product keeps a bf16 copy of its fp32 input, so roughly $(4 \cdot 4 + 16 \cdot 2)/80 = 0.6$ before the extra copies (the reviser should redo this count against the saved tensors). Predict the 0.6 from the tally, then show 0.58 and 0.60. Also "raises the largest minibatch\nby a quarter at most" (grep "by a quarter at most") sits beside "15 to 25%" (PyTorch; the stored numbers give 16 to 28%) and "about a quarter" (JAX, 27%); say "by about a quarter at best".

**MEM-10** (major, pedagogy: measured without the model). "Mixed Precision". Anchor: "The step gets faster twice."
The chapter's premise is to predict from $P$ and $\beta$ before timing. Here it does not, and the prediction is easy and instructive. The step performs about $1.1 \times 10^{12}$ FLOPs (forward $4.1 \times 10^{11}$, weight gradients the same, one input gradient $2.75 \times 10^{11}$). At the bf16 rate of 165 TFLOP/s that is 6.7 ms; at half that rate for tf32, 13.3 ms. Stored: bf16 9.67 ms (PyTorch) and 8.14 ms (JAX), tf32 15.0 and 13.8 ms, fp32 23.0 and 21.2 ms. So tf32 runs at about 90% of its roof and bf16 at 70 to 80% of its roof, which is why the bf16 gain is 1.55 to 1.7 and not 2. The text instead says bf16 is "close to the factor of two in arithmetic rate and bytes"; 1.55 is not close to two, and bytes are beside the point for a compute-bound step. Fix: give the FLOP count and the two predicted times before the cell, then read the output as a fraction of the roof and say what takes the rest (the fp32 loss and update, the casts, the small last layer). Quote ratios, not milliseconds; "about a third" and "1.5 to 2 times" survive re-execution, but "close to the factor of two" should go. No rate is given for true fp32 on this card in `tab_gpu_specs`; if one is known, predict that too, otherwise say that the table has no entry.

**MEM-11** (moderate, thought: verbatim duplication with Basics). "Activation Checkpointing". Anchor: "It stores only the\ninputs of selected segments of the network, and during the backward pass it\nreruns the forward computation of each segment" (grep "reruns the forward computation of each segment").
This sentence, and the next two on four forward passes against three, repeat `chapter_builders-guide/gpus-devices-memory.md` (`subsec_activation_checkpointing`) almost word for word, including the argument that a backward pass costs two forward passes. Under the author's rule this is a small result and may be restated, but as one clause, not a copied paragraph. Fix: "Activation checkpointing (:numref:`subsec_activation_checkpointing`) stores only the input of each segment and recomputes the rest, at the cost of one more forward pass, a third more time by the count given there." Then go straight to what is new: the transformer's natural segments and the per-token formula. The checkpointing formula itself is good; state in the text that it predicts the cell too: $2048 \times (40 + 149)$ KiB $\approx 0.37$ GiB against the measured 0.42, and $2048 \times 606$ KiB $\approx 1.18$ against 1.25.

**MEM-12** (minor, explanation). "Activation Checkpointing", JAX tab. Anchor: "`--xla_cpu_enable_concurrency_optimized_scheduler=false`".
A scheduler flag of XLA's CPU backend in a GPU chapter serves almost nobody, is unverified (I believe the flag exists) and will date. It does carry one real idea, which deserves a plain shared sentence: in a compiled program the compiler decides when the recomputation runs, so checkpointing guarantees what is stored, not what the peak is. Keep that sentence, move the flag to a code comment or drop it. The same tab explains why the JAX peak falls by 1.7 where PyTorch's falls by 3; that contrast should be visible to both readers.

**MEM-13** (major, pedagogy: check that cannot fail; promise not kept). "Gradient Accumulation". Anchor: "We check\nthis on a linear model with four micro-batches of 32 examples" (grep "this on a linear model with four micro-batches").
The cell confirms that the mean of four means of equal size is the overall mean (difference $1.2 \times 10^{-7}$ in both tabs). By the author's decision on confirm-only cells this is the kind to cut: linearity of the gradient cannot fail. Meanwhile the subsection's two substantive claims have no measurement: "the peak memory follows\nthe micro-batch" and "A micro-batch of thousands of tokens\nis far into the compute-bound regime, where accumulation adds almost no\ntime". And `sgd.md` promises that this subsection "implements this technique". Fix: replace the cell by an accumulated training step on the two-block GPT of "Measured Peak Memory": the same 4,096 tokens as one batch and as four micro-batches, printing peak memory and time for each. Predicted before the cell: the peak falls from about 3.5 GiB to about 1.5 GiB plus the 0.8 GiB of gradients (which also demonstrates the $16N$ against $12N$ remark under :eqref:`eq_mp-budget`), and the time is unchanged within noise. The $1/m$ scaling then appears as a line of the loop, with the identity as an `assert` if wanted. If code changes are deferred, the prose should at least say that the cell checks only the scaling and point to exercise 7 for the cost.

**MEM-14** (moderate, thought and candour). "Choosing a Memory Technique". Anchor: "The model then trains, but every step".
The recipe (bf16, per-block checkpointing, 128 micro-batches of four sequences) is presented as a success without its cost, and the chapter's model gives the cost in one line: about $6N$ FLOPs per token times $5.2 \times 10^5$ tokens times $4/3$ for checkpointing is about $4 \times 10^{15}$ FLOPs, at best 25 s per optimizer step on this card, weeks for a run of $10^5$ steps. Saying so is the honest ending and the real motivation for the multi-GPU sections that follow: one card can now hold the step but cannot finish the run. Also unexplained: why micro-batches of four sequences when the section has just shown that seven to ten thousand tokens fit with checkpointing. Either use the larger micro-batch (fewer weight reads, by the section's own efficiency argument) or give the reason.

**MEM-15** (minor, pedagogy). Table `tab_mp-techniques`. Anchor: "a loss computed in chunks of tokens".
The table lists as a technique "of this section" something the section does not teach; it exists only as exercise 8. Since the logits are, by the section's own account, the largest per-token term after checkpointing, two or three sentences on the chunked loss in "Activation Checkpointing" (what is chunked, what is recomputed, what it costs) would earn the table row. The first row's symptom, "a step is slow and compute-bound", is a speed symptom in a table of memory techniques; say that mixed precision is here for speed and that its memory effect is small, which is what the section found.

**MEM-16** (minor, language). Throughout. Anchors: "the running example" (16 occurrences), "Mixed precision leaves the running example at one to three sequences".
The prose is plain and free of the October mannerisms, but it is dense with restated numbers and the same handful of phrases. "Mixed precision leaves the running example at one to three sequences of 1,024 tokens per step" is the third telling. After the model is introduced, call it "our GPT" or "the model", and let each number appear once in the body and once in the Summary. Sentences that are hard to parse on first reading: "It passes through up to two such fp32 arrays, 196 KiB per\ntoken each, before the log-probabilities are released" (what is "it", and why two?). Name the two arrays (the gradient of the loss with respect to the log-probabilities and with respect to the logits, if that is what they are; confirm from the trace) instead of "up to two".

**MEM-17** (minor, pedagogy: ending). Summary. Anchor: "and a\nmeasurement of the peak checks the count" (grep "measurement of the peak checks the count").
The measurement did more than check: it added a term of $8V$ bytes per token and lowered the answer from 3,200 to 2,800 tokens. The Summary should state the corrected budget, since that is the section's finding. The section otherwise has a single ending; the recipe paragraph before the Summary is a result, not a recap, and can stay once MEM-14 is addressed.

- `memory-precision-measured-peak-memory-1` and `-measuring-memory-2`: trace and plot. A data plot that teaches; prose matches the stored figure. Private API (MEM-7).
- `memory-precision-mixed-precision-1`: PyTorch 87 KiB (0.58) and head 306 KiB; JAX 140 KiB (0.60) and head 228 KiB. Contradicts the shared sentence on $h$ (MEM-8).

- `memory-precision-activation-checkpointing`: PyTorch 606 to 40 KiB, peak 1.25 to 0.42 GiB, 47 to 58 ms; JAX 936 to 40 KiB, peak 0.78 to 0.45 GiB, 45 to 56 ms. Matches "five vectors of width $d$, 40 KiB", "a fifth to a third longer", "a factor of about three" and "about 1.7". Good experiment.
- `memory-precision-gradient-accumulation`: confirm-only (MEM-13).
- Numbers no cell prints: every figure for the billion-parameter model beyond $16N$ (3,200, 2,800, 10,000, 9,300 tokens) is hand arithmetic from printed values. "about 10,000 tokens" is 10,400 from the stored numbers and "more than three times" holds against 2,800.
- Hardware dependence: the memory numbers do not depend on the GPU model; the timings do. Ratios only are quoted, which is right. The premise "24 GiB" and the tf32 behaviour are RTX 4090 facts and are labelled as such.

## Exercises

Eight problems; strong, and better aimed at understanding than the body's last third.

- 1 (where the peak lands): good; part 2 has a clean answer (more than about $0.6d \approx 1{,}200$ tokens).
- 2 (optimizer-step memory): `foreach`, `fused`, `donate_argnums` and `alias_size_in_bytes` exist as named, to my knowledge. Fine.
- 3 (clipping under accumulation): excellent, a real bug class. Attribution present.
- 4 (fp16 gradient window): the fp16 constants are right ($2^{-14}$, 65,504, $2^{-24}$); `GradScaler` starts at $2^{16}$ (correct). "optax ships no automatic loss scaler" is unverified and will date. This exercise holds most of what the section says about fp16 against bf16; that is acceptable given `numerics.md`.
- 5 (checkpoint placement): with $c = 20$ and $n = 18$ the minimizing segment length is below one, so "the best segment length" is per-block checkpointing and two of the three schedules to compare coincide. That may be the intended insight (a block is so much larger than its input that finer is always better), but then say so in the problem ("show that for $c > n$ per-block checkpointing is optimal"), and note that Basics already derives $s = \sqrt{K}$ for $c = 1$. Part 2 (recursion) is good.
- 6 (selective recomputation): API-heavy but asks a real question. The PyTorch names (`create_selective_checkpoint_contexts`, `torch.ops.aten.mm.default`) are version-sensitive; check on the pinned release.
- 7 (cost of accumulation): excellent; the crossover is at $B_\textrm{micro} \approx 2I^*$, about 330 examples. This is the measurement the body lacks (MEM-13).
- 8 (the loss in chunks): excellent, and the right extended problem.

## For the chapter review

- Position is right: after compilation (speed on one card), before the multi-GPU sections, which it motivates with the 17 GiB that no technique here reduces. The motivation would be stronger with the step time (MEM-14).
- It depends heavily on the Builders' Guide: $16N$, activations, checkpointing, `peak_memory`, mixed precision, loss scaling and tf32 are all introduced there, and three of them are re-explained here at near full length (MEM-1, MEM-11). The chapter review should decide what chapter 13 may assume from chapter 5 and hold every section to it.
- It uses the roofline model only once, in the accumulation paragraph. Memory is predicted by counting, which is the right model for this section, but the two speed claims (bf16, accumulation) should use $P$, $\beta$ and $I^*$ (MEM-10, MEM-13).
- The running example here (SwiGLU, 18 blocks, $d = 2048$) differs from the GELU variant used for one paragraph in `compilation.md`.
- Suspected chapter-wide pattern: tab fragmentation is worst where the frameworks genuinely measure different things (allocator against compiler plan). A chapter-level convention is needed: shared prose states what any implementation must satisfy; tabs state numbers.
- Suspected chapter-wide pattern: numbers for the billion-parameter model are hand-computed in prose from printed values of small models, in each tab separately; they will drift on re-capture unless a cell prints them.
- `fast-transformer.md` cites `subsec_mp-mixed`, `subsec_mp-measuring` and `subsec_mp-checkpointing`; if this file is split, those labels must survive.
