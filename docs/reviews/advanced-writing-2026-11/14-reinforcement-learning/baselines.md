# Baselines and Variance Reduction

Source: `chapter_reinforcement-learning/baselines.md` (lines 1 to 917, slides ignored). About 5,500 words of body prose and 2,350 words of exercises; 22 code cells per tab. PyTorch and JAX tabs, stored outputs for both; the printed numbers agree to the last digit except two (reward-to-go noise 12.4 against 12.3; median 35.0 against 35.5).

## Verdict

The mathematics is right and the section knows something most treatments do not: that a baseline, a step size and a loss divisor are three different things that practitioners confuse, and that lower variance pays off by making a longer step usable, not by making a small step better. The control-variate passage with its Gaussian example ($1.34 \to 0.69$, $b^* = 1.18$, $0.23$ of the variance left; all recomputed and correct) is at the bar. But the section tries to be a derivation, a measurement study and a guide to language-model post-training at once, and it is the hardest of the three to read. It runs to 5,500 words with seven named estimators, two probe problems, three enumerations and a training study that needs four paragraphs of confounders and 200 off-notebook seeds before its result can be stated. The most important fix is focus: make the section answer one question (which weights keep the mean, and what does lower variance give), move the group and length-divisor material for language models to `rl-sequences.md` where GRPO is assembled, and report the training study from what the notebook prints.

## What works

- The diagnosis in the opening: "an action is either ignored or rewarded. A detour on a successful episode is pushed up, and the step into a hole on a failed one is never pushed down." The reader sees what is wrong with the weights before any remedy.
- "Statisticians call this a control variate. Subtract something whose mean you know, and the estimate keeps its mean while its variance can fall." The whole section in two sentences.
- "The Conditional Identity" states the admissibility condition as a rule the reader can apply ("The condition is about the prefix: a quantity that depends on $a_t$ or on anything that happens after it is not covered"), and exercise 1 applies it to five candidates.
- Honesty that causality is not automatically a variance reduction: "Removing a term of mean zero does not by itself lower the variance, though, because the removed term may be correlated with what remains", backed by the step-cost rows (19.5 against 5.8) and a counter-case at a charge of 0.01.
- The Gaussian example of "Control Variates" and :numref:`fig_rl_variance_reduction` (rendered: the parabola in panel (c) with $\bar R$ and $b^*$ marked carries the argument). "above the mean reward because actions far from zero have large scores and, here, large rewards" explains why the optimal constant is not the mean.
- The two questions a reader would ask, asked: "If the mean is unchanged, how can learning change?" and "why not subtract an estimate of $Q(s_t, a_t)$".
- "Batch Centering versus Variance Scaling": centering is a baseline, dividing by $\sigma$ is a step size, with the bound $\sigma \le 1/2$ predicting "at least twice as long" before the measurement gives about three.
- The loss-divisor cell: four divisors on one batch, three parallel and one tilted (cosine 0.937), sizes 0.868 against 0.074 exactly as the mean episode length 47/4 predicts. Compact and it could have failed.
- "Lower variance does not make a small step better, since many small steps average the noise away; it makes a longer step usable". The best sentence in the section, and the design that earns it (three arms with a provably common mean, compared at one step size) is sound.
- The stall mechanism for reward-to-go at $\eta = 16$ ("every later episode returns zero, every weight is zero, and the policy never moves again") is specific and explains the failures in the table.

## Structural findings

1. **The section has three subjects.** (a) Which modifications of the weight keep the mean (the lemma, the conditional identity, reward-to-go, baselines, control variates, the advantage). (b) Bookkeeping choices of implementations (batch centering, $\sigma$, leave-one-out, the group, the loss divisor), about half of it addressed to language-model training. (c) A measurement study (frozen policy, then training at two step sizes). Subject (a) plus the frozen-policy table is a complete section of about 3,000 words. Recommended: keep (a), keep batch centering, normalization and the leave-one-out identity (they are rows of the comparison), keep the lake half of the loss divisor, and move "The Group as the Batch" and the GRPO, DAPO and Dr. GRPO sentences of "The Loss Divisor" to `chapter_deep-reinforcement-learning/rl-sequences.md`, which assembles GRPO and already cites the same two variants (the cell IDs `rl-sequences-the-group-is-the-baseline-2` and `-3` show the cells came from there). This removes about 900 words, three cells and exercises 6 and 10. If the author prefers to keep the group material here, the seam for a split is at "## Centering, Scaling, and Normalization".
2. **Repeats `policy-gradient.md`.** The opening redisplays :eqref:`eq_reinforce` and re-quotes its noise; "The Zero-Mean Lemma" restates and re-proves the identity used in the proof of :eqref:`eq_pg_theorem`; "Reward-to-Go and Causality" re-derives the theorem's first form; the paragraph on the dropped $\gamma^t$ repeats the theorem's third consequence with the same two citations; the probe policy and its linear system are rebuilt and re-explained. With the lemma owned by the previous section (see `policy-gradient.md`, structural finding 1), this section can open on the conditional identity and obtain the reward-to-go form in two lines by citing :eqref:`eq_pg_theorem`.
3. **The variance-optimal baseline is in an exercise.** The section's title question, which baseline minimizes variance, is answered in the text only for the one-dimensional Gaussian example. The general answer ($b^*(s) = \mathbb{E}[\|\boldsymbol\psi\|^2 \hat G]/\mathbb{E}[\|\boldsymbol\psi\|^2]$, a mean of the return weighted by the squared score) is in exercise 3, and the fact that $V^\pi$ is not it is in exercise 9. See BL-5.
4. **The training study is a research experiment.** By the guide's own test (section 9: "If an experiment needs a paragraph of confounders before its result can be read, it is a research experiment"), "Step Size and Variance in Training" needs simplifying. It has six arms at one step size, three at another, a paragraph on why matching step lengths does not work, a paragraph on seed noise with resampling statistics, and results quoted from 200 seeds that the notebook does not run. The insight is worth keeping; the apparatus is not. See BL-10 to BL-13.
5. **Naming.** "Five Estimators Compared" compares seven: the five, a "sixth row, **causal**", and an "exact baseline" arm introduced inside a paragraph about `train`. Name the set once, in one list, and title the section for its subject.

## Passage-level findings

**BL-1** (minor, language). Heading: introduction. Quote: "In the previous section we trained a policy by raising the log-probability". A recap opening with a redisplayed equation. One clause of connection is enough; open on the second paragraph's observation that on the lake a weight is either zero or $\gamma^{T-1}$.

**BL-2** (moderate, thought). Heading: The Zero-Mean Identity. Quote: "One identity, already used in the proof of :eqref:`eq_pg_theorem`, guarantees this". The text itself says the identity was already used, then proves it again as a Lemma, having first re-derived the softmax case from a redisplayed :eqref:`eq_softmax_score`. See structural finding 2. If the Lemma stays here, the theorem's proof in the previous section must cite forward, which is worse.

**BL-3** (moderate, correctness). Heading: The Conditional Identity. Quote: "We can therefore multiply any score in the REINFORCE estimator by such a quantity". As written this is false: multiplying the score in $R(\tau)\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta$ by a prefix-determined $c$ gives $c\,R(\tau)\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta$, whose mean is not the gradient. What the identity allows is adding or removing a term of the form $c$ times a score. Rewrite: "We can therefore add to the estimator, or remove from it, any term of the form $c\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)$ with such a $c$, without moving the average."

**BL-4** (minor, explanation). Heading: Reward-to-Go and Causality. Quote: "Implementations, ours included, also drop the factor $\gamma^t$". Candid and correct, and the numbers that follow (cosine 0.991, about 8 degrees, mean 1.4 times longer) are consistent with the stored table (0.987 from 2,000 draws; 1.38). From here on, "same mean" and "unbiased" mean "same mean as :eqref:`eq_rtg`", not "equal to $\nabla_\theta J$". The text is careful about this in most places but the Summary says "without changing the expected gradient". Fix the convention in one sentence here ("from now on we compare estimators by whether they share the mean of :eqref:`eq_rtg`") and use it in the Summary.

**BL-5** (moderate, explanation). Heading: Control Variates. Quote: "The best coefficient is $c^*$, computed separately for each state". The sentence then sends the reader to an exercise for the formula. State it: for a vector score the trace of the covariance is minimized by $b^*(s) = \mathbb{E}[\|\boldsymbol\psi\|^2 \hat G \mid s]/\mathbb{E}[\|\boldsymbol\psi\|^2 \mid s]$, the average return weighted by the squared length of the score. Then, in "The Advantage and the Learned Baseline", say what the reader will otherwise assume wrongly: $V^\pi(s)$ is the unweighted average, so it is not the variance-optimal baseline; it is used because it can be learned by regression, because it turns the weight into an advantage, and because the gap is usually small (the Gaussian example quantifies it: the variance at $b = \bar R$ is $0.69^2 = 0.47$ against $0.23 \times 1.34^2 = 0.41$ at $b^*$). "The natural state-dependent baseline" is honest wording but does not tell the reader this.

**BL-6** (moderate, pedagogy). Heading: The Leave-One-Out Baseline. Quote: "We verify both statements on a problem small enough to enumerate". The two statements are one line of algebra ($R_i - b_i = \frac{n}{n-1}(R_i - \bar R)$) and one application of independence. The cell introduces a new two-state problem, 20 lines of enumeration, and prints two `True`s. Eighty lines later, "We check this exactly for groups of two" enumerates the same identity on the verifier task and prints two more. Under the author's decision on confirming cells, neither is needed; keep at most the first, and only because exercise 5 extends its arrays. The sentence "Both identities hold to machine precision" is the formula that follows each of these checks.

**BL-7** (moderate, pedagogy). Heading: The Group as the Batch. Quote: "every $K\geq2$ reaches the ceiling of $0.5$ within the same budget". The cell is introduced by "How much does the bias matter?" and its output is `0.062` for $K=1$ and `0.500` for every other $K$. The $K=1$ row cannot fail (every weight is zero by construction), and the other four rows all saturate, so the sweep shows nothing about the factor $(K-1)/K$ or about group size. This is the guide's "showcase where both arms reach the maximum score". Either shorten the budget until the arms differ and read the difference, or drop the sweep and keep the sentence about $K = 1$. "for each of the ten seeds we tried" is a number no cell prints.

**BL-8** (minor, thought). Heading: The Loss Divisor. Quote: "Language-model training faces the same choice with responses as episodes and tokens as steps". Three methods named in one sentence, none of which the reader has met, inside a FrozenLake measurement. My recollection agrees with the attributions (GRPO averages over each response's tokens; DAPO averages over all tokens of the group; Dr. GRPO removes the length and $\sigma$ divisors), but I hold the DAPO detail with moderate confidence only: mark unverified. See structural finding 1 for where this belongs.

**BL-9** (minor, explanation). Heading: Estimators at a Frozen Policy. Quote: "`estimate` sums the weighted scores of a batch with `d2l.tabular_score` and divides". This paragraph narrates six helpers in turn before the reader knows what will be measured. It is a recurring pattern in the section (also "`backward_scan` runs this recursion for any vector and factor", "`exact_gradient` differentiates `d2l.policy_objective`", "`train` is REINFORCE by plain SGD with one of the `weightings`", "`to90` finds the first update"). Lead with the three quantities in plain words (how long the mean is, which way it points, how noisy a draw is relative to its own mean) and let docstrings carry the rest.

**BL-10** (minor, thought). Heading: Estimators at a Frozen Policy. Quote: "The ordering of the other rows also belongs to this policy". True and important, and it undercuts the paragraph before it: in the step-cost rows printed by the same cell, centering does not help at all (5.4 for reward-to-go, 5.6 centered, 5.8 normalized), which the prose does not read. One sentence: "With the charge, centering by the batch mean no longer helps (5.6 against 5.4); only the state-dependent baseline does (3.0)." That is a stronger lesson than the 40 percent quoted for the lake rows.

**BL-11** (moderate, correctness). Heading: Step Size and Variance in Training. Quote: "Centering is no faster than the plain return and slower than reward-to-go". The stored medians over twenty seeds are 58.5 (centered), 63.0 (return) and 51.0 (reward-to-go). The first clause has the stored direction reversed (centered is 4.5 updates faster). The second builds an explanation ("because it shrinks the step") on a gap of 7.5 updates, while the section's own noise paragraph says that two arms with identical means differ by more than six updates one time in seven at twenty seeds. Say only what survives: normalization is clearly fastest (32 against 50 to 63) and takes steps about three times as long as centering; the other four cannot be ordered at twenty seeds.

**BL-12** (moderate, correctness). Heading: Step Size and Variance in Training. Quote: "over 200 seeds outside the notebook, 51 for reward-to-go and the value baseline and 50 for the exact baseline". The two paragraphs quote a 200-seed study the reader cannot run or see: "its median falls from 50 to 20 updates, and that of reward-to-go from 51 to about 30", "About one reward-to-go run in eight never reaches 90 percent, against none of the 200", "a median of 26 updates, and one run in twenty". The notebook's own table, on 100 seeds, says 35 (35.5 in JAX), 15 of 100 (nearer one in seven), 26 and 5 of 100, 20.5 and 0. The prose should read the printed table and may add one clause that a larger run agrees. As it stands the reader compares "about 30" with a printed 35.0.

**BL-13** (moderate, focus). Heading: Step Size and Variance in Training. Quote: "Drawing twenty of the 200 seeds at random, the medians of reward-to-go and the exact baseline". A paragraph of resampling statistics from the same off-notebook data ("about one time in seven", "49 draws of 50", "more than fifteen updates"). The lesson (twenty seeds reorder close medians; report ranges; check on more seeds) is right and takes two sentences. The method paragraph before it ("Matching the average step length does not remove this ...") is likewise a design note for the authors; exercise 8c covers it.

**BL-14** (minor, focus). Heading: Step Size and Variance in Training. Quote: "How deterministic does the policy become along the states the agent visits?" The entropy plot and its paragraph are not called for by the argument and end on a side remark about averaging over sixteen states. The stored plot shows both medians below 0.1 nats by update 40. Its one useful sentence ("A stalled run is as deterministic as a successful one") belongs with the stall mechanism two paragraphs earlier; the `entropy` helper, the third column of every curve and the cell can go.

**BL-15** (moderate, language). Heading: Step Size and Variance in Training. Quote: "We can now say which weights may be used, how much variance each removes". The "We can now" recap. The two sentences that follow it (a cart "never visits the same state twice") are a good bridge; keep them as the last lines of the body.

**BL-16** (minor, explanation). Heading: introduction. Quote: "Episodes are cut after 200 steps." The previous section removed the time limit on principle ("the sum of rewards of a cut episode is not its return") and this one restores a cut in a clause. The justification is given and is sound; add that the cut episodes are treated as failures with return zero, so that the reader does not wonder whether a bootstrap is needed.
