# Policy Gradient

Source: `chapter_reinforcement-learning/policy-gradient.md` (lines 1 to 686, slides ignored). About 4,900 words of body prose and 1,800 words of exercises; 14 code cells per tab. The file carries PyTorch and JAX tabs with stored outputs for both (the chapter index says "The code is in PyTorch and JAX"); the two stores agree on every printed number.

## Verdict

This is good textbook teaching, and its first third is at the bar. The opening turns behavior cloning into REINFORCE by one change of weight and asks whether the guess is principled; the two-action "smallest case" then gives the reader the estimator, its mean and its noise in ten lines, and predicts the flat start of the lake's training curve before the curve is drawn. Every derivation I checked is correct, including the policy gradient theorem with its discounted occupancy and the $1/(1-\gamma)$ factor, and every stored number matches the prose. The weaknesses are in the second half. The policy gradient theorem arrives after the experiments, is used by nothing in this section, and its proof and first consequence are then taught again at the head of `baselines.md`. Unbiasedness is tested by sampling twice (verifier task, lake) after being proved. The cost of on-policy data is argued with a comparison that does not show it. The single most important fix: decide where the zero-mean property of the score and the theorem live, state them once, and let the other section cite them.

## What works

- The opening. "An obvious guess keeps the update and changes the weights ... Is this guess principled?" The reader holds the algorithm before the derivation and wants to know whether it is right. Preserve it word for word.
- "The Smallest Case". One state, two actions, $J = p$; the estimate's mean $p(1-p)$, its relative standard deviation $\sqrt{(1-p)/p}$, and the sentence "the estimate is right on average and useless in almost every single draw: it is usually zero, and occasionally about $1/p$ times too large". Then the lake at $p = 0.014$: ratio $8.4$, and "Four batches in five then give an estimate of zero". All numbers recomputed and correct ($p_0 = 0.01394$, $0.986^{16} = 0.799$). This is the MacKay move: a sharp small example that predicts the experiment.
- "The kernel still decides which trajectories we sample, but the estimator never evaluates it. This is what makes the method *model-free*, and it names the assumption the derivation needs". The assumption is then tested by exercise 1 (the recommender whose clicks depend on $\theta$), which is the best exercise in the section.
- The regularity remark after the log-derivative trick ("The sum over trajectories is infinite ... Moving the gradient inside is still allowed") says in two sentences why the interchange holds, which most treatments skip.
- The choice of the calm lake is argued, with a number: the exact gradient is one third as long on slippery ice at the uniform start. I checked the identity behind exercise 4c ($Q_\textrm{slip}(s,a) - V = -\tfrac13(Q_\textrm{calm}(s,\bar a) - V)$); it holds.
- "A sampled step never lowers the success on this task, because the only weighted score is that of the correct response." A true, specific observation that the next section relies on (baselines make failures count).
- Reading the learned policy grid: "one of the three shortest paths to the goal, chosen by the episodes that happened to succeed first" (there are three; the stored grid shows 0, 1, 2, 6, 10, 14, 15).
- Seed handling is honest: twelve seeds, median with a band from slowest to fastest, ranges in the prose ("after 25 to 50 updates"), all matching the store (25 to 49; 3,210 to 6,191 steps).
- "Plateaus and Convergence" states what is proved, by whom, and what can still go wrong, in two paragraphs.

## Structural findings

1. **The zero-mean property of the score has no owner.** It is used inline in the proof of the theorem here ("the score of $a_t$ has mean zero given $s_t$: ..."), then stated as a Lemma with the same one-line proof in `baselines.md`, then extended to the conditional identity there, and the reward-to-go form (the theorem's first consequence) is derived a second time under "Reward-to-Go and Causality". Under the repetition rule one section owns this. Recommended: state the Lemma here, in "The Score Function", right after :eqref:`eq_softmax_score` (it is a property of the score and takes two lines), use it by name in the theorem's proof, and have `baselines.md` cite it and add only what is new there (the conditional form and subtraction).
2. **The theorem is placed where nothing uses it.** "The Policy Gradient Theorem" sits between the experiments and "Costs and Limitations". The section's code weights by the whole return throughout, so the reader meets a theorem whose first consequence ("an estimator that drops them has the same mean and usually less noise") is acted on only in the next section. Two workable orders: (a) move the theorem up to follow "The REINFORCE Estimator", so that derivation precedes all experiments, or (b) move it to open `baselines.md`, where the causal estimator is its immediate use, and keep here only the sentence that rewards before an action carry no signal. I prefer (a) with finding 1, because the section is named for the result and exercise 4 and exercise 8 depend on it.
3. **Three checks of one proved fact.** The estimator is proved unbiased, then `policy-gradient-the-reinforce-estimator-5` tests it on the verifier task (10,000 draws), then `policy-gradient-unbiased-and-how-noisy-2` tests it on the lake (50 draws), and exercise 6 tests it a third time. Under the author's decision on confirming cells, keep one. The lake cell teaches something no derivation gave (a draw at $n = 4$ is off by 3.85 times the gradient's length); keep it for that number and drop the test framing. See PG-6 and PG-12.
4. **Library plumbing in the middle of the argument.** "The Lake" introduces `Batch`, `rollout`, `policy_step`'s divisor and Adam in about 450 words before the first curve. `Batch` records `next_obs` and `term`, which this section never reads ("the flag matters only once episodes are cut"). The reader needs: episodes are stored flat, `episode_returns` gives $R(\tau)$, `per_step` spreads it over the steps. The termination discussion belongs where truncation first matters (`deep-rl.md`, "Time Limits").
5. **Appendix.** The section never points to the appendix, and it reads without it, which is right. One pointer is owed: `subsec_mdl-stochastic-gradient-estimators` in `chapter_mdl-calculus/mdl-integral-calculus.md` states the same score-function identity (:eqref:`eq_mdl-score-function-gradient`), names it REINFORCE, and adds the moving-support counterexample. A clause at "The Log-Derivative Trick" would serve the reader who wants the general statement.
6. **Length.** About 4,900 words of body is right for the content. Finding 4 and PG-6 recover roughly 500 words.

## Passage-level findings

**PG-1** (moderate, explanation). Heading: The Score Function. Quote: "Its components sum to zero, so the score has no component along the one direction". True, and a nice remark, but it is the less important of two zero sums. The fact the rest of this section and all of the next depend on is the other one: the score averaged over the policy's own action is zero, $\sum_a \pi_\theta(a\mid s)\,(e_a - \pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s)) = 0$. A reader who meets "the score of $a_t$ has mean zero given $s_t$" in the theorem's proof 280 lines later may think it is this sentence. Add the Lemma here (see structural finding 1) and distinguish the two: "summed over coordinates for a fixed action" against "averaged over actions".

**PG-2** (minor, pedagogy). Heading: The Score Function. Quote: "We check the formula against automatic differentiation of `Policy.log_prob` at a random table". A check of two lines of calculus that prints `True`. It is one of four cells in the three sections whose whole output is `True` (here, `baselines-the-leave-one-out-baseline-2`, `rl-sequences-the-group-is-the-baseline-3`, `deep-rl-the-gaussian-policy-2`). This one tests the library against the text, which has some value at first use; if one of the four goes, it is not this one, but the JAX tab note that follows it is plumbing and can become a code comment.

**PG-3** (minor, explanation). Heading: The REINFORCE Estimator. Quote: "Twelve samples move it to $0.32$, where the exact gradient would give $0.52$." The reader cannot tell whether $0.32$ against $0.52$ is a bad draw or a typical one. It is typical: the twelve-sample estimate has a relative standard deviation of $0.74$ (recomputed; single-sample standard deviation $1.34$, as `baselines.md` says). Say so in a clause, since noise is the section's theme; exercise 9b then confirms what the text stated.

**PG-4** (moderate, pedagogy). Heading: The Verifier Task. Quote: "The error of the mean of $M$ estimates keeps falling as $M$ grows, on average like $0.66/\sqrt M$, and shows no floor: the estimator is unbiased." The stored line is `M=10: 0.126, M=100: 0.061, M=1000: 0.047, M=10000: 0.009`. Against $0.66/\sqrt M$ these are 0.6, 0.9, 2.2 and 1.4 times the predicted rate, so the sequence barely falls from 100 to 1,000, and the next sentence ("a single average can land well below that rate or up to about twice above it") exists to excuse that. Four points from one seed cannot show "no floor", and the estimate here is a binomial count whose mean was computed exactly two paragraphs earlier, so the check cannot teach anything beyond the algebra. Cut the `M` line and the two sentences. Keep the RMS line (0.66, a prediction that could have failed if the binomial argument were wrong), the empty-group line, and the one-step line.

**PG-5** (minor, thought). Heading: The Lake. Quote: "while the noise of a one-episode estimate is about the same, so a batch must be nine times larger for the same precision". "About the same" is asserted and is the half of the argument the exercise does not derive (exercise 4c gives only the factor $1/3$ in the mean). Either give the reason in a clause (at the uniform start both maps induce the same chain, so the returns and the visited states have the same distribution, and only which command produced each move differs) or soften to "of similar size".

**PG-6** (minor, explanation). Heading: The Lake. Quote: "`Batch` stores the transitions of several episodes in flat arrays". See structural finding 4. The paragraph spends three sentences on a flag that "matters only once episodes are cut". Move those to `deep-rl.md`.

**PG-7** (moderate, thought). Heading: The Lake. Quote: "Two details differ from :eqref:`eq_reinforce`." The section's first run of REINFORCE is therefore not the algorithm just derived: the divisor is the step count and the optimizer is Adam. The text is candid about both, and the reason for Adam is good. But the first difference is avoidable. `baselines.md` removes it with one factor ("multiplying every weight by $N/n$ turns the first average into the second"); doing the same here leaves one difference instead of two, and makes the later frozen-policy measurement (`reinforce_estimate`, which divides by $n$) describe the estimator the training loop ran.

**PG-8** (minor, language). Heading: The Lake. Quote: "between visits Adam's momentum carries the last change on for a few dozen updates, then they stay put". Optimizer plumbing inside the reading of a policy, with a number no cell prints. The point of the paragraph is that unvisited rows receive no gradient; end the sentence at "which becomes rare as the path firms up".

**PG-9** (moderate, correctness of presentation). Heading: Statement and Proof. Quote: "**Proposition (policy gradient theorem).** *For the objective". The statement and proof are correct (checked: the split of the return at $t$, the Markov step giving $\gamma^t Q^{\pi_\theta}(s_t,a_t)$, the regrouping by state, the factor $1/(1-\gamma)$). What is missing is the hypotheses, which the Proposition form promises: a finite MDP, $\gamma < 1$ (or episodes that end with probability one), a policy differentiable in $\theta$ with $\pi_\theta(a\mid s) > 0$, and dynamics that do not depend on $\theta$. All four are scattered in the earlier text; one line collects them. Also say what the second form is for: it is the form in which the state weighting can be read off, and the one exercise 4 turns into $\rho_\gamma(s)\,\pi_\theta(a'\mid s)\,(Q - V)$. That tabular formula is the most useful single consequence for this chapter (it explains the plateaus of the last subsection) and deserves to be in the text, not only in an exercise.

**PG-10** (moderate, explanation). Heading: Consequences. Quote: "The theorem has three consequences." One paragraph carries three results of very different weight, and the third (the dropped $\gamma^t$) is the discounting convention that governs every later experiment in this chapter and the next. As written the reader learns that "many implementations" drop the factor and that the result "is not the gradient of any objective", but not what this book's code does: this section's code keeps the discount inside $R(\tau)$ and is unbiased, while from `baselines.md` on the code drops $\gamma^t$. Say that here in one sentence, and give the practical reason the convention survives (with $\gamma^t$ kept, steps late in an episode receive almost no weight, so most of the data is wasted; exercise 8c asks for this, but the text should not leave the main reason to an exercise). The claims about :cite:`Nota.Thomas.2020` match my memory of the paper.

**PG-11** (moderate, thought). Heading: The On-Policy Data Requirement. Quote: "On the same map, tabular Q-learning with $\alpha = 0.9$ found, in almost every run". The paragraph sets 3,000 to 7,000 steps (REINFORCE, batch mean at 0.7) against "about 3,500 steps" (Q-learning, whole run). Those are the same order, the two criteria differ (a stochastic policy's batch return against the existence of a greedy path), and the 27,000 steps of the full run reflect only the choice of 256 updates. So the comparison does not show the cost the subsection is about. The real evidence for that cost comes later, on Pendulum (a few hundred thousand steps against about ten thousand for SAC). Here, state the requirement, count the steps, and drop the Q-learning comparison or say plainly that on this small map the two are comparable.

**PG-12** (minor, pedagogy). Heading: Unbiasedness and Variance. Quote: "so the test finds no bias; exercise". A fifty-draw test with a relative error of 0.1 to 0.5 has almost no power, as the exercise admits ("cannot reveal a bias smaller than the noise of fifty draws"). The cell's lasting content is the first column (3.85, 1.78, 0.96: the $1/\sqrt n$ law and the size of the noise at a real policy), which `baselines.md` builds on. Rewrite the paragraph around that and reduce the bias remark to a clause.

**PG-13** (minor, correctness). Heading: Plateaus and Convergence. Quote: "when the start distribution gives every state positive probability". The hypothesis is stated, but the lake of this section has a point-mass start at cell 0, so the cited theorem does not cover the experiment as run, and the reader is not told. I believe the conclusion about stationary points survives on the states reachable from the start, since every softmax policy visits all of them with positive probability (unverified against the papers; :cite:`Agarwal.Kakade.Lee.ea.2021` assume full support). One clause: "on the lake the start is a single cell, and the statement then holds for the cells reachable from it".

**PG-14** (moderate, language). Heading: Plateaus and Convergence. Quote: "We can now improve a policy from its own episodes and their rewards alone". The "We can now" recap before the Summary, which the author has asked to remove. Its second and third sentences repeat the Summary ("right on average", "more than three times the gradient's length"). Keep only the question that leads on: how much of the noise can be removed without moving the mean.
