# Dynamic Programming

Source: `chapter_reinforcement-learning/value-iter.md` (to the `<!-- slides -->` marker). About 7,000 words of body prose plus 2,300 words of exercises; 17 code cells; five figures and two cell-drawn plots.

## Verdict

This is one of the better sections I have read in the book and, on rigor and on reading its own results, better than the dynamic-programming chapter of Sutton and Barto. The contraction property is proved, not asserted, in five lines; uniqueness, the geometric rate, the stopping certificate and the optimality of greedy policies are derived from it; policy improvement and potential-based shaping are proved; and the lake is then taken apart cell by cell to explain why the optimal policy walks away from the goal. Every number in the prose matches the stored output, and I reproduced all of them on an independent NumPy model of the lake. What keeps it from a 5 across the board: the one result everything rests on (existence of an optimal stationary policy) is cited where five more lines with tools already on the page would prove it; the policy-iteration demonstration is degenerate (the first greedy step is already optimal, so nothing iterates); the value definition is not quite the return of the previous section; the "optimal" policy is judged by a success rate for which it is not optimal, without saying so; and reward shaping is taught twice and its usefulness asserted but not shown. The most important fix is the foundation: make the definitions exact (VI-1, VI-2) and close the existence gap (VI-3).

## What works

- **The equation of cell 14** (under "The Expectation Form"): $V^\pi(14) = \frac13 + \frac{\gamma}{3}[V^\pi(13) + V^\pi(14)]$, followed by "The unknown $V^\pi(14)$ appears on both sides, because a slip can leave the agent where it was". One line shows why the equations must be solved together and why a game tree is easier. It is checked later with the computed values. This is the page I would show someone as the book's standard.
- **The derivation of the optimality equation by two comparisons** (`subsec_vi_optimality`): the bracket of $\pi^*(s)$ cannot exceed the largest bracket, and no bracket can exceed $V^*(s)$ because "take $a$ once, then follow $\pi^*$" is a policy. Correct, short, and it uses the history-dependent clause of the proposition honestly.
- **Calm ice before contraction.** Value iteration is first a shortest-path wavefront with a closed form ($\gamma^{j-1}$, six sweeps, :numref:`fig_rl_value_wavefront`), and only then "Slips change one thing ... No finite number of sweeps then reaches them, and we must ask whether the iteration converges at all, how fast, and when to stop." The three questions are then answered in that order. This is motivation done properly.
- **Stopping rules.** The naive rule stops at sweep 128 with a true error eleven times the tolerance, "and nothing the rule can see reveals this"; the certificate stops at 164, six sweeps late. An experiment that could have come out otherwise and that changes what the reader will code. Sutton and Barto give the naive rule without comment.
- **"A policy depends only on which action value is largest, so it can settle long before the values do"** (sweep 14 against 164). A quotable sentence with a number behind it.
- **"Commands That Turn Away from the Goal".** The per-cell table (toward, into a wall, away; one-step hole risk for $\pi^*$, the shortest path and the least available) and the visit-count decomposition ("The shortest path fails less on its own route than in the cells that slips carry it to") are the most enjoyable pages of the two sections. The reader learns what an optimal policy under noise looks like, which no theorem tells them.
- **The $\gamma = 1$ remark after the contraction results** ("At $\gamma = 1$ the proof shows only that $\mathcal{T}$ does not increase distances") and the reuse of the always-*up* policy as the counterexample. Hypotheses are stated where they matter.
- **Exercises.** The best set I have seen in the main text: the certificate, the undiscounted lake, the measured rate as a spectral radius, the performance-difference identity, the action gap.

## Structural findings

1. **Length and a possible seam.** At 7,000 words the section is inside the stated range, and its parts depend on each other (the anatomy of $\pi^*$ needs `policy_evaluation` at $\gamma = 1$; shaping needs `policy_value`). If it is to be split, the seam is before "The Optimal Policy on Slippery Ice": the first file would be the method (values, Bellman equations, value iteration, contraction, policy evaluation and iteration, about 4,600 words), the second its application to the lake (the anatomy of $\pi^*$ and reward shaping, about 2,400 words, which would also absorb the "Reward Design" material of `sec_mdp`). I would not split. I would shorten: remove the second telling of shaping's setup (VI-14), the three assertion sentences (VI-9), and the backup-diagram panel that belongs to the next section (VI-6). That is about 400 words.

2. **The reader waits 1,700 words for a promised explanation.** Under "Slippery Ice" the text says "In four cells the slippery optimum points away from the goal, and in several others into a wall; :numref:`subsec_vi_slippery_ice` explains each of them." Then come stopping rules, policy evaluation, policy iteration and generalized policy iteration. The order is forced by the code (the explanation uses `policy_evaluation` and `uniform`), but the wait can be shortened by giving one cell's explanation at once (cell 10: *left* cannot reach hole 11 and still slides to 14 one time in three) and deferring the rest.

3. **What the section proves and what it cites.** Proved: the Bellman expectation and optimality equations (given existence), contraction, uniqueness, rate, optimality of every greedy policy of $V^*$, policy improvement, finite termination of policy iteration, shaping invariance. Cited: existence of a uniformly optimal deterministic stationary policy (Puterman), the certificate (exercise), the converse of the shaping theorem. That is a good ratio, and the hypotheses ($\gamma < 1$, finite MDP, absorbing terminal states) are stated at each result. The one gap is VI-3.

4. **Missing for an implementer:** the cost of a sweep in arithmetic operations. The section counts "backups" ($|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{A}| = 64$ per sweep) but a backup with a dense kernel costs $|\mathcal{S}|$ multiplications, so a sweep is $O(|\mathcal{S}|^2|\mathcal{A}|)$ against $O(|\mathcal{S}|^3)$ for a solve; with at most three successors per pair, as on the lake, a sparse sweep is $O(|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{A}|)$. One sentence under "Policy Iteration" would make the remark "the cubic cost of a solve is what makes sweeps preferable" checkable.

## Passage-level findings

**VI-1** (moderate, correctness; certain). Under "Policies and Values". Quote: "collects the discounted return $\sum_t \gamma^t r(s_t, a_t)$ of :numref:`sec_mdp`". The return of `sec_mdp` is $R(\tau) = \sum_t \gamma^t r_t$ with the *realized* rewards; that section distinguishes them explicitly ("The reward $r_t$ in a trajectory is the realized one that the simulator pays; the expected reward $r(s_t, a_t)$ is its average"). The sum written here is a different random variable with the same expectation. The definition of $V^\pi$ is therefore correct in value but is not "the average return" of the object the reader was given. The expectation is also written with a subscript that lists the sampling rules and does not show the conditioning on the start state. Fix: define $V^\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}_\pi[R(\tau) \mid s_0 = s]$ and $Q^\pi(s, a) = \mathbb{E}_\pi[R(\tau) \mid s_0 = s, a_0 = a]$, say once that $\mathbb{E}_\pi$ means actions from $\pi$ and next states from $P$, and add "since $\mathbb{E}[r_t \mid s_t, a_t] = r(s_t, a_t)$, this equals $\mathbb{E}_\pi[\sum_t \gamma^t r(s_t, a_t)]$". If `sec_mdp` defines the return from step $t$ with $G_t = r_t + \gamma G_{t+1}$ (see the review of that section), the expectation equation follows by taking expectations of the recursion, and the paragraph "Split a trajectory after its first transition" becomes two lines.

**VI-2** (minor, explanation). Under "The Optimality Equation". Quote: "there is a deterministic, stationary policy $\pi^*$ that is optimal at every state simultaneously". *Stationary* is used here for the first time and never defined (`sec_mdp` says "independent of time"); "policies that depend on the history" are named but the reader has only seen $\pi(a \mid s)$. Add one sentence before the proposition: a policy is stationary if its rule $\pi(a \mid s)$ is the same at every step; a history-dependent policy may choose $a_t$ from $(s_0, a_0, \ldots, s_t)$. Also under "Policies and Values" the sentence "In general a *policy* $\pi(a \mid s)$ is a conditional distribution" redefines what `sec_mdp` defined in the same notation; one of the two should be a clause.

**VI-3** (moderate, thought). Under "The Optimality Equation". Quote: "For a discounted finite MDP none of this is needed, as :numref:`sec_mdp` stated:". The existence of $\pi^*$ is the foundation: $V^*$ is defined as $V^{\pi^*}$, the optimality equation is derived from it, and the "fourth consequence" (every greedy policy of $V^*$ is optimal) relies on it. It is the only load-bearing result in the section that is cited, and the text does not say so. Two fixes, in order of preference. (a) Prove the stationary case, which needs nothing new: the contraction gives $\mathcal{T}$ a unique fixed point $\bar V$ (the iterates $\mathcal{T}^k 0$ are a Cauchy sequence in $\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}$); a policy $\pi$ greedy for $\bar V$ has $\mathcal{T}^\pi \bar V = \bar V$, so $V^\pi = \bar V$; for any stationary $\pi'$, $\mathcal{T}^{\pi'} \bar V \leq \mathcal{T} \bar V = \bar V$, and monotonicity (already used in the policy-improvement proof) gives $V^{\pi'} = \lim (\mathcal{T}^{\pi'})^k \bar V \leq \bar V$. Five lines, placed after the contraction proposition, and then only the extension to history-dependent policies is cited. This requires stating the optimality equation first as the definition of the operator and its fixed point, then identifying the fixed point with the best value: a reordering of `subsec_vi_optimality` and "Convergence via Contraction", not new material. Exercise 8c already asks for the last step by another route. (b) If the order stays, say plainly at the proposition "we do not prove this; it is the one result of this section taken on trust", in the candid register of guide 10.2, and say in the "second consequence" that the existence of the fixed point comes from the proposition.

**VI-4** (minor, explanation). Under "Convergence via Contraction". Quote: "The difference between two maxima is bounded by the largest difference of their inputs". This is the only step of the proof that is not immediate and it is asserted. One line proves it: for every $a$, $f(a) \leq g(a) + \max_{a'} |f(a') - g(a')| \leq \max_{a'} g(a') + \max_{a'} |f(a') - g(a')|$; take the maximum over $a$ and exchange the roles of $f$ and $g$. The proposition's statement should also carry its hypotheses, which the proof uses: finitely many actions (the maxima exist), $P(\cdot \mid s, a)$ a distribution, $\gamma \geq 0$; the bound is a contraction when $\gamma < 1$. It does not use finiteness of $\mathcal{S}$ beyond boundedness of the tables, which is worth a clause because the next chapter has continuous states.

**VI-5** (minor, explanation/notation). Under "The Optimality Equation". Quote: "The action-value version of :eqref:`eq_bellman_optimality` follows by inserting". The operator $\mathcal{T}$ is then defined on state-value tables only, but `sec_qlearning` opens by redefining $\mathcal{T}$ on action-value tables and uses that form sixteen times, citing this section's contraction. Add here, after :eqref:`eq_bellman_operator` or after the proof: "the same symbol denotes the corresponding map on tables $Q(s, a)$, whose fixed point is $Q^*$; the proof is the same with the maximum inside the average". Then the next section cites instead of re-deriving.

**VI-6** (minor, pedagogy). Under "Backup Diagrams". Quote: "The last diagram shows what a learner must do without the model". Panel (d) of :numref:`fig_rl_backups` uses $y$ and $\hat Q$, neither defined in this section, and describes Q-learning one section early. Panels (a) to (c) summarize this section and earn their place. Move panel (d) to `sec_qlearning`, where it is the first picture the reader needs, and let this subsection be a caption-length paragraph under "The Optimality Equation" (a 60-word `###` for one figure is a heading too many).

**VI-7** (minor, notation). Caption of :numref:`fig_rl_contraction`. Quote: "radius $\gamma^k r_0$ around $V^*$". $r_0$ is the reward at step 0 everywhere else in the two chapters, and the text one paragraph later calls the same quantity $e_0$ ("$\leq \gamma^k e_0$"). Rename to $e_0$ in the caption and in the figure's two labels (`tools/gen_mdl_*` generator for `mdl-rl-contraction.svg`). The figure itself is good: I checked $V^* = (5, 4)$, $V_1 = (2.4, 2.4)$, $V_2 = (2.92, 1.92)$.

**VI-8** (minor, language). Under "Convergence via Contraction". Quote: "Four consequences follow." An announcement (guide 10.3), though the four are then stated. More useful would be a ranking: the certificate is the one the reader will code, uniqueness and the rate are what make the method trustworthy, and the fourth is why `argmax` is safe. Say which matters for what, in one sentence, in place of the count.

**VI-9** (moderate, pedagogy; author decision 4). Under "Slippery Ice". Quote: "and asserts that the guarantee is never violated". Three cells assert a theorem that was proved a page earlier: `assert (err[k] <= err[0] * gamma ** k + 1e-12).all()` (cell `value-iter-value-iteration-3`), `assert (true_err <= certified + 1e-12).all()` (cell `value-iter-value-iteration-4`), and the cell-14 line "$\tfrac13 + 0.95\,(0.509 + 0.724)/3 = 0.724$, the same number on both sides", which checks the fixed-point equation on a converged iterate. None can fail unless the code is wrong. The plots and prints around them do teach (the measured rate $0.92$ against $0.95$; 128 against 164), so the cells stay. Fix: keep the asserts as silent guards if wanted, delete the clause quoted above, and cut the cell-14 check to a clause ("with $V^*(13) = 0.509$ the equation of cell 14 gives $0.724$, as it must"). The fourth assert, that policy iteration and value iteration agree to $10^{-12}$, compares two independent implementations and is the one worth a sentence.

**VI-10** (moderate, pedagogy; proposes an experiment change for the development machine). Under "Policy Iteration and Generalized Policy Iteration". Quote: "Policy iteration needed two exact solves. The greedy policy of the uniform policy's values is already $\pi^*$". The demonstration is degenerate: one greedy step and a confirming solve. The reader does not see a policy improve round by round, the monotone climb of the proposition is invisible, and the comparison "two solves against 164 sweeps" overstates policy iteration on a lucky start. On my NumPy reconstruction of the lake the same loop takes 3 rounds from `shortest`, 5 from always-*left*, 6 from always-*up*, and 3 to 6 from thirty random deterministic policies. Fix: start from `shortest` (the story the section is already telling: three rounds turn the calm-ice policy into $\pi^*$) or from always-*up* (the policy `sec_mdp` introduced, whose values are all zero), and print $V^{\pi}(s_0)$ and the number of changed commands per round. Then the sentence about the uniform start can stay as a remark.

**VI-11** (minor, correctness; the inconsistency is certain, the mechanism I checked only on my reconstruction, since Gymnasium is not installed here). Same subsection. Quote: "at cell 6, *left* and *right* have the same action value, and rounding can flip `argmax` between them from one round to the next". On the $4 \times 4$ lake the tie is exact in floating point: both commands average the same two values, $V(2)$ and $V(10)$, with the same weights, plus a zero from a hole; I get bitwise-equal entries. The next cell depends on that: `ok = [(mdp.backup(V_k).argmax(axis=1) == pi_star).all() for V_k in history]` followed by `assert all(ok[np.argmax(ok):])` compares actions, and would fail if `argmax` flipped at cell 6 anywhere in 987 sweeps; the stored output shows it passed. So the prose warns of a flip that the following cell assumes cannot happen. Fix: say that the tie on this map is exact and `argmax` therefore stable, and that on larger maps ties are broken by rounding (exercise 4 gives state 51 of the $8 \times 8$ map; I did not verify that one), or make the `ok` test robust by comparing the value of the greedy policy with $V^*$ instead of its actions.

**VI-12** (minor, explanation). Same subsection. Quote: "The test for the end is the proposition's equality condition, that the greedy step gains nothing at any state." The proposition's condition is $V^{\pi'} = V^\pi$; the code tests $\max_a Q^\pi(s, a) \leq V^\pi(s)$, that is $\mathcal{T}V^\pi = V^\pi$. The two are equivalent by the last line of the proof, and the second needs no extra solve. Say so: "By the last line of the proof it suffices to test $\mathcal{T}V^\pi = V^\pi$, which costs one backup and no second solve."

**VI-13** (moderate, explanation; certain). Under "The Optimal Policy on Slippery Ice". Quote: "The values say that $\pi^*$ is the best policy. How much better is it than the obvious alternative". The section then measures "better" by the probability of reaching the goal within 100 steps, which is not the objective $\pi^*$ is optimal for. $\pi^*$ maximizes the discounted return at $\gamma = 0.95$; the policy that maximizes the probability of reaching the goal is different. On my reconstruction the $\gamma = 1$ optimum reaches the goal with probability $0.824$ without a limit (against $0.780$ for $\pi^*$) and the best time-dependent policy reaches it within 100 steps with probability $0.744$ (against $0.730$). Exercise 10b asks for the second number, so the authors know; the body does not say it, and a careful reader will take $0.730$ for the best achievable. Add one sentence under "Success Rates": "$\pi^*$ is optimal for the discounted return, which prefers an early success to a late one; a policy that only maximizes the chance of success is more patient and reaches the goal slightly more often (exercises ... )." Related: the opening and the Summary say "reaches the goal about sixteen times as often as the shortest path", which is the 100-step figure ($0.730/0.045$); without the limit it is $0.78$ against $0.04$, about twenty times. Add "within the simulator's 100 steps" in one of the two places.

**VI-14** (moderate, thought; guide 7.7). Under "Reward Shaping and the Optimal Policy". Quote: "This is the condition that :numref:`sec_mdp` stated in words." Shaping is told twice across the two sections: the approach bonus and its loop (there by arithmetic, here "This is the loop of :numref:`sec_mdp` with values attached"), the potential equation (displayed in both), "Around a loop its terms cancel up to the discount" (both), the purpose of a potential ("spreads the goal's reward over the route", both, nearly verbatim), and the terminal-state condition (asserted there, derived here). Fix: this subsection owns the proof, the terminal condition and the slippery-ice computation; `sec_mdp` owns the bonus, the loop arithmetic and the definition (see MDP-11 in that review). Here, cite the equation by `:eqref:`eq_mdp_shaping`` instead of displaying it again, and cut the sentence about loops.

**VI-15** (moderate, correctness; arithmetic certain). Under "Potential-Based Shaping". Quote: "A potential that rises toward the goal, such as $\Phi = -0.3\,\ell$, spreads the goal's reward over the route, so that a learner receives a signal on every move instead of only on the last one." Two problems. First, the claim is never tested: no cell in this chapter trains a learner with a potential, so the paragraph that begins "If a potential cannot change the optimal policy, why use one?" answers with an assertion. Second, two paragraphs later the text requires $\Phi = 0$ at the terminal states. With that change the shaped reward for stepping into a hole is $+0.3\,\ell(s)$: $+1.5$ from cell 4, $+1.2$ from cell 8, $+0.9$ from cell 6, $+0.6$ from cell 10, against $1.3$ for entering the goal. The text notes that the unzeroed potential "would charge the agent for falling in"; it does not note that the corrected one pays for it. The theorem is unaffected, but the signal a learner receives on its first episodes is the opposite of what the sentence promises. Fix: state what makes a potential good (it should approximate $V^*$; shaping by $\Phi$ acts on a tabular learner like starting its values at $\Phi$, a result I recall as Wiewiora 2003 and have not checked), observe that $-0.3\,\ell$ with zeros at the holes rates a hole above every frozen cell, and either pick a better potential (the calm-ice values `V_calm` are at hand and are zero at every terminal state) or leave the claim of usefulness to `sec_qlearning` with an experiment. Exercise 11c should follow.

**VI-16** (minor, correctness). Summary. Quote: "because in every cell it chooses the command with the least risk of a hole". The body says less, and correctly: "Over all eleven cells the risk of the optimal command is the least available". Least one-step risk fixes the command in seven of the eleven cells, but not in the others: at cells 0, 2 and 14 every command has zero risk and at cell 6 two commands tie, so there the values decide (the body explains cells 2 and 14 that way). "Because" is therefore slightly too strong. Write "it never takes a larger one-step risk of a hole than it must, even when the safest command points away from the goal".

**VI-17** (minor, language). Under "Commands That Turn Away from the Goal". Quote: "It commands the move into hole 5, which happens one time in three, because both of its slips land on safe cells". "It" has lost its antecedent (the previous clause is about `argmax` and *right*), and the sentence reads as a paradox. **VI-18** (moderate, pedagogy; author decision 6). Closing paragraph. Quote: "We can now compute the value of every state and every command of a known finite MDP". The "We can now ..." recap before the Summary. The paragraph's first and last sentences are a good ending ("Everything in this section read the table $P$ ... **VI-19** (minor, explanation). Under "Policy Iteration and Generalized Policy Iteration". Quote: ":numref:`fig_rl_gpi` draws four such schemes." - **Against Sutton and Barto, chapter 4.** This section is more rigorous (they prove policy improvement and assert convergence of value iteration by reference; here the contraction, the certificate and shaping invariance are proved), more careful numerically (a stopping rule that is shown to fail, a certificate that works), and better at reading a solution (the anatomy of $\pi^*$ has no counterpart there). It is narrower: Sutton and Barto treat asynchronous and in-place dynamic programming, prioritized sweeps and the efficiency of dynamic programming in the text (here: one exercise), and they teach policy iteration on examples where it visibly iterates (the gridworld, Jack's car rental), which this section does not (VI-10). Their notation $v_\pi$, $q_\pi$, $G_t$, $p(s', r \mid s, a)$ differs; the choice of $V^\pi$, $Q^\pi$, $P$, $r(s,a)$ here is the common one in the research literature and can be kept through both chapters, provided the return from step $t$ gets a symbol (see the `sec_mdp` review).
