# Learned Models and Planning

Source: `chapter_deep-reinforcement-learning/model-based.md`. About 5,000 words of prose before the exercises (6,900 with code), 1,000 words of exercises. Stored outputs checked in both tabs (`outputs/pytorch/...` and `outputs/jax/...`); the distillation plot and `img/mdl-rl-model-planning.svg` were rendered and inspected.

## Verdict

A good section, and the best of the three that close the chapter. It has one question that it states and keeps ("how far from its data can a model be trusted?"), one task, one small model, and a chain of experiments in which each answers a question the previous one raised: one step by hand, the error along a rollout, five candidate plans checked against the real environment, the planner from 200 to 800 real steps, the coverage table that explains the two slow seeds. The reader leaves with a principle (a model is accurate where its data went; replanning and refitting are how an agent lives with that), not a tour of methods. Dyna, MBPO, AlphaZero and MuZero are each tied to something the section did. The most important thing to fix is the passage the section is named for: the "Compounding Error" table does not show compounding. On held-out random episodes the error grows about linearly and on one seed in each tab it falls between ten and 25 steps; the exponential growth appears only later, in the coverage table, where it is mixed with missing coverage, and the schematic in panel (c) of the figure draws an exponential that the first table contradicts. The section needs two sentences that say when errors compound (unstable dynamics, inputs off the data) and when they merely add, and the two tables read accordingly. After that: the last third (distillation, imagined transitions) rests on an experiment designed so that the actor falls short for reasons the text cannot separate, and on two experiments the reader never sees.

## What works

- **The opening.** "A child who has been pushed on a swing a few times can say where the swing will be a moment later and how hard to push to send it higher. Could an agent learn the physics of the pendulum from a few hundred steps and plan its way up, with no critic and no policy?" A problem the reader feels, tied to the number the previous section ended on. Keep.
- **Continuity with the tabular chapter.** "Regression takes the place of counting ... Search takes the place of value iteration" maps the new section onto `sec_qlearning` in two sentences, and the later remark that the table minimizer "would be the average of the outcomes observed there: the counted model ... reduced to its mean" makes the least-squares model a generalization of something the reader built.
- **"One Step by Hand".** The hand computation of the reward, $-(2.795^2 + 0.1 \cdot 5.690^2 + 0.001 \cdot 0.08^2) = -11.05$, and of the 16 degree move against the printout is the kind of small concrete case the bar asks for. All numbers check.
- **The five-plan check against the real environment** (cell `model-based-random-shooting-1`). Replaying the same five torque sequences in the simulator and finding the model 40 to 70 points optimistic is honest and instructive, and it sets up "choosing among candidates needs only their ranking, not their values".
- **The coverage subsection.** The same five models tested on the planner's own episodes, with seeds 3 and 4 wrong by 7 to 15 degrees after one step, is the section's best experiment: it explains a failure the reader just saw, it reuses one function, and it connects to the lake ("a pair that had never been tried looked worthless to the counted model; here the network extrapolates").
- **"The Cost of a Real Step".** "It moves the cost from experience, which is scarce on a robot, to computation, which is scarce on the robot's controller." A judgment, stated once, with the 0.05 second control period to make it concrete.
- **Candour about the matched-budget comparison**: the remark that much of MBPO's gain "can come from the extra updates per real step, which replay supplies without a model", with the REDQ citation, is the kind of judgment a survey would not make.
- **Exercises 1, 2 and 4** ask for a prediction from the section's own tables before any code is run.

## Structural findings

1. **The compounding argument is asserted, not shown, and its mechanism is half right.** See MB-4. This is the one place where the section's central technical claim and its evidence part ways.
2. **The section is one and a half sections.** "A Dynamics Model from Data" and "Planning in the Model" (about 3,300 words of prose) are a complete, well-built section. "Search as Policy Improvement" (about 1,500 words) is a second topic, policy improvement by search and distillation plus Dyna, with a weaker experiment and two experiments that happen offstage. At 5,000 words the section is over the guide's ceiling (4,500). A split is defensible, with the seam before `## Search as Policy Improvement`; the second half would then need the distillation experiment redesigned so that it succeeds (MB-10) and the MBPO comparison either shown or cut to its citation (MB-12). If the section stays whole, the savings are in the code walkthrough paragraphs (MB-2), the two-curves paragraph (MB-9) and the MBPO paragraph.
3. **Three experiments are reported but not run in the notebook**: fresh against warm-started shooting ("In separate runs with five seeds"), soft actor-critic's steps to $-200$ on this section's score at two update ratios, and the MBPO comparison. Each is a result the summary or the argument leans on. One offstage result per section can pass as an authors' remark; three make the reader take a third of the section's numbers on trust. See MB-7, MB-8, MB-12.
4. **What a reader would look for and not find.** A learned *latent* model and a value at the end of the plan (Dreamer, MuZero, TD-MPC) are how model-based control is done in 2026; the section names them in one closing sentence and sends the reader to Further Reading. That is the right call for length, but the introduction names MuZero as if the section would explain it, and the text never says that AlphaZero's model is the rules of the game while MuZero's is learned, which is the distinction this section is about (MB-1).

## Passage-level findings

**MB-1** (moderate, explanation). Opening. Quote: "AlphaZero and MuZero learned to play board games beyond human strength in this way". In a section on *learned* models the reader should be told which of the two learns its model. AlphaZero searches with the known rules; MuZero learns a model that predicts only rewards, values and policies. The distinction returns in "this loop of acting with search, labeling and distilling is the loop of AlphaZero", again without it. Fix: one clause in the opening ("AlphaZero with the rules of the game as its model, MuZero with a model it learned"), and in the distillation subsection say that AlphaZero's loop is ours with a perfect model.

**MB-2** (moderate, language, pattern). Throughout. The cells are introduced by one formula: a paragraph that begins with a function name and narrates its arguments. Eleven instances, for example "`run_episode` acts in a *vector environment*, gymnasium's container that steps several copies", "`model_data` turns the stored transitions into the inputs", "`DynamicsModel` is a multilayer perceptron with two hidden layers", "`kstep_error` starts a rollout at every step of an episode", "`mpc` builds such a controller from a model". The author's decision is that cells are introduced the way the argument calls for. Most of these paragraphs carry plumbing (vector environments, closures, "Its optimizer is created once with the model, in `make_model`") that belongs in docstrings or comments. Fix: lead each cell with the question it answers (as "How many real steps does the planner need?" already does) and move API detail into the code. The paragraph beginning "Data come from whole episodes" can lose half its length this way.

**MB-3** (minor, explanation). Under "Regression in Place of Counting". Quote: "Without standardization least squares would mostly fit the reward." True, and worth the sentence, but the more useful practical remark is missing: the reward here is a known function of the state and the torque, and many planners use the known reward and learn only the dynamics. The stored five-plan check shows what learning it costs (see MB-6). One sentence saying why the section learns the reward anyway (to match the counted model, which estimated both) would preempt the question.

**MB-4** (major, correctness). Under "Compounding Error". Quotes: "so an error at one step changes the input of the next: a small error in the angle changes the torque that gravity exerts, which changes the velocity, which changes the angle further" and "On every seed the error after ten steps is several times the error after one step". The stored table does not show compounding. PyTorch: seed 0 goes 0.44, 1.91, 2.52, 1.51 (the 25-step error is *below* the 10-step error); seed 4 goes 0.04, 0.12, 0.13, 0.33. JAX: seed 2 goes 0.61, 2.75, 4.12, 4.02. From one to five steps the error grows four to fivefold, which is linear accumulation; from five to 25 steps (five times as many) it grows by a factor between 0.8 and 6. The reason is physical: near the bottom gravity is a restoring force, so a perturbation of the angle oscillates and does not grow, and the feedback loop the prose describes ("changes the angle further") is the mechanism of the *upright* position. The prose half concedes this ("it stays below about 20 degrees: under random torques the pendulum keeps swinging near the bottom") but keeps the heading and the causal story. Real compounding appears in the coverage table on seeds 0 to 2 (0.3, 1.4, 3.3, then 42 degrees at 25 steps), which the text reads correctly ("the pendulum is unstable and a small error in the predicted angle decides on which side it falls"), but there it cannot be separated from coverage. Panel (c) of :numref:`fig_rl_model_planning` draws a clean exponential in $k$, which the first table contradicts. Certainty: high for the numbers; high for the stable/unstable reading (it is the linearized pendulum). Fix: state the principle in two sentences before the table. A one-step error $\varepsilon$ adds up to about $k\varepsilon$ where the dynamics are stable and is multiplied at every step where they are not; in addition the model's inputs drift off its training data, the same shift that made the clone's errors grow with the horizon in :numref:`sec_imitation`. Then read the first table as the stable case and the coverage table's seeds 0 to 2 as the unstable one, and redraw panel (c) with both curves or relabel it. Retitle the subsection ("Error along a Rollout") if the exponential claim goes.

**MB-5** (minor, thought). Same subsection. Quote: "The depth of a plan is a dial like the $n$ of the $n$-step returns in :numref:`sec_actorcritic`." Good comparison. But the trade-off is then argued with "each further step adds the model's error to the prediction", which is additive language one paragraph after the multiplicative story. Make the two consistent once MB-4 is settled.

**MB-6** (moderate, thought). Under "Random Shooting". Quotes: "not the 60 to 75 points the model predicts", "The likely cause is coverage" and "here its values are wrong, and its choice is right". Three things. (a) In the PyTorch store the predictions are $-170.7$ to $-174.5$, so the predicted saving against $247$ is 73 to 77 points; in JAX it is 62 to 65. "60 to 75" does not cover the default tab. Write "60 to 80" or "some 60 to 75". (b) "The likely cause is coverage" is a guess the notebook could settle and does not. A competing cause is at least as plausible: the reward $-\vartheta^2$ with $\vartheta$ wrapped has a kink exactly at the bottom, where the cost peaks, and a smooth network fitted to 200 points rounds a peak off, which makes the bottom look cheaper than it is. Either test it (print the model's predicted reward at the resting state against $-\pi^2$) or drop the diagnosis and keep the observation. (c) "its choice is right" rests on one draw of five candidates whose predicted returns differ by at most four points; the order of the other four is only roughly right (in PyTorch the predicted second best is the real third and the predicted fourth is the real second; in JAX the last two are swapped). Five candidates give a rank correlation of about 0.7 and 0.9 in the two tabs, which favours the claim but is thin support for the sentence the section builds on. The claim that ranking survives a biased model is important, so show it properly: the rank correlation between predicted and real returns over 100 candidates costs one line and a second of compute.

**MB-7** (moderate, pedagogy). Under "Warm Starts". Quote: "In separate runs with five seeds, scored on another set of ten starts, we compared it with a planner that draws 500 fresh, uniformly random sequences at every step." The comparison that justifies the warm start is not in the notebook, uses a different set of starts from every other score in the section, and reports two ranges the reader cannot reproduce. Fix: give `mpc` a `warm=True` argument and print one row per arm for two or three seeds after 600 steps (it reuses `mpc_run`), or cut the numbers and keep the argument ("a good plan is then found once and refined from step to step"), sending the comparison to an exercise.

**MB-8** (moderate, correctness). Under "Swing-Up from a Few Episodes" and "The Cost of a Real Step". Quote: "first reaches $-200$ after about 5,000 to 7,500 steps at one update per step". :numref:`sec_sac` reports the same agent crossing $-200$ "after about five to eleven thousand environment steps" (5,400 to 11,200) at one update per step and 2,400 to 4,000 at ten, measured on the five-episode average of training returns. This section's figures (5,000 to 7,500; 1,600 to 4,000) come from a different score (greedy actor, ten fixed starts) in runs no cell here performs. The text says so in a subordinate clause ("its greedy actor scored the same way after every episode"), and repeats the numbers twice, but a reader who turns back finds different numbers for what looks like the same quantity. Certainty: high that the two sections quote different ranges; I could not rerun either. Fix: quote the SAC section's own ranges and say in one clause that the scores differ slightly in definition, or make the comparison at the precision both support ("several thousand at one update per step, two to four thousand at ten"). The conclusion ("about ten times fewer ... a few times fewer") survives either way. State the comparison once; at present it appears in both subsections.

**MB-9** (minor, thought). Under "Coverage". Quote: "The planner explores without any explicit exploration rule, because it goes where the model promises reward and the model is corrected wherever it was wrong." Too strong. The correction happens only where the model is wrong on the optimistic side: a model that wrongly predicts low reward for a region never sends the planner there, and the error stays. The preceding sentence about the lake ("a pair that had never been tried looked worthless") is exactly that case. Fix: "is corrected wherever it promised too much; where it promises too little, nothing sends the planner to find out", which also prepares exercise 4(c) and the pessimism of the next section.

**MB-10** (major, pedagogy). Under "Rounds of Search and Distillation". Quotes: "The actor alone improves on every run" and "These runs do not show why it falls short, and three causes are candidates." The rendered plot shows the actor's median rising to about $-270$ by round 3 (600 steps) and then staying flat or falling to about $-340$ by round 8, with the worst seed near $-1{,}100$ throughout; "improves on every run" hides the plateau, and I could not find the "$-190$" upper end in the PyTorch plot (the best actor at round 8 is near $-320$; the best at any round is about $-230$). More important, the experiment was built with two handicaps the text then lists as possible causes (200 labels per round, earlier labels discarded), and it ends with the admission that it cannot say which matters. The writing guide's rule applies: an experiment that needs a paragraph of confounders before its result can be read should be simplified. Fix: aggregate the labels across rounds in the main experiment (two changed lines: keep a growing array of labeled states and labels). If the actor then reaches the planner's level, the section shows the AlphaZero loop working and the remaining gap, if any, has one cause left, which exercise 5(b) (DAgger) tests. If it does not, the text can say so with one candidate fewer. Either outcome is a cleaner lesson than the present three-way shrug. This needs a rerun on the development machine.

**MB-11** (minor, language). Under "Distillation into the Actor". Quote: "The two curves measure different things." The paragraph that follows spends 80 words explaining that the plot puts a single training episode and a ten-start mean on one axis. Plot the same quantity for both (score `search` on the ten starts each round; it costs one `score` call per round, already done once at the end) and the paragraph, the dip at 1,200 steps and its explanation all go.

**MB-12** (moderate, pedagogy). Under "Imagined Transitions as Data". Quotes: "Does this reduce the real steps on Pendulum? We ran soft actor-critic with its temperature fixed at 0.2" and "A model helps most where this section used it: to look ahead before acting and to turn the search into a policy." A negative result from an unseen experiment, with a setup that differs from every other run in the section (fixed temperature, "another set of ten fixed starts, from which a good controller loses less than from ours"), carries a general conclusion. Pendulum has three state dimensions and soft actor-critic already solves it in about two thousand steps at ten updates per step, so there is little room for imagined data to help; the claim "helps most where this section used it" does not follow from that null. The cited REDQ result is the sound evidence, and it is a statement about update ratios, not about models being unhelpful. Fix: keep the description of Dyna and MBPO, keep the REDQ sentence as the judgment ("much of the gain is the update ratio"), cut the sentence beginning "A model helps most", and reduce the Pendulum run to one sentence that points to exercise 6 ("on Pendulum we found no saving at a matched update ratio; exercise ... repeats the comparison"). The Summary's last sentence should be softened the same way.

**MB-13** (moderate, pedagogy). End of body. Quote: "We can now fit a model of the dynamics from a few hundred steps, plan through it, and teach a policy what the search finds." The recap paragraph before the Summary that the author asked to remove. Its last sentence is the hand-off to :numref:`sec_offline` and is worth keeping as the body's final sentence: "Every step here could test its trust in the model by acting and refitting. :numref:`sec_offline` takes away the acting."

**MB-14** (minor, thought). Under "Search Seeded by the Actor". Quote: "This is the greedy step of policy iteration in :numref:`subsec_vi_policy_iteration`". Close but not the same object: the greedy step changes one action and then follows the policy, judged by the policy's exact values; here the whole 25-step sequence is perturbed open loop and judged by a truncated model return. "This plays the role of the greedy step" is accurate. The two caveats that follow ("only inside the model and only over the horizon") are well put.
