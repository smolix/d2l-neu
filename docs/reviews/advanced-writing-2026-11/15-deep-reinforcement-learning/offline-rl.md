# Offline Reinforcement Learning

Source: `chapter_deep-reinforcement-learning/offline-rl.md`. About 4,200 words of prose before the exercises (5,300 with code), 1,700 words of exercises. Stored outputs checked (the two tabs are identical: the section is pure NumPy); the scatter plot and `img/mdl-rl-data-rules.svg` were rendered. The lake experiment was reimplemented in NumPy (same map, slip rule, discount, log sizes, 40 logs of each kind; a different random stream) to test the explanations of the pessimism results. It reproduces $V^*(s_0) = 0.180$, the behaviors' values $0.008$ and $0.087$, and the section's medians to within sampling.

## Verdict

The first half of this section meets the bar. It poses a question the reader feels (the hospital, the logged recommender), picks an instance where every prediction can be checked against an exact optimum, separates what a learner *predicts* from what its policy *earns*, and finds the cause of the gap in a plot and a pooled count: the maximum picks the thin pairs whose errors happen to be positive. The two-armed bandit is a model worked example. The section teaches a principle the reader keeps (distrust an estimate in proportion to how little data it rests on, and know that the penalty helps when the log is narrow) and names CQL and TD3+BC only as the two things that principle becomes without counts. The second half has a problem the author needs to know about. The two results that carry the "cost of pessimism" are not explained by the reasons the text gives. At $\kappa = 0.3$ on the narrow logs the collapse is produced by the clip at zero in `offline_q` (cells whose rows are clipped to all zeros tie four ways and the greedy policy acts at random there): without the clip the same penalty gives the best policy of any arm in my rerun (median about $0.16$ against $0.09$ for the naive table). On the uniform logs the damage comes from the per-step charge acting as a cost of living, not from the charge "differing from pair to pair": a charge that is the same for every pair hurts more. The conclusions drawn (the penalty must match the data; pessimism is unnecessary under full coverage) are standard and broadly true, but this experiment does not establish them in the way the prose says. The second structural gap: a section in the *deep* reinforcement learning chapter contains no network, and the lake, with nonnegative rewards and a zero floor, cannot show the failure that deep offline methods exist to prevent.

## What works

- **The opening paragraph** and its turn: "Now suppose there are no more episodes." Three concrete settings, then the question. The second paragraph states the whole argument of the section in five sentences, ending on "It is the mirror image of the optimism that drove exploration".
- **Predicts against earns.** The `summary` line ("predicts 0.25, earns 0.11; above V* on 39") is the right diagnostic, and the exact evaluation by $(I - \gamma P_\pi)^{-1} r_\pi$ over 40 logs removes rollout noise from every comparison. Few textbook treatments of offline learning are this clean.
- **Coverage against competence**: "Coverage and competence pull in opposite directions, and the two logs sit at the two ends." The two logs are well chosen.
- **The stitching paragraph**: "Suppose one random episode reaches cell 10 and then falls into a hole, while another reaches cell 10 by a different route and slides on to the goal." One concrete case that defines the term, tied to a measured result (the naive table earns about $0.14$ from data whose collector earned $0.008$).
- **The bandit by hand.** Every number checks ($b(1) = 0.136$, $b(2) = 1.358$, lower bounds $0.364$ and $-0.358$, expected reward $0.4375$, and $n_2 = 17$ as the first count at which a $0.7$ arm passes). The paragraph "Pessimism has a cost" gives the price in the same example.
- **UCB and LCB as one term with two signs**: "they differ in sign because they differ in feedback."
- **"Calibration and Model Selection"** says what most treatments leave out: every comparison used a model no deployment has.
- **The one-step counterexample to return conditioning** (A pays 1 half the time, B always pays 0.6). Eight lines that a reader will remember longer than the paper citations around them.
- **Exercises**: the CQL-in-a-table exercise (I checked its closed form and part (b)) and the importance-sampling exercise are among the best in the two chapters.

## Structural findings

1. **The clip at zero drives a headline result.** See OFF-7 and OFF-8. This is the most important finding.
2. **No deep experiment in a deep chapter, and the lake hides the deep failure.** On the lake an untried pair has value zero and zero is the floor, so absent support is harmless by construction ("The difficulty there was thin support, not absent support", as the text says in "Beyond the Table"). With a network, values at untried actions are produced by generalization and can be arbitrarily high; that, not the winner's curse among thin pairs, is what BCQ, CQL and IQL were built against. The reader meets it only as the clause "with deep networks the gap can be far larger" and in the last, extended exercise. Either promote a compact form of exercise 9(a) into "Beyond the Table" (the DQN of :numref:`sec_dqn`, a frozen CartPole buffer, the predicted start value plotted against the ceiling of about 99 that no policy can exceed: one cell, one plot, CPU minutes), or say plainly in the text what the lake cannot show and why. The first is better: it is the experiment that makes CQL's term necessary instead of merely named.
3. **Two families, and the third is missing.** :numref:`tab_rl_offline_families` lists policy constraint and value penalty. In-sample learning (IQL: never evaluate the critic at an action outside the log) is the third standard answer and the one most used as a starting point for fine-tuning; it is in Further Reading but not in the table. The sentence that follows the table ("A hard rule that allows only logged actions inside the maximum would change nothing on the lake") is exactly the tabular shadow of that idea and could carry the one-line mention. Likewise offline-to-online fine-tuning, which the closing paragraph calls "the common practical answer", appears only in the recap (OFF-12).
4. **Length and seams.** At 4,200 words the section is inside the ceiling. It is not two sections. What can go: the loop-against-solver check (OFF-4), half of "Choosing the Penalty" (OFF-8), and the function-by-function narration (OFF-3).
5. **Return conditioning belongs here and is the right size.** The two paragraphs plus exercise 4 treat Decision Transformer by its principle and its two limits. The first paragraph is closer to a literature summary than the rest of the section (OFF-11).

## Passage-level findings

**OFF-1** (minor, correctness). Under "Which Targets Can Use Another Policy's Data". Quote: "REINFORCE and actor-critic are therefore *on-policy*". Soft actor-critic, two sections back, is an actor-critic that learns from a replay buffer; its actor's gradient is pathwise through the critic and needs no ratio. As written the sentence contradicts :numref:`sec_sac`. Fix: "REINFORCE and the actor-critic of :numref:`sec_actorcritic`", and add soft actor-critic to the off-policy side in a clause (the figure's panel (b) lists only "Q-learning, DQN" and could add it too).

**OFF-2** (minor, explanation). Same subsection. Quote: "Q-learning learns $Q^*$ from any log". The next paragraph takes it back ("Off-policy does not mean that any data will do"). State it right the first time: "from any log that covers the pairs it needs". Also the hat comes and goes: the target is written with $\hat{Q}$ here, :eqref:`eq_sarsa` and :eqref:`eq_offline_pessimistic` use $Q$, and the error is $\hat{Q} - Q^*$. Use $\hat Q$ for every learned table.

**OFF-3** (moderate, language, pattern). Under "Two Logs of the Slippery Lake" and "Distribution Shift against the Exact Optimum". The same function-name formula as in the previous section, nine times: "`greedy` turns a table of action values into its greedy policy", "`make_log` collects a log with `d2l.rollout`", "`count` tallies $n(s, a)$", "`offline_q_sweeps` shuffles the logged transitions", "`summary` prints the medians over the 40 logs ... `tally` counts on how many logs". Some of these paragraphs are mostly API ("returns the transitions as a `d2l.Batch` with fields `obs`, `act`, `rew`, `next_obs` and `term`"; "a rule that sent every tie to the first action would walk left from every row of zeros"). Fix: introduce each cell by its question, as "Where do the inflated predictions come from?" does, and move the plumbing into docstrings.

**OFF-4** (moderate, pedagogy). Under "Distribution Shift against the Exact Optimum". Quote: "Do the loop and the direct solution agree? We run both on the first uniform log". A cell whose only job is to confirm that Q-learning swept over a fixed log converges to value iteration on the empirical model. That is certainty equivalence, which :numref:`sec_qlearning` already established, and the check costs 200 sweeps and a paragraph. The idea it protects is valuable (the fixed point "depends on the log alone"), the check is not. Fix: state the fact with the reference, keep `offline_q` only, and drop `offline_q_sweeps` and cell `offline-rl-distribution-shift-against-the-exact-optimum-2` (or move them to an exercise next to the dataset-size one).

**OFF-5** (moderate, explanation). Same subsection. Quote: "With a positive `kappa` the function also charges an untried pair an infinite penalty, but the clip returns it to the same floor of zero, so pessimism leaves untried pairs where the naive table already put them". The paragraph introduces `kappa`, the clip and the infinite penalty 600 words before pessimism is motivated, and the reader cannot yet know why any of it matters. It also presents the clip as harmless, which it is not (OFF-8). Fix: define `offline_q` here without `kappa` language beyond "an argument we use below", and move the explanation of the penalty and the clip to "The Penalty as One Line", where :eqref:`eq_offline_pessimistic` shows them.

**OFF-6** (minor, thought). Same subsection. Quote: "two of them are overestimated by $0.26$ and $0.20$, more than the optimum itself". The comparison is between errors in $Q$ at cells 14 and 10, next to the goal, where $Q^*$ is several times $V^*(s_0) = 0.18$, and the value of the start cell. It sounds dramatic and compares unlike things. Give the errors relative to the true values of those pairs, or drop the clause. The pooled statistic that follows (a quarter of the picks rest on five or fewer samples; their median error is $+0.22$ against $+0.08$ for all thin pairs) is the real evidence and matches the output.

**OFF-7** (major, correctness). Under "Narrow Data and Uniform Data". Quote: "With every action tried tens of times, there are few lucky estimates to protect against, and a penalty that differs from pair to pair only changes which well-supported action looks best." Two problems. (a) The first clause sits oddly beside the section's own table: on the uniform logs the naive prediction exceeds $V^*$ on 28 of 40 logs, so there is overestimation to remove, and the penalty removes it (no prediction above the optimum) while lowering the return. (b) The stated mechanism is not what happens. In my rerun, a penalty that is the *same* for every tried pair ($\kappa$ over the root of the median count) lowers the return on the uniform logs more than $\kappa/\sqrt{n}$ does (median $0.06$ against $0.10$ at $\kappa = 0.1$; naive $0.13$). A uniform per-step charge is a cost of living. On this lake the optimal policy takes long detours to stay away from holes and $V^*(s_0)$ is only $0.18$, so even a charge of $0.015$ a step, accumulated over tens of steps, favours short episodes, including ones that end in a hole. Certainty: high for the rerun's numbers, moderately high for the reading. Fix: say that the penalty is charged per step and so shortens the horizon the learner is willing to plan over; on the narrow logs the charge is small along the operator's well-trodden path and large off it, which is the selective effect one wants, and on the uniform logs it is about the same everywhere and only taxes long paths. That explanation also makes the next subsection's accumulation argument arrive naturally.

**OFF-8** (major, correctness). Under "Choosing the Penalty". Quote: "At $\kappa = 0.3$ the start value is driven to zero on nearly every log, and the policy loses to the naive table on 36 of the 40 narrow logs and on all 40 uniform ones." On the narrow logs this result is an artifact of the clip at zero. With the clip, about 45% of live cells end with a row of zeros at $\kappa = 0.3$ in my rerun, `greedy` splits those ties four ways, and the policy is random there. Without the clip (values allowed to go negative, untried pairs excluded by a large penalty, terminal cells fixed at zero) the same $\kappa = 0.3$ gives a median value of about $0.16$ on the narrow logs, better than the naive table on 32 of 40 logs and better than $\kappa = 0.1$ (about $0.12$). So on narrow data a larger penalty helps more, up to a point ($\kappa = 1$ fails either way); what the section shows instead is that a pessimistic table truncated at the environment's floor loses its ordering. The text's own explanation (charges accumulate to $\kappa/(\sqrt{n}(1-\gamma))$, which exceeds $V^*$) explains why the *values* go negative, not why the *policy* gets worse: subtracting a similar charge from every path does not change their order. The paragraph then needs 120 words about a bound that "asks for" a thousand samples while a few hundred suffice, and sends the resolution to an exercise. Certainty: high that removing the clip reverses the narrow-log result in a faithful reimplementation; the exact counts will differ in the book's random stream. Fix (needs a rerun on the development machine): remove the clip from `offline_q`, or keep it only for reporting the prediction at the start cell and take the greedy policy from the unclipped table. Then rerun `offline-rl-kappa-over-root-n` and `offline-rl-choosing-the-penalty`, and rewrite this subsection around what the unclipped sweep shows: a broad range of $\kappa$ that helps on narrow logs, a range that hurts on uniform logs, and failure at both only when the charge swamps the rewards. The model-selection point ("nothing in the log fixes $\kappa$") stands and becomes sharper, since the best $\kappa$ differs between the two kinds of log. The sentence in the earlier subsection, "The clip at zero ... keeps the penalty from driving values below it", should go with the clip. Exercise 3 parts (b) and (c) depend on the clip's behavior and need revisiting.

**OFF-9** (minor, language). Under "Choosing the Penalty". Quote: "Most pairs along the optimal path then hold a few hundred samples, short of the thousand the bound asks for, and still, as exercise :exref:`ex_offline-rl-how-much-pessimism` shows, $\kappa = 0.3$ does little harm there: the charge actually collected is already small enough." A sentence that argues with itself and reports an unseen result. It goes away with OFF-8.

**OFF-10** (moderate, explanation). Under "Constraining the Policy or the Values". Quotes: "Up to a constant, the squared distance is the negative log-likelihood of the logged action under a Gaussian centered at the policy's action, so the term is a sample estimate of the divergence in the other direction, $D_{\textrm{KL}}(\pi_e \Vert \pi)$" and "Direct preference optimization, which closes the next section, learns from a fixed dataset of preferences between pairs of responses under the same constraint". The first is correct (up to a scale as well as a constant, and up to the entropy of $\pi_e$) but it is a second idea compressed into a sentence about a first: the displayed objective penalizes $D_{\textrm{KL}}(\pi \Vert \pi_e)$, the method named as its instance penalizes the reverse, and the reader is not told whether the difference matters. :numref:`subsec_kl_direction` owns that question; cite it and say in one clause what the forward direction does here (it is behavior cloning, so the actor is pulled toward *every* logged action, not kept away from unlogged ones). The DPO sentence is a forward reference to four sentences at the end of the next section, is repeated in this section's closing paragraph ("there, DPO learns a language model from a fixed dataset of preferences under the KL constraint above"), and is answered there by "it inherits the questions of :numref:`sec_offline`". Three pointers, no content at either end. Keep one, in the next section, and make it say something (see `rl-sequences.md`, SEQ-11).

**OFF-11** (minor, pedagogy). Under "Return Conditioning". Quote: "A small network that conditions on the return by ordinary supervised learning recovers much of its performance, so much of the gain comes from conditioning on the return rather than from the sequence architecture". A paper's finding retold with nothing in the section to stand on; the reader has seen no Decision Transformer and no benchmark. The claims about the three cited papers match my knowledge of them (RvS; the two analyses of stochastic environments). Fix: lead with the mechanism in one sentence (behavior cloning with the return as an extra input, as exercise 4 defines it), give the one-step example immediately, and keep the benchmark remarks to one sentence after it. The order "example, then what the literature found" suits this passage better than the reverse.

**OFF-12** (moderate, pedagogy). End of body. Quote: "We can now learn a policy from a fixed log, compare what it predicts with what it earns, and tell a supported estimate from a lucky one." The recap paragraph before the Summary. It also introduces new material in passing: "The common practical answer is to stop being purely offline: learn from the log, then collect a little new data and fine-tune." That sentence is the most useful practical statement in the section and it appears only inside a recap. Move it to the end of "Calibration and Model Selection" (where the reader has just learned that $\kappa$ cannot be chosen from the log), with the pointer to Further Reading, and delete the rest of the paragraph.

**OFF-13** (minor, explanation). Under "Calibration and Model Selection". Quote: "Its weight is the product of these ratios over an episode, the product whose variance grows with the horizon in :numref:`sec_ppo`". The paragraph names two estimators without naming them (importance sampling; fitted or model-based evaluation on held-out data) and is dense for a first meeting. Since exercise 7 works the first one, the text can name it, give the weight in symbols once, and say in a sentence why a greedy candidate gets weight zero on almost every episode.

## Exercises

Nine, about 40% of the body by length, and nearly all earn their place. Exercise 6 (the bandit: $2 b(a^*)$ bound for the lower-confidence rule, the optimistic rule's dependence on the *chosen* arm's count) is the theory of the section in three parts and should be flagged in the text where :cite:`Rashidinejad.Zhu.Ma.ea.2021` is cited. Exercise 8 (CQL in a table) is correct and illuminating: the penalty $\alpha_{\textrm{c}}(\mu/\hat\pi_e - 1)$ responds to the *share* of an action in the log, $\kappa/\sqrt{n}$ to its count. Exercise 4(c) (clone only the episodes that reached the goal) is a neat tabular stand-in for return conditioning. Exercise 3 must be reworked with OFF-8. Exercise 9 should give up its part (a) to the main text (structural finding 2) and keep (b) and (c). Exercise 1 (Expected SARSA) is fine but sits oddly as the first exercise of an offline section; it tests the first subsection only.

## For the chapter review

- Position is right: it takes the previous section's question (trust beyond the data) and removes the remedy (acting). The mirror with :numref:`sec_exploration` is the section's organizing idea and it works.
- It assumes `sec_qlearning` (certainty equivalence, SARSA, $\pi_e$ for the behavior policy: notation consistent), `sec_valueiter` (exact policy evaluation), `sec_imitation` (the clone), `sec_exploration` (UCB, Hoeffding), `subsec_dqn-max-bias`, `sec_regularized` (the KL objective), `sec_ppo` (ratios). All restated enough to follow.
- Notation: $n(s, a)$ is a count here and $n$ was the plan depth one section earlier; $K$ is the number of arms here and the group size in `rl-sequences.md`; $q$ is the failure probability because $\delta$ is the temporal-difference error; $\kappa$ matches the UCB scale of `sec_exploration`; $\beta$ matches `sec_regularized`; $\alpha_{\textrm{c}}$ is new for CQL. Hats on $Q$ are inconsistent (OFF-2).
- Overlap with neighbours: none harmful. Further Reading's table of deep offline methods and this section's two-row table should agree on the families (add the in-sample row or say why not).
- Pattern for the chapter: design choices in helper functions (here a clip and a tie rule) that silently shape a headline result. Worth a targeted look in the other sections wherever a result is explained in prose but the explanation was not tested by an ablation.
