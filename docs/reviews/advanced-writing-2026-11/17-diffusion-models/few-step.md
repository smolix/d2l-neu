# Few-Step Generation

Source: `chapter_diffusion-models/few-step.md` (to the slides marker). About 3,200 words of prose, 1,350 words of exercises (11 problems), 31 code cells (PyTorch and JAX counted separately). Stored outputs read for both frameworks; the flow-map figure, the mixture panels and the image rows rendered and inspected; the Gaussian flow-map example, the one-step mean, the identities, and Exercises 1 and 10 recomputed by hand.

## Verdict

This is the section of the three that best teaches a principle instead of a literature. It names one object (the flow map), gives a three-line worked example in which the average velocity can be computed by hand, derives one loss from one identity, and places four published methods in a two-by-two table as the choices that loss leaves. A reader who meets a new few-step paper in 2028 will be able to ask which identity it enforces and where its velocity comes from, which is what a textbook should leave behind. The derivations are correct. The weaknesses are in the claims around the mathematics: the reason given for the stop-gradient is not the reason, the promise that inversion and editing "work as in" the samplers section does not hold for the network that is trained, the MeanFlow student's samples are a fifth too tight and nobody says why, and the paragraph that explains the one-step gap on images ("a matter of budget") quotes unattributed numbers and is contradicted by its own last sentence. The single most important fix is to make those four passages as exact as the derivation they surround. The second is the experiment on MeanFlow's adaptive weight, which is striking in two dimensions and, as written, leaves the reader believing that the published recipe drops modes.

## What works

- "A velocity model says which way a trajectory points; a flow map says where it arrives." The section's idea in one sentence a reader will remember. The Summary's "A flow map learns where a trajectory arrives instead of where it points" repeats it; keep the first, vary the second.
- The one-dimensional Gaussian example under "The Integral of the Velocity": $\Phi_{1 \to 0}(x) = 2 + 0.5\,x$, average velocity $0.5\,x - 2$, start velocity $x - 2$, "and its Euler step returns 2 for every $x$. The average velocity removes only half of the noise, because half of the noise's spread must survive as the spread of the data." This is the MacKay move: the smallest case in which the two objects differ, computed exactly, with the difference explained in words. All numbers check.
- The three identities are each given a physical reading ("two jumps make one"; "the mapped point moves with the field, like a particle"; "every point of one trajectory maps to the same place") and a statement of what each costs to enforce. The reader learns why there are several families of methods, not just that there are.
- The two-by-two table `tab_diffusion-flow-map-methods`, and one function `flow_map_error` that implements all four cells in fourteen lines.
- The paragraph on time features ("Diffusion code often embeds $1000\,t$, which puts frequencies up to a thousand into $d\bar{\mathbf{u}}/dt$ and turns the target into noise") is the kind of practical detail that decides whether a reader's implementation trains, tied to the derivative that has just been introduced.
- Re-noising is explained from the limitation that forces it ("because the model knows only where trajectories end"), which makes the case for two times without argument.
- The image experiment is fair and modest: same noise for teacher and student, half the teacher's budget, FD against evaluations on one plot, and the rows of samples "match column by column".
- Exercise 1 (one and two Euler steps on two points, giving $a\tanh(ax)$) and Exercise 10 (score-difference distillation as a reverse KL, with the mode-seeking computed by quadrature) are excellent, and Exercise 10 gives the distribution-matching objective exactly.

## Structural findings

1. **Order and arc are right.** Failure, a partial remedy (straightening), the object, the identity, the loss, two experiments, the alternative. Do not reorder.
2. **The one-step mean is taught for the fifth time.** `gaussian-paths.md` derives it in prose, shows it in a cell ("One step lands every start on the data mean $(-0.5, -0.7)$"), and sets it as an exercise; the appendix has it; here it appears in the opening, in "The Average Where the Data Are Not", in the Summary and in Exercise 1a. The cell `few-step-the-average-where-the-data-are-not` repeats the earlier cell with the exact field instead of the learned one and adds two and four steps. Keep the cell (the 2- and 4-step rows are the baseline the students are measured against) but cut the retelling to one clause with a reference, and let the paragraph after the cell start at two steps.
3. **Reflow is taught by reference.** "Straighter Paths Help but Do Not Finish" is three paragraphs with no equation and no cell; its one quantitative statement is quoted from the appendix. This is consistent with the author's rule (long derivation in the appendix) and the main text can be followed without it, but the subsection then asserts "Straightening does not finish the job" against the appendix's own result that one reflowed step matches thirty-two. The reasons given (inherits errors, costs a run, the paper distilled afterwards) are sound; the reader needs one sentence on why two moons are not images. Exercise 2 would settle it on the mixture in a few lines and is a candidate for promotion if space allows.
4. **The closing section is a survey stub.** "The Other Route: Matching Distributions" is two paragraphs of citations pointing to the GAN chapter, plus a paragraph on guidance distillation. It is honest and short, and the cross-reference to :numref:`sec_gan_beyond` is the right division of labour (that section owns the argument for why a one-step regression student averages). But progressive, adversarial and distribution-matching distillation are what most deployed few-step image models used through 2025, and the section gives them about 120 words. The objective of distribution matching is stated only in Exercise 10. One displayed line in the text (the gradient $\mathbb{E}[(\nabla \log q_{\boldsymbol\theta,t} - \nabla \log p_t)^\top \partial\mathbf{x}_t/\partial\boldsymbol\theta]$, "the student's score minus the teacher's") would let this paragraph teach instead of point. The guidance-distillation paragraph belongs in further reading or in Exercise 8, where it already is.
5. **Will it date?** MeanFlow is a 2025 paper, and the further-reading file already cites its 2026 successor. Because the section derives it as "the Eulerian identity written for the average velocity" and not as a system, the derivation will survive the method. The parts likely to date are the loss-weighting experiment (tied to one paper's ImageNet recipe) and the "students in the papers" paragraph.

## Passage-level findings

**FEW-1** (moderate, correctness). Under the opening. Anchor: "A flow map keeps the bijection between noise and samples, so inversion and" The exact flow map is a bijection. The network trained in this section computes $\bar{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{x}, s, t)$ for $s \leq t$ only (both losses draw $s$ below $t$), so it jumps toward the data and cannot jump back; inversion would need the other direction trained, or a root-finding loop. What the student does keep is that the same noise gives the same image, which the image rows show. Fix: "A flow map is deterministic, so the same noise gives the same sample and a student can be compared with its teacher image by image; inverting it needs the map in the other direction, which the losses below do not train (exercise)." The same overstatement recurs under "Consistency Models" ("and the bijection between noise and samples survives") and under "The Other Route" ("Both give up the bijection"); the second is fine. Fairly sure; I have not seen the section's code invert anything.

**FEW-2** (minor, thought). Under the opening. Anchor: "evaluations suffice without a critic. The answer is to learn the solution" Good. But the opening spends its first paragraph recalling four earlier sections before the question arrives. Lead with the failure (it is vivid) and put the bookkeeping of earlier sections in a clause.

**FEW-3** (minor, explanation). Under "Why One Step Fails". Anchor: "A long Euler step makes two errors. It aims where the trajectory points at" The two errors are one error stated twice: aiming along the initial tangent is ignoring the bend. If two distinct errors are meant (the direction at the start is an average over all data; the path then curves), say which is which and which the experiment shows.

**FEW-4** (minor, language). Under "The Average Where the Data Are Not". Anchor: "0.64/0.22/0.14 instead of 0.5/0.3/0.2: the heaviest mode gains because the" A good reading of an output, with a cause. Keep. (Stored: 0.644/0.217/0.14 and 0.644/0.221/0.135.)

**FEW-5** (moderate, thought). Under "Straighter Paths Help but Do Not Finish". Anchor: "distribution and never increases the transport cost, and on two-moons data" The appendix proposition holds for the exact marginal field integrated exactly; a trained model's reflow keeps the model's distribution, not the data's, which is the "inherits the original's errors" of the next paragraph. As written, "keeps the data distribution" and "inherits the original's errors" sit two sentences apart without being reconciled. Fix: "proves that reflow with the exact field keeps the data distribution and never increases the transport cost". The appendix reference resolves to the right passage (`subsec_mdl-reflow`, the proposition and the two-moons cell).

**FEW-6** (minor, correctness, unverified). Same subsection. Anchor: "still followed reflow with distillation for" True of the original paper as I recall it (the one-step CIFAR-10 numbers are for a distilled 2-rectified flow). Later work (Lee, Lin and Fanti, 2024, from memory) reports competitive one- and two-step results from reflow alone with better time sampling and losses. "Do not finish" may read as dated; soften to "did not finish in the original work".

**FEW-7** (minor, explanation). Under "The Integral of the Velocity". Anchor: "Three identities characterize the flow map, and each gives a way to train" The announcement pattern (guide 10.3) and the only place in the section where the reader is asked to hold three things at once. The figure helps for composition; its Lagrangian and Eulerian sketches are small and I could not tell them apart without the text (in one the fan of arrows shares its tail, in the other its head). Label the moving end in each sketch ("end moves", "start moves"), or enlarge the right-hand panels.

**FEW-8** (minor, explanation). Same subsection. Anchor: "Composition needs only the" The line break after "only the" in the source suggests an edit in progress; more to the point, the three "needs" sentences are the place to say which is cheapest and which is used in practice. A judgment is available: composition needs no derivative but two extra network evaluations and gives a discretized target; the Eulerian form needs one Jacobian-vector product and gives an exact one.

**FEW-9** (minor, correctness). Under "Consistency Models". Anchor: "difference, divided by the step, becomes the derivative above, which" Correct. The published form also holds the target at a moving average of the student's weights, which Exercise 6b knows and the text does not mention; one clause.

**FEW-10** (moderate, correctness). Under "Average Velocity and the MeanFlow Identity". Anchor: "constant when the loss is differentiated. It is required. Without it the" The reason given, "the target moves with every update of the network", is true with the stop-gradient as well: the target is computed from the network either way. What the stop-gradient changes is that no gradient flows through the target. That matters for a precise reason the preceding paragraph has just set up. The argument that the conditional velocity may stand in for the marginal one needs the target to be affine in the velocity and the network to appear only in the prediction; then squared error is minimized by the average target. Differentiate through the target and the network also controls the target's variance over examples, through the term $(\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\bar{\mathbf{u}})\,(\boldsymbol\epsilon - \mathbf{x}_0)$, and it can lower the loss by shaping its Jacobian instead of satisfying the identity. (It also needs a second-order derivative.) Suggested rewrite: "It is required. The averaging argument above holds only if the network enters through the prediction alone. If the target is differentiated too, the network can lower the loss by changing the Jacobian inside the target, which changes how much the target varies across examples, and training on the mixture fails: almost every sample strays (exercise)." Fairly sure of the mechanism; Exercise 6a asks exactly this ("What can the network change to lower this loss without satisfying"), so the answer should not be contradicted by the text.

**FEW-11** (minor, thought). Same subsection. Anchor: "the end time $s$ is free or fixed at zero, and the methods in the" "the methods in the literature are the four combinations" claims more than the table supports. Consistency distillation as published is a finite difference with a moving-average target; consistency trajectory models add a denoising and an adversarial term; consistency training in its improved form changes the metric and the schedule. Write "the main methods fall into the four combinations, each with additions that further reading lists".

**FEW-12** (minor, explanation). Same subsection. Anchor: "with the output at about the cost of a backward pass." Give the reader the total: one training step of the two-time loss costs a forward pass, a Jacobian-vector product and a backward pass, roughly one and a half to two velocity-model steps, plus a teacher evaluation when there is a teacher. No cell times it; Exercise 7c asks for it. A number here would let the reader weigh the "half the teacher's 10,000" budget claim later.

**FEW-13** (moderate, explanation). Under "On the Running Example". Anchor: "Both students reproduce the mixture in one evaluation, with weights within" The stored outputs say otherwise for one of them: MeanFlow's one-jump spread is 0.33 against 0.48 for the data (the mean squared distance to the mode centre is 30% low), and it stays at 0.37 to 0.39 after two and four jumps. The paragraph does report this three sentences later ("MeanFlow's samples stay too tight, though"), so the opening sentence should not say "reproduce". More important, no cause is offered. The reader has met this number before: in `samplers.md` a ten-step Euler sampler gives spread 0.38 because "a stride holds the noise estimate at its value at the start, which for Gaussian data always removes too much noise". A student whose $\bar{\mathbf{u}}$ has not fully learned the correction term is partly an Euler step and should contract in the same way; alternatively the cosine schedule ends training before the bootstrap target has converged. I do not know which holds. The section should find out (train twice as long and print the spread) or say that it does not know. As it stands the central experiment ends on an unexplained defect of the method the section is named for.

**FEW-14** (moderate, pedagogy). Same subsection. Anchor: "MeanFlow's ImageNet recipe divides each example's squared error $e$ by" The description of the recipe is right as far as I can verify ($w = 1/(e + c)^p$ under a stop-gradient, $p = 1$, small $c$), and the result is dramatic: "more than nine samples in ten on the heaviest mode" (0.944 and 0.945 stored). But the reader is left to conclude that the published method collapses modes. The effect is a property of two dimensions. There $e$ is a sum of two squares and varies by orders of magnitude between examples at the same point, so $1/(e + c)$ selects the nearest target. On an image $e$ sums thousands of coordinates and concentrates, so the weight is nearly the same for all examples at a given $(s, t)$ and acts mostly as a weighting across times. Add that sentence, or the experiment is read for more than it shows. Consider moving the cell to an exercise: it tests a detail of one paper's recipe that the section does not use, and the section's own conclusion from it is only "which is why the other losses take the plain mean".

**FEW-15** (minor, explanation). Under "On Images". Anchor: "Half of the pairs have" Why half? The mixture students used none. The answer (the student starts as the teacher and must not forget the velocity while learning the jump) is asked in Exercise 4c. Give it one clause here.

**FEW-16** (moderate, correctness). Under "On Images". Anchor: "The remaining gap at one evaluation is a matter of budget. The students in" Three problems. (a) "The students in the papers" names no paper; the figures ("roughly 100,000 to 800,000 iterations at batches of 512", "one-step FIDs about 1.4 to 1.7 times those of their teachers") cannot be checked. From memory, consistency distillation on CIFAR-10 used 800,000 iterations at batch 512 and reported a one-step FID about 1.7 times its teacher's, but on ImageNet $64 \times 64$ about 2.5 times, and the continuous-time models at ImageNet $512 \times 512$ about 1.2 times; the range depends on which papers are meant. Unverified. (b) "two to three orders of magnitude more than ours" holds per example seen (640,000 against 51 to 410 million) but not per iteration (5,000 against 100,000 to 800,000 is a factor of 20 to 160). (c) The paragraph's last sentence, "Training our student longer lowers its one-step FD only slowly", undercuts its first, and no cell shows it. Fix: cite the papers, state the comparison per example, and either show a longer run or delete the claim. A candid version: "We do not know how much of the remaining gap is budget. Published students train on a few hundred times more examples and report one-step FIDs within a factor of two of their teachers (cite); ours improves slowly beyond 5,000 updates."

**FEW-17** (minor, correctness). Under "On Images". Anchor: "fifteen Euler steps of the teacher, and one about seven." Read off the log-log plot; consistent with the stored table (student 2: 4.74 between teacher 10 at 6.65 and teacher 20 at 3.58; student 1: 11.7 between teacher 4 at 27 and teacher 10 at 6.7). Fine as a plot reading; "about" is doing the right work.

**FEW-18** (minor, thought). Under "The Other Route: Matching Distributions". Anchor: "its student can be no better than those trajectories. The distillation" For MeanFlow there is no teacher, and the trajectories are those of the exact marginal field, so this is no limit at all for the bottom row of the table. And distribution matching against a teacher's score matches the teacher's distribution, not "the distribution of the data"; only the variant with real images in the loss can pass the teacher, as the last sentence says. Rewrite the first two sentences to separate: flow-map distillation is bounded by its teacher's ODE; score-difference distillation by its teacher's score; adversarial training against real images by neither.

**FEW-19** (minor, focus). Summary. Anchor: "mode weights; on images a short student comes within about 1.5 times the" Supported (1.37 and 1.47). The Summary should also carry the limit the body found: one jump is 4 to 5 times the teacher's FD at our budget.
