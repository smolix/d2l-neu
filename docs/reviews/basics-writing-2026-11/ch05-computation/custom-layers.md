# Custom Layers and Functions

Source: `chapter_builders-guide/custom-layers.md`. The PyTorch reader sees about 2,260 words of body prose (1,810 shared, 430 inside 12 PyTorch tab blocks) and about 660 words of exercises. The source holds about 3,670 words of body prose across the four tabs.

## Verdict

The most even of the four sections and the one with the best opening. It motivates the topic with a fact a reader will remember (RMSNorm was published in 2019 and reached PyTorch's library in 2024), teaches the layer with a two-number worked example, and reaches a new idea at the end: a backward pass that is deliberately not the derivative of the forward pass. The prose is plain and the framework tabs are short. It falls short of the bar in two ways. The middle re-verifies, cell by cell, properties of registration that the two previous sections established, so the reader watches `True` printed four times. And neither of the two more interesting constructions is carried to its payoff: the random-feature layer never fits anything, and the straight-through estimator is checked for its gradient but never used to learn. The most important fix is to give the straight-through estimator a ten-line experiment in which a rounded weight learns with it and does not learn without it.

## What works

- **The opening.** "Research moves faster than libraries, however, and a layer that a paper proposes can be missing from them for years." Then the dates, then the three requirements a custom layer must meet. The reader knows the problem, why it matters, and what success looks like before any code.
- **The RMSNorm worked example.** "For $\mathbf{x} = (3, 4)$ the mean square is $(9 + 16)/2 = 12.5$, the root mean square is $3.536$, and the output is $(0.849, 1.131)$, whose mean square is 1. The input $(300, 400)$ gives the same output." A definition, a number, and the property (scale invariance) shown by a second number. The stored output prints both rows as (0.8485, 1.1314). This is the Cover and Thomas pattern done well.
- **Figure `fig_bg_rmsnorm`.** Two inputs on one ray landing on the same point of the circle is the picture of scale invariance; the right panel shows what the gain does. The sentence that reads it ("keeps the direction of $\mathbf{x}$, discards its length") is exact.
- **The link back to `CenteredLayer`**: layer normalization "first subtracts the mean of the entries of each example's vector, as a per-example `CenteredLayer` would". The toy example of the first subsection turns out to be half of a real layer. More connections of this kind would lift the section.
- **The straight-through estimator.** The zero gradient is shown first (`tensor([0., 0., 0.])`), then named as the problem ("The gradient is zero, as calculus says, and useless for training."), then replaced. The sentence "Automatic differentiation cannot produce this rule, because it is not the derivative of what the forward pass computes" tells the reader why a new mechanism is needed. The PyTorch warning that calling `forward` directly "never runs" the custom backward pass, "and no error says so", is the kind of fact that saves a day.
- **The closing bridge**: "RMSNorm squares its inputs, and the sum of squares can overflow in low precision." A real open question that the next section answers.

## Structural findings

- **"Registration" repeats the two previous sections.** Parameters found by the optimizer, containers, save and load, device moves: each was shown in `sec_model_construction` and `sec_parameters`, and each is shown again here for `RMSNorm` with a cell apiece. The subsection's own conclusion is that nothing new happened ("None of these four checks required code in `RMSNorm` beyond the registration of its gain."). One cell (build a model containing the layer, copy its state into a clone, compare outputs) makes the point; the rest is a sentence.
- **Buffers are introduced a third time.** "Layers with Fixed State" restates what a buffer is and shows once more that a plain attribute would be left out of the state. What is new here is only the example. That is acceptable if the example pays off, which it currently does not (CL-6).
- **Two of three constructions stop before the reader sees them work.** RMSNorm is tested against the library. Random features produce a matrix. The straight-through estimator produces the gradient (1, 2, 3). The section is about extending what a model can do, and in none of the three cases does a model do something it could not do before.
- **Tabs are light here** (12 blocks, mostly one or two sentences) because the four frameworks agree on the lesson. This is the shape the other sections should aim for.

## Passage-level findings

- **CL-1** (minor, explanation). Opening. Quote: "Sometimes the problem is the derivative instead. Rounding, which is needed" The second motivation arrives in a two-sentence paragraph and assumes the reader knows why anyone stores weights as 4-bit integers. Fix: one clause of purpose ("to fit a large model on a phone or a single GPU, its weights are often stored as 4-bit integers") and a link to the 8B model: 8 billion weights at 4 bits are 4 GB.
- **CL-2** (minor, language). "Layers without Parameters". Quote: "Feeding data through confirms that it does what it says:" Filler before a cell. Fix: say what to look for ("the five inputs 1 to 5 have mean 3, so the output is $-2$ to $2$").
- **CL-3** (minor, thought). Same subsection. Quote: "return X - X.mean()" The layer subtracts the mean over the whole minibatch, so the output for one example depends on the others. The text does not say so; exercise 1 makes the reader discover it, which is good, but the later remark that layer normalization centers "as a per-example `CenteredLayer` would" then reads as if the layer above were per-example. Fix: one sentence where the layer is defined ("over all entries of the minibatch; exercise 1 asks what that implies").
- **CL-4** (minor, explanation). "Layers with Parameters". Quote: "depends on the weights. A *normalization layer* removes this dependence: it" Normalization layers are introduced in one sentence, two chapters before the book treats them. The sentence is adequate. It would help to say that this is the first of a family the reader will meet again (batch normalization, layer normalization), so they file it correctly.
- **CL-5** (moderate, pedagogy). "Registration". Quote: "one in four ways: its parameters are found, it works inside containers, its" Four checks, four cells, three of which print something the reader has seen printed for other modules in the last two sections. Fix: keep the save, load and compare cell, which exercises all four at once, and state the other three in the sentence already there.
- **CL-6** (moderate, pedagogy). "Layers with Fixed State". Quote: "model. A simple example is a layer of *random features*" For this reader random features are not a simple example: nothing so far suggests why multiplying by a random matrix and taking a cosine is useful. The text asserts that "a linear layer on top of them can fit nonlinear functions of the input while only the linear layer is trained" and moves on. That claim is the memorable part, and exercise 4, part 3 (fit $y = \sin(3x)$) already contains the demonstration. Fix: move that fit into the text, six or eight lines with one plot. Then the argument for a buffer becomes concrete: reload the trained linear layer onto a fresh `RandomFeatures` and watch the fit turn to noise.
- **CL-7** (minor, thought). Same subsection. Quote: "but receives no gradient. Custom layers are where buffers are created." A sentence that sounds like a principle and says little; built-in layers create buffers too, as the last sentence of the subsection notes. Fix: cut.
- **CL-8** (major, pedagogy). "Functions with Custom Gradients". Quote: "To check the rule, we multiply the rounded values by known weights" The check confirms that the backward rule returns its input. The section's claim is stronger: that with this rule "a weight that is rounded in the forward pass" learns. Nothing is trained, and nothing is quantized to the 4 bits that motivate the subsection. Fix: add a short experiment. Fit a rounded weight vector to a target by gradient descent, once with `w.round()` (the loss does not move) and once with `RoundSTE.apply(w)` (it falls to the rounding error). Exercise 6 does a harder version; the simple one belongs in the text.
- **CL-9** (minor, explanation). Same subsection. Quote: "outside, where the clipped output does not change. The surrogate is a" The clipped variant is described in a sentence and then cited two paragraphs later as the reason the general mechanism is needed ("such as the clipping rule above"). The reader has to hold an unseen rule in mind. Fix: either show it (it is exercise 5, part 1) or drop the two "Other surrogates" sentences and say only that the identity is one choice among several.
- **CL-10** (minor, pedagogy). Same subsection. Quote: "For this particular rule there is also a shortcut without a hand-written" The stop-gradient form is what most code uses, and it is presented as an afterthought. Keep the order (the general mechanism explains the trick), but say that the shortcut is the common form in practice.
- **CL-11** (minor, language). Closing. Quote: "*kernels*, programs that run on the accelerator itself, is a separate craft" A third meaning of "kernel" within a few pages: Keras calls a weight matrix a kernel in the tabs, exercise 4 approximates a kernel function, and the next chapter's convolution has a kernel. Fix: "writing new low-level operations for the GPU" and no new term.

## Exercises

Six, well chosen and graded. Exercise 2 (scale invariance implies $\mathbf{J}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$, so the gradient is orthogonal to the input) is the best: a three-step argument with a consequence the reader can check numerically. Exercise 1 turns the toy layer into a real question about batch dependence. Exercise 4 is good; if its part 3 moves into the text (CL-6), parts 1 and 2 remain a complete problem. Exercise 5, part 2 (GELU or Softplus with a hand-written backward) is a drill and can go. The attribution lines under exercise 5 are longer than the exercise needs.
