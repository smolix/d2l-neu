# Initialization

Source: `chapter_builders-guide/init.md`. The PyTorch reader sees about 2,320 words of body prose (1,890 shared, 410 inside 10 PyTorch tab blocks) and about 790 words of exercises. The source holds about 3,760 words of body prose across the four tabs.

## Verdict

This section contains the best teaching in the first half of the chapter and the weakest. The best is the middle: a three-line argument that a residual block multiplies the mean square of its activations by $1 + g^2$, a prediction ($2^{16}$ after 32 blocks; at most $\sqrt{e}$ with the $1/\sqrt{N}$ scaling), and a measurement that confirms it. That is how MacKay would do it, and it is the one place in these four sections where mathematics explains a design choice of real models. The weakest is around it: the demonstrations of the API still use the first edition's toy examples (standard deviation 0.01, the constant 42, a three-case distribution on $\pm[5, 10]$, adding 1 to a weight matrix), which teach nothing about initialization and follow the climax as an anticlimax. And the section's central claim, that a badly initialized network does not train, is never shown: no model is trained. The most important fix is to end the section on the residual result and to let it finish with a training run.

## What works

- **The opening restates what it needs.** The He rule of the previous chapter is given again in two sentences with its meaning ("Each layer then passes on the mean square of its input unchanged"), and only then does the text show where it fails. This is the model for how to depend on another chapter.
- **The puzzle in the third paragraph.** "In a residual network the activations grow even though every layer follows this rule ... After 32 blocks the standard deviation of the activations has grown by a factor of $2^{16} \approx 65{,}000$, before training has started." The reader wants to know why.
- **"Variance Growth across Blocks".** The derivation names its one non-routine step ("The branch output is uncorrelated with $\mathbf{x}_k$, because the weights of the output projection have mean zero and are independent of $\mathbf{x}_k$"), displays one equation, and reads it in words with numbers: "a factor of 2 after 2 blocks, 16 after 8, and $65{,}536$ after 32."
- **"Scaling Down Residual Branches".** $(1 + 1/N)^N \le e$ is an elegant bound, the numbers 1.5, 1.60 and 1.64 are right, and the link to GPT-2 tells the reader this is what real models do.
- **The measurement.** One table and one logarithmic plot; the stored PyTorch output (1.93, 14, 9.07e4 unscaled; 1.5, 1.64, 1.6 scaled; 1 for zero) agrees with every prediction to the accuracy claimed.
- **The exercises on normalized branches, gradients across depth and data-driven calibration** extend the argument instead of repeating it.

## Structural findings

- **Order.** The section runs: defaults, API, residual analysis (the peak), truncated normals, a made-up distribution, editing weights by hand. Everything after the plot is of lower interest than what came before. Reorder so that the mechanics come first and the residual analysis last: Library Defaults; Applying Initializers (using He as the example, with truncated normals as a short second example of a library initializer); Initialization of Residual Networks. "A Custom Distribution" and "Direct Edits" can be cut to a paragraph or moved to exercises.
- **No training.** The chapter introduction promises that "a badly initialized model does not train". This section measures standard deviations after a forward pass and says "Initial activations this large make optimization impractical." A reader is asked to believe the sentence the section exists to demonstrate. One short experiment would close the argument: train the 32-block stack on Fashion-MNIST with the three gains for a few epochs and plot the three loss curves. It would also answer the question the text leaves open: when both work, is there a reason to prefer the scaled or the zero projection?
- **The API demonstrations do not use the section's own content.** "Applying Initializers" opens with "To use He initialization, or the residual scaling later in this section, we have to apply a rule of our own", and then applies $\mathcal{N}(0, 0.01^2)$, Xavier, and the constant 42. A function `init_he` applied to a ten-layer ReLU stack, with the output scale printed before and after, would teach the mechanism and the reason for it in the same cell.
- **Terminology drifts from the neighbouring sections.** The inner network $f$ of a residual block is the *body* in the two previous sections (and in the code here: `self.body`), and the *branch* throughout this prose. The symbol $g$ is a scalar gain of an initializer here and, in bold, the learned gain vector of RMSNorm in the next section; the previous section scaled the same residual term with $\alpha$. Pick one word and one symbol for the chapter.

## Passage-level findings

- **INI-1** (moderate, explanation). "Library Defaults". Quote: "The default is not He initialization, so in general it does not keep the" The text measures the default's range and standard deviation and never says what it does to a stack of ReLU layers. For PyTorch the answer is simple and striking (standard deviation $1/\sqrt{3 n_\textrm{in}}$, so each layer with a ReLU multiplies the mean square by $1/6$), and it is left to exercise 1. Nor does the text say why the models of earlier chapters trained anyway. Fix: add the factor $1/6$ and one sentence ("with one or two hidden layers this hardly matters; with ten it does").
- **INI-2** (minor, explanation). Same subsection. Quote: "distribution on $[-b, b]$ has standard deviation $b/\sqrt{3}$." The fact is needed to read the PyTorch cell's second line but is placed before four tabs of prose, three of which do not use it. Fix: move it into the sentence that introduces the predicted standard deviation.
- **INI-3** (minor, thought). Same subsection. Quote: "When should we override the default? Four situations recur: a deep network" A list of four situations in one sentence, of which the section treats one (residual structure). Fix: keep two (depth without normalization; structure the per-layer rule ignores) and drop "reproducing a paper" and "parameters we create by hand", which are not reasons about initialization.
- **INI-4** (moderate, pedagogy). "Applying Initializers". Quote: "nn.init.normal_(module.weight, mean=0, std=0.01)" and "nn.init.constant_(module.weight, 42)". Toy values that the section itself calls a poor choice ("a constant is a poor choice for training, but it makes the mechanics visible"). The mechanics are equally visible with a rule the reader has a reason to apply. Fix: as under Structural findings, demonstrate `apply` with He initialization on a deep ReLU stack and show the scale it restores.
- **INI-5** (minor, explanation). "Applying Initializers", PyTorch tab. Quote: "registered; replacing it would leave `net.parameters()` and any existing" A real trap, stated in the abstract. Fix: half a sentence of what the reader would see ("the optimizer goes on updating a tensor the model no longer uses").
- **INI-6** (minor, explanation). "Variance Growth across Blocks". Quote: "of the output projection have mean zero and are independent of" Correct and well placed. One step before it is taken on trust: "The first layer and the ReLU pass on $m_k$, by the argument for He initialization". Since the whole derivation is three lines, restate that argument in a clause (the layer doubles the mean square, the ReLU halves it).
- **INI-7** (moderate, thought). "Starting a Block at Zero". Quote: "shallow function whose depth comes into play as training proceeds. Symmetry" The paragraph explains why a zero output projection is safe and stops. The section now offers two repairs and no guidance. Fix: one or two sentences on the choice (zero makes the initial function exactly the shallow network and is the usual choice with normalization layers; $1/\sqrt{N}$ keeps every branch active from the first step), ideally supported by the training run proposed above.
- **INI-8** (minor, language). "Measuring Variance across Depth". Quote: "The three treatments, unscaled, scaled, and zero, set the gain of the output projection to 1, to" "Treatments" is laboratory vocabulary introduced without need, and it recurs in two exercises. Fix: "the three choices of gain".
- **INI-9** (moderate, thought). Same subsection. Quote: "blocks it lands between about 20,000 and 200,000, in two runs out of three" The reader sees one run (9.07e4 in the stored PyTorch output, above the prediction) and is told about a distribution over runs they cannot see, followed by a correct but compressed explanation ("the typical run lies below the square root of the average"). Fix: either print three seeds so the scatter is visible, or say only what the one run shows: "within a factor of a few of the predicted 65,536; the factor of each block depends on its random weights, and 32 such factors multiply." The remark about typical versus average would make a good exercise.
- **INI-10** (minor, thought). "Truncated Normals". Quote: "five standard deviations. "Therefore" attributes a motive to those models that the cited papers do not state, and "use up dynamic range" is not explained. Fix: state the fact (they use a truncated normal with standard deviation 0.02) and the effect (no weight beyond two standard deviations) without the causal claim.
- **INI-11** (moderate, pedagogy). "A Custom Distribution". Quote: "initializer ourselves. - **INI-12** (minor, explanation). "Direct Edits", PyTorch tab. - **INI-13** (minor, thought). Summary. Quote: "not He initialization, so a model whose behavior matters sets its" An overstatement: most models are trained with library defaults plus normalization layers, as the closing paragraph of the body concedes ("Keeping the activations at scale during training takes more"). ## Exercises

Eight for the PyTorch reader (about a third of the body's length), the strongest set in the first half. Exercise 1, part 3 (why the second moment stops shrinking once the default biases are restored) is a real discovery. "Normalized branches" ($1 + N g^2$ in place of $(1 + g^2)^N$) is the natural next step of the section's argument and could be promoted into the text in place of the custom distribution. "Zero initialization" duplicates the symmetry argument of the previous chapter and is the one to cut. "Kaiming initialization" is an implementation drill with a useful second part.
