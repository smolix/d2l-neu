# Parameters, State, and Memory

Source: `chapter_builders-guide/parameters-state-memory.md`. The PyTorch reader sees about 2,830 words of body prose (1,970 shared, 820 inside 19 PyTorch tab blocks) and about 600 words of exercises. The source holds about 6,000 words of body prose across the four tabs.

## Verdict

The first two thirds are good textbook writing: a concrete question (why does an 8-billion-parameter model need 128 GB before it has seen an example?), a distinction that changes how the reader thinks (parameter, buffer, local variable, plain attribute), and a table of bytes per parameter that the reader will reuse for the rest of the book. The last third ("Shared, Frozen, and Averaged Parameters") is a second section attached to the first. It needs language models, embeddings, fine-tuning, batch normalization, exponential moving averages and Adam's update rule, none of which this reader has met, and two of its demonstrations show a framework side effect and not an idea. The most important fix: make the section about one question (what tensors does a model hold, and what do they cost?) and reduce the last third to weight tying, which is the one topic there that deepens that question.

## What works

- **The opening.** "Its weights take 32 GB ... Training it takes four times as much memory, 128 GB, before it has seen a single example ... That is more than an 80 GB GPU holds." A reader who has only trained Fashion-MNIST models now has a reason to count.
- **The rule for kinds of state** (PyTorch tab under "Parameters and Buffers"): "make a tensor a *parameter* if the optimizer should update it, a *buffer* if checkpoints need it, and a local variable if it lives for one call." One sentence, usable for life. It is preceded by the right example (input statistics that "must be saved with the model, since predictions are wrong without them").
- **The bytes-per-parameter table** and the sentence after it: "These 16 bytes are a floor." The text says what the floor omits (activations, workspaces, allocator reserve) and where they are measured. The hand count 1344 + 2 · 8320 + 650 = 18,634 matches the stored output, as does 0.28 MiB.
- **The tied-gradient identity.** A displayed equation, one plain sentence that reads it ("The tied gradient is the sum of the untied gradients."), and a check that returns `True`. This is the Cover and Thomas rhythm, and it is the only place in the first half of the chapter where an equation is verified by construction of a twin model.
- **The predicted 0.3686** under "Weight Averages": the text derives the printed number from the recurrence $e \leftarrow 0.9\,(e + 0.1)$ (it does give 0.3686 after five steps, and the stored output is 0.36856). The habit of predicting an output before reading it is what makes the chapter more than documentation, even though this instance is in the wrong place (PSM-9).

## Structural findings

- **Three sections in one.** "The State Tree" answers what a model holds. "Training Memory" answers what it costs. "Shared, Frozen, and Averaged Parameters" answers three further questions (how to tie, how to freeze, how to average) whose common thread is only that they concern parameters. The guide's test (state the section's idea in one sentence) fails for the third part.
- **The third part runs ahead of the book.** Tying needs tokens, a vocabulary, an embedding and a language model (chapter 8 and later); freezing needs fine-tuning (far later) and batch normalization (chapter 7); the EMA needs `sec_training_recipes`; all of the memory arithmetic needs Adam (chapter 9, never used in chapters 1 to 4). Each is defined in a clause, which is the right technique, but six forward-defined concepts in 1,100 words is too many for a reader who came for parameters.
- **A slow start.** "Access by Path" spends six cells establishing that `net[3].bias` is a parameter with a shape, that `net[1].body[0].weight` has shape 64 × 64, that `.grad` is `None`, and that there are 12 named parameters. The previous section already printed named parameters and explained paths with a figure. Two cells would do: the named list, and the comparison with `state_dict` that sets up buffers.
- **Buffers are motivated three times in the chapter**: by the constant `alpha` at the end of the previous section, by the standardizing layer here, and by random features in the custom-layers section, each time with the demonstration that a plain attribute is left behind. This section is the right home; the other two should refer to it.

## Passage-level findings

- **PSM-1** (moderate, explanation). Opening. Quote: "weight needs a gradient of the same size, and the Adam optimizer, which we". Adam is three chapters away and the whole "Training Memory" part rests on it. The clause given is adequate, but the reader is entitled to know why this optimizer and not the one they use. Fix: add one sentence where the table appears: "Almost all large models are trained with Adam or a variant of it, so this is the count that matters in practice; minibatch SGD, which we have used so far, would store 8 bytes."
- **PSM-2** (minor, language). "Access by Path". Quote: "Our specimen is a residual MLP: an input layer maps 20 features to 64, two". "Specimen" is used four times for the model and becomes a coined name ("On our specimen:"). Fix: "our residual MLP", as the freezing subsection already says.
- **PSM-3** (moderate, pedagogy). "Access by Path", PyTorch tab. Quote: "this model yet, so there is nothing to see:" A cell that exists to show nothing. Together with the two shape cells before it, this is where the section drags. Fix: cut the three cells; state in one sentence that each parameter carries a `.grad` that is empty until backpropagation runs.
- **PSM-4** (minor, correctness). "Parameters and Buffers". Quote: "class Whitener(nn.Module):" The layer subtracts a mean and divides by a standard deviation per feature. That is standardization. Whitening also removes correlations between features. Fix: rename to `Standardizer`; the prose already says "standardizes".
- **PSM-5** (moderate, thought). "Bytes per Parameter". Quote: ":cite:`Micikevicius.Narang.Alben.ea.2018`: $2 + 2 + 4 + 4 + 4 = 16$ bytes". The paragraph previews mixed precision (16-bit compute copies, an fp32 master copy, automatic casting) to conclude "A multiplier therefore means little until we know the dtype of each tensor." This undercuts the "four times" the section has just taught, uses three concepts from a section two ahead, and the arithmetic cannot be followed without them. Fix: one sentence. "Computing in 16-bit numbers (:numref:`sec_numerics`) speeds training up but does not lower this floor, because the recipe keeps a 32-bit copy of the weights." Leave the sum to the numerics section.
- **PSM-6** (moderate, pedagogy). "Counting Without Allocating". Quote: "8B model this order is backwards: to learn whether its 32 GB of weights fit". The problem is real but the text solves it by multiplication in the next sentences ("$4096 \times 128{,}256 = 525{,}336{,}576$ entries, 2.1 GB in fp32") before any code runs, so the meta-device cell confirms a product. The second cell (`to_empty`, loading without drawing) is about loading, a topic of a later section. Fix: keep the hand count as a paragraph at the end of "Bytes per Parameter"; move the meta device to the saving and loading section, where "each weight is written once, from the file" is the point, or to an exercise.
- **PSM-7** (minor, explanation). Same subsection. Quote: "maps a hidden state of width 4,096 to scores for the 128,256 tokens of its". "Tokens" and "vocabulary" are used here and defined 110 lines later under "Tied Parameters". Fix: define at first use, or say "one score for each of the 128,256 word pieces the model can output".
- **PSM-8** (minor, thought). "Tied Parameters". Quote: "the studies that introduced it found that it also improves the predictions" The reader is given a saving of 31% and an improvement, and no reason for either to be plausible beyond "an analogous meaning: one vector per token". Fix: one sentence of intuition (a token that is a likely next word after a context should have an output vector close to the vectors of the contexts' words; tying forces input and output similarity to agree).
- **PSM-9** (major, pedagogy). "Frozen Parameters", PyTorch tab. Quote: "parameters. Freezing has two more effects that raise no error. First, it" The first "effect" is demonstrated by freezing `net` after an Adam step and printing `moments == 2 * n`, that is, `True` for the second time. The cell shows that nothing happened. For this reader, who has never fine-tuned a model or built an optimizer by hand, the observation that an optimizer keeps state for parameters frozen after its construction is a maintenance note. Exercise 6, part 3, already asks the reader to work it out with real numbers (96 GB that stay allocated), which is the better place. Fix: cut the demonstration; keep one sentence and the exercise.
- **PSM-10** (moderate, pedagogy). Same subsection. Quote: "`requires_grad` and `.eval()` are independent switches: the first stops" This is a true and useful rule, but batch normalization is two chapters ahead and the reader cannot yet see why running statistics exist. Fix: keep the rule in one sentence with a forward pointer, and move the cell to the batch normalization section, where the reader has the layer in front of them.
- **PSM-11** (moderate, pedagogy). "Weight Averages". Quote: "it evaluates better than the last iterate depends on the decay and on the" The subsection introduces a technique and in the same breath declines to say whether it helps. A reader has no reason to learn it here. The prediction of 0.3686 is delightful but relies on a property of Adam (a constant gradient moves each entry by the learning rate) that the reader must take on faith. Fix: cut the subsection from this section. Keep the EMA as one sentence in the list of "state that changes outside backpropagation and belongs in the checkpoint", and move the worked prediction to the section that evaluates averaging.
- **PSM-12** (minor, language). Closing. Quote: "started from a value that some rule chose. The next section asks which rule" Good closing; keep. The preceding sentence lists five capabilities and would be stronger with three.

## Exercises

Six, mostly good. Exercise 1 (running statistics: predict $\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}$ after $k$ batches, then find the loss whose gradient step reproduces the update) is a fine problem with a real insight in part 3, but it depends on batch normalization and belongs with that section. Exercise 2 asks for "a one-line rule" that the text has already stated; it earns its place only through the integer counter (`num_batches_tracked`), so say so in the prompt or cut it. Exercise 6 (fine-tuning memory of the 8B model with and without a stale optimizer) is the best use of the section's arithmetic and should stay. Exercises 4 and 5 differ by framework in substance, not only in API; fine, but the PyTorch exercise 4 (`deepcopy` of a tied model) is the most instructive and its idea could be offered in all tabs.
