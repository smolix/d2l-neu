# Residual Networks: ResNet, ResNeXt, and DenseNet

Source: `chapter_convolutional-modern/resnet.md`. About 3,450 words of prose before the exercises, 1,110 words of exercises.

## Verdict

The most important section of the chapter and, against the bar, the weakest of this half. Its opening is excellent: the degradation problem is stated as a puzzle ("A deeper network that fits even its training data worse does not overfit; the optimizer fails to find a good setting of its parameters"), and the October complaint that ResNet "never states the problem it solves" is fully answered. After that the section does three things that undercut it. First, it explains the fix with an argument about nested function classes that its own opening has already shown to be beside the point: plain networks are nested too, and the puzzle is optimization. Second, it never demonstrates the problem or the fix. The only experiment trains ResNet-18 and reports 93%; the plain-against-residual comparison, which is the whole section, is exercise 4. Third, the last third builds ResNeXt and DenseNet blocks in four frameworks, checks their shapes, and trains neither, ending DenseNet with the remark that the design did not become standard. The single most important change: replace the function-class section with a paired experiment (plain and residual networks at two depths, training loss on shared axes) and build the explanation on what it shows.

## What works

- The first paragraph. It starts from the reader's expectation ("We might expect that adding still more layers keeps helping, at worst until the network starts to overfit"), reports the test, and draws the inference that makes it a puzzle: "the deeper network could copy the shallower one and pass its result through the extra layers unchanged, so a setting that is at least as good exists. Why does training not find it". This is the best section opening in the half-chapter.
- "Each block adds a learned correction to its input instead of replacing it, so a block whose correction is zero passes its input through unchanged." The idea in one jargon-free sentence, as the guide asks.
- The care about the final ReLU: "a block whose branch outputs zero is the identity only on inputs that are already nonnegative. Every block of the network receives such inputs, since each one follows a ReLU." Most treatments skip this.
- The honest reading of the result: "Its training loss falls below 0.01, far below its validation loss of about 0.27: with 11.2 million parameters for 60,000 training images, the network fits the training data almost perfectly and new data much less well." The stored plot agrees (validation loss rises after epoch 3) and the sentence sets up the training-recipes section.
- The motivation of ResNeXt in one line: "doubling the width of a block quadruples its arithmetic."
- The closing judgment on DenseNet's memory ("a direct implementation materializes a new, wider concatenation for every layer ... so its memory grows quadratically with the depth of the block") is precise and is the kind of systems fact readers remember.
- Exercises 3, 5 and 9 are among the best in the chapter (see below).

## Structural findings

- **The central claim is never shown.** The section quotes four numbers from He et al. for the degradation problem and then trains one residual network. Nothing in the reader's own run shows a deeper plain network training worse, or a residual connection curing it. Exercise 4 asks the reader to do it and hedges ("Does Fashion-MNIST at these depths show the degradation problem?"), which suggests the authors are not sure it does at depths 18 and 34. Find a setting where it does and put it in the text. Depth is cheap at native $28 \times 28$ resolution: narrow plain and residual stacks of, say, 20 and 56 layers (the CIFAR design of the paper, 16 to 64 channels) train in minutes. Plot four training-loss curves. This is the experiment the section exists for; the batch-norm section's paired LeNet run is the model.
- **"Function Classes and Depth" argues for something the opening already granted.** The opening says a setting at least as good exists in the deeper plain network. The function-class section then defines $\mathcal{F}$, $L^*_\mathcal{F}$ and nesting, shows a figure contrasting non-nested with nested classes, and concludes that nesting "does not promise that the optimizer finds that function". So the framework's own conclusion is that it does not explain the problem. Worse, :numref:`fig_functionclasses` invites the reading "plain networks are the left panel, residual networks the right", which contradicts the opening and the sentence "A plain layer ... can compute the identity only for particular values of its weights". The real content is the last sentence of the section: "A residual block changes the parameterization so that the identity lies at the simplest point of the parameter space, where the correction is zero." Keep that sentence, expand it (the plain layer represents the identity at one particular weight setting that nothing draws the optimizer toward; the residual block represents it at zero, the point that small initialization and weight decay favor), and cut the definition of $L^*_\mathcal{F}$ and the figure, or keep the figure with a caption saying both kinds of network are nested and the difference lies in how easily the optimizer reaches the inner class. See RES-1 and RES-2.
- **The explanation of why residual networks train is given without a statement of what is known.** The section offers two reasons (identity at zero; a direct gradient path) as settled. The batch-norm section had the courage to say "Why batch normalization helps so much is less settled than what it computes." The same is true here, and the source paper says so: He et al. argue that the difficulty of their plain networks is unlikely to be caused by vanishing gradients, since those networks use batch normalization and their gradient norms were healthy. The section's gradient paragraph ends "In a plain network the gradient must pass through every layer and is scaled by each", which reads as the vanishing-gradient explanation the paper set aside. A short paragraph in the manner of the batch-norm one would fix this: what is observed (residual networks optimize easily at depths where plain ones do not); the views that have evidence (a network that behaves like a collection of mostly short paths, the subject of exercise 5; gradients that stay correlated across inputs; a smoother loss surface); and that the paper's own conjecture was about optimization speed, not about vanishing signals.
- **The index's promise is not kept.** The chapter introduction says residual connections "let a deep network start out as a shallow one". In `Residual` both convolutions use He initialization and both batch-norm scales start at 1, so at initialization the branch is as large as the input. Zero-initializing the scale of the last batch normalization in each block makes every block the identity at the start; it is one line, :numref:`sec_training_recipes` lists it as a recipe ingredient, and exercise 3 circles it ("Name parameter values of `bn2` that make the block the identity in both modes"). Either do it here and say so, which ties the section's main idea to its code, or state plainly that the block as initialized is not the identity and that the argument concerns what is easy to reach.
- **ResNeXt and DenseNet: one is used later, the other is not, and the text says neither.** `ResNeXtBlock` is saved to `d2l` and is the building block of :numref:`sec_cnn-design`; the reader should be told ("we use this block again when we search over network designs"), since otherwise 35 lines of code per framework end in a shape check. DenseNet gets a formula, a figure, three code cells and three shape checks, is never assembled ("the assembly follows the ResNet code above") and never trained, and closes with "residual connections, not dense ones, became the standard". By the guide's rule for families (§4.3: mention a relative only if it changes one identifiable thing, and say what in one sentence) DenseNet is a paragraph and a figure: concatenate instead of add; features are reused; memory grows. If the idea is kept, connect it to where concatenating skip connections did survive (the U-Net of segmentation and diffusion models) so that the reader has a reason to care. The code can go to exercise 9, which is already the better treatment.
- **The section title names three architectures.** With the cuts above the title can name the idea: "Residual Connections" or "Residual Networks", with ResNeXt as a subsection.

## Passage-level findings

**RES-1** (major, thought). Under "Function Classes and Depth". Quote: "A larger architecture has a larger function class $\mathcal{F}'$."
Followed by "If the classes are *nested*". The paragraph treats nesting as the property in question, but the section's premise is that deeper plain networks already contain the shallower ones (opening paragraph) and still train worse. The reader is led through a definition, an inequality and a figure, and arrives at "It does not promise that the optimizer finds that function". See the second structural finding for the replacement.

**RES-2** (moderate, correctness). Same section. Quote: "can compute the identity only for particular values of its"
True and sufficient to make plain networks nested (on the nonnegative inputs each layer receives), which is the opposite of what the figure two paragraphs earlier suggests. Say so explicitly: "so the function classes of plain networks are nested as well. What the degradation results show is that gradient descent does not find these values."

**RES-3** (moderate, thought). Under "Residual Blocks". Quote: "for instance by setting the weights and bias of its last layer to zero".
In the block as built the last layer of the branch is a batch normalization and the convolutions have no bias. Zero weights in `conv2` give a zero input to `bn2`, whose output is then $\beta$ in training mode and a constant depending on the running mean in prediction mode, which is exercise 3. The simple statement that fits the code is "by setting the scale $\gamma$ of its last batch normalization to zero". This is also the natural place to mention zero-initialization (fourth structural finding).

**RES-4** (major, explanation). Under "Residual Blocks". Quote: "Two things remain on this path in ResNet: the ReLU after each addition, which"
The paragraph that begins "The shortcut also helps the gradient" carries the second half of the section's explanation and loses the reader. In eleven lines it states the Jacobian, qualifies the claim twice, refers to "The pre-activation design of the exercises" (not yet seen), forward-references the pre-norm transformer block and the ConvNeXt block, and only in its last sentence states the contrast that should have come first ("In a plain network the gradient must pass through every layer"). There is no worked case. Rewrite in the guide's order. Two blocks first: $\mathbf{x}_2 = \mathbf{x}_0 + g_1(\mathbf{x}_0) + g_2(\mathbf{x}_0 + g_1(\mathbf{x}_0))$, so the output contains the input itself plus corrections, and the derivative with respect to $\mathbf{x}_0$ contains the identity plus terms that pass through one or both branches. Then the general statement. Then one sentence on the plain network for contrast. Move the ReLU and downsampling qualifications to a following short paragraph, and drop the three forward references (the transformer chapter can point back).

**RES-5** (moderate, language). Under "Residual Blocks". Quote: "therefore average-pool the input of the".
The paragraph beginning "ResNet's residual block follows VGG's design" runs to about fifteen sentences and holds the block's layout, the missing bias, the identity-on-nonnegative-inputs subtlety, shape matching, and then a five-sentence digression on ResNet-D that uses "the bottleneck blocks of ResNet-50" before bottleneck blocks have been introduced. Exercise 7 restates the ResNet-D shortcut in full. Split the paragraph in three (layout; when it is the identity; matching shapes), cut the ResNet-D sentences to "With stride 2, this convolution reads only one of every four input positions; exercise 7 repairs that."

**RES-6** (minor, explanation). Section opening. Quote: "became more accurate as they became".
"The networks of this chapter so far became more accurate as they became deeper" is true of the ImageNet results quoted and false of the chapter's own table, where the 22-layer GoogLeNet ties the 8-layer AlexNet and the 11-layer VGG wins. Write "On ImageNet, each of the networks so far was deeper and more accurate than the last".

**RES-7** (minor, language). Under "Stem, Stages, and Head". Quote: "The body consists of four *stages*, each a sequence of residual blocks with".
The prose calls the unit a stage; the method that builds it is called `block` and takes `num_residuals`; the stem method is `b1`. A reader matching prose to code has to translate. Rename the method `stage` (and `b1` to `stem`), or say once that `block` builds a stage.

**RES-8** (minor, language). Under "Stem, Stages, and Head". Quote: "weights start from He initialization, which draws them with variance".
He initialization is explained in :numref:`sec_alexnet`, mentioned in :numref:`sec_blocks`, and explained again here and once more in each framework tab note below the cell. One clause and a reference suffice at the third use.

**RES-9** (moderate, thought). Under "Training". Quote: "Batch normalization makes the larger peak"
This paragraph changes the learning rate from the 0.03 of all earlier networks to 0.1 and then reports three experiments the reader does not see ("NiN diverges at a peak of 0.1 in some runs", "at 0.03 it ends about a point lower", "with a constant rate of 0.1, the validation accuracy moves by up to a few points"). It is author's-notebook material (guide §4.5) and it is the third such passage in the half-chapter. It also breaks the "one procedure" premise of the chapter's comparisons one paragraph before the comparison with VGG. Keep one sentence: "Batch normalization permits a larger peak, 0.1, as :numref:`sec_batch_norm` led us to expect; at 0.03 the result is about a point lower." If the proposed learning-rate sweep is added to the batch-norm section, that sentence then rests on something the reader has seen.

**RES-10** (minor, thought). After the run. Quote: "That is about a point above the quarter-width VGG, the best network of"
The comparison differs in architecture, normalization, peak learning rate and input size at once. The text flags the input size only. Say what the comparison can bear: ResNet-18 is at least as accurate as the best earlier network with a quarter of its parameters and a smaller input; the run does not isolate the residual connection (which is what the paired experiment would do).

**RES-11** (moderate, explanation). Under "ResNeXt". Quote: "$O(c b)$ multiply-adds per position and the grouped $3 \times 3$"
The chapter counts exactly everywhere else, and here switches to $O(\cdot)$ for quantities that are $2cb$ and $9b^2/g$. More important, the claim that grouping makes a block "wider at the same cost" is never made concrete. The numbers are at hand and settle it in two lines. A ResNet-50 bottleneck with $c = 256$, $b = 64$: $256 \cdot 64 + 9 \cdot 64^2 + 64 \cdot 256 = 69{,}632$ weights. The ResNeXt block of :numref:`fig_resnext_block` with $b = 128$, $g = 32$: $256 \cdot 128 + 9 \cdot 128^2/32 + 128 \cdot 256 = 70{,}144$. Twice the width in the middle at the same cost. Then the ImageNet numbers (23.9% to 22.2%) have something to attach to.

**RES-12** (minor, explanation). Under "ResNeXt". Quote: "because grouped convolutions make less efficient use of the hardware than dense ones".
An unexplained assertion in a chapter that otherwise explains its cost claims. Either give the reason in a clause (less arithmetic per byte of memory moved, the subject of :numref:`sec_efficient_cnns`) or cut.

**RES-13** (minor, language). Under "DenseNet". Quote: "where $[\cdot, \cdot]$ denotes concatenation. Every layer is connected to all".
The displayed expression nests three levels of brackets and is harder to read than the idea. A recursion says it in one line: $\mathbf{x}_k = [\mathbf{x}_{k-1}, f_k(\mathbf{x}_{k-1})]$, next to the residual $\mathbf{x}_k = \mathbf{x}_{k-1} + g_k(\mathbf{x}_{k-1})$. The parallel form also makes the comparison the subsection is about.

**RES-14** (minor, explanation). Under "DenseNet". Quote: "Because each layer reuses the features of all earlier ones, DenseNet reached".
"Because" asserts the mechanism; the paper's evidence is the parameter count. "DenseNet reached the ImageNet accuracy of ResNet with fewer parameters, which its authors attribute to the reuse of features" is what is known.

**RES-15** (minor, language). Summary. Quote: "which trains at a peak learning rate of 0.1".
The summary reports a hyperparameter and an accuracy but does not answer the opening question ("Why does training not find it"). Add the answer the section gives, in one sentence, with its status.

## Exercises

Nine problems and 1,100 words, the heaviest set in the half. Several are excellent. Exercise 3 (zero `conv2`, then the output of `bn2` in training and in prediction mode; whether ResNet-34 contains ResNet-18 "block by block") is a searching test of the section's main claim and exposes the gap noted in RES-3. Exercise 5 (count the paths through exactly $\ell$ of $K$ bodies) carries an explanation the main text should at least mention. Exercise 9 (DenseNet accounting) is a complete and better substitute for the DenseNet code in the text. Exercise 4 (plain networks) belongs in the main text. Exercises 2 and 8 both treat pre-activation, one on paper and one in code; merge them into one problem with two parts. Exercise 6 part 2 is long but each step teaches. Exercise 7 asks "Is the difference larger than the variation between runs?", which is the right habit to teach. After moving exercise 4 and merging 2 with 8, seven problems remain, within the band.
