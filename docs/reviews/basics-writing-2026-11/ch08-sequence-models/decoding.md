# Decoding and Generation

Source: `chapter_recurrent-neural-networks/decoding.md`. About 3,600 words of prose (two short tab notes aside, all shared), 900 words of exercises, two schematic figures, two plots, 24 code cells.

## Verdict

A well-organized section with a clear thesis (maximize when few outputs are acceptable, sample when many are) and one good experiment that supports it: beam search wins on log-probability and loses badly on distinct-3. The writing is plain and the greedy counterexample is a model of economy. Its weakness is that most of its other demonstrations are run on a model too small to show the differences the prose describes. The reader is told what top-$k$, top-$p$ and min-$p$ do to text and shown five lines of near-gibberish in which the difference cannot be seen; the one quantitative comparison of the truncation rules supports a conclusion about min-$p$ that the same output supports equally for top-$p$. The fix is to let statistics over many continuations carry the comparison, as the last experiment already does.

## What works

- **The thesis paragraph.** "Even if we could find it, the most probable sequence is often not what we want. ... When many continuations are acceptable, as in writing a story, each of them is individually improbable, and a model that has learned this spreads its probability across them." A reader who remembers one paragraph of the section should remember this one.
- **Myopia in four numbers.** $P(A) = 0.5$, $P(B) = 0.4$, then $0.3$ and $0.9$: "$0.5 \times 0.3 = 0.15$, whereas starting with $B$ gives $0.4 \times 0.9 = 0.36$." The smallest example that shows the failure.
- **Beam search placed between two extremes.** "Greedy decoding commits immediately; exhaustive search never commits at all." and the count "$5 + 10 + 10 = 25$" against 125.
- **A neat observation in the implementation.** "Since the best $w$ candidates contain at most $w$ continuations of any single prefix, it suffices to expand each prefix by its $w$ most probable tokens".
- **Each rule's failure named.** Top-$k$: "a fixed $k = 20$ admits nineteen poor options". Top-$p$: "if the top token holds 0.95 of the mass, top-$p$ with $p = 0.9$ keeps one token and acts greedily". The rules are presented as successive repairs, each with the case that breaks it.
- **Cutoffs as lines on one plot.** "Top-$k$ is a vertical line at rank $k$ ... Min-$p$ is a *horizontal* line". The figure (stored: top-$p$ keeps 154 tokens, min-$p$ 15) lets the reader see three rules as three cuts through one curve.
- **The evaluation experiment.** Stored output: log-probability per token of -2.22, -1.54, -2.89 and distinct-3 of 0.14, 0.20, 0.99 for greedy, beam and min-$p$. "The two averages disagree." The prose is worded to survive re-execution ("by a margin that varies from run to run and occasionally reverses").
- **"perplexity selects the model, and human judgment, or a proxy for it, selects the decoding strategy."** A sentence that earns its place as a summary.

## Structural findings

- **Greedy decoding and temperature are taught twice.** `rnn-implementation.md` introduces both, with outputs and the same diagnosis (loops, invented words). This section re-derives them and reruns the temperature sweep. See IMP-16; the cleaner split is for this section to own everything beyond a first greedy sample.
- **Machinery without a use.** Length normalization, the end-of-sequence token and the "finished" flag of `beam_search` are explained and implemented, but no experiment in the section has an end token, so every candidate has the same length and normalization does nothing (DEC-4).
- **The section retrains the model** (ten epochs) before it can start. This is a consequence of one notebook per section, and costs the reader a cell and a plot they have seen. A sentence saying so is enough; it has one.
- **The link back to `sequence.md` is missing.** That section promised that ":numref:`sec_decoding` compares such rules for text" as a response to rollout error. This section never mentions the rollout. One sentence where sampling's tail is introduced ("one absurd token, fed back as context, can derail everything after it") would close that loop: this is the rollout of :numref:`sec_sequence` with tokens in place of numbers.

## Passage-level findings

- **DEC-1** (minor, language). Opening and "The Decoding Problem". Quotes: "$1027^{50} > 10^{150}$ possible continuations, far too many to compare." and "continuation is more than $10^{150}$. A". The same count twice within forty lines, and the opening's second and third paragraphs preview greedy decoding, sampling and both families before "The Decoding Problem" states them again. Fix: cut the opening to its first paragraph plus one sentence of route, and let the first subsection carry the count.
- **DEC-2** (minor, explanation). "The Decoding Problem". Quote: "distribution with settings called temperature, top-$k$, top-$p$, and". Four names the reader cannot yet attach to anything, in a roadmap sentence (guide §6.2). "Sampling methods, which first reshape or truncate the distribution" is enough.
- **DEC-3** (minor, thought). "Greedy Decoding". Quote: "text that repeats tends to keep repeating. Taking the most probable token at". The explanation of why maximization repeats is circular as written (models favor repetition because text that repeats repeats). For the small recurrent model at hand there is a mechanical account that the reader can check: greedy decoding is a deterministic map from state to state, so once a state recurs the output cycles for ever. Say that, then cite Holtzman et al. for the observation that large models do it too.
- **DEC-4** (moderate, pedagogy). "Beam Search". Quotes: "favors short candidates. This matters once an end-of-sequence token lets" and "In the code below it is the argument `alpha`, the name used". Length normalization is motivated, given an equation, an exponent "typically around 0.75" and an implementation, and then never observed: the two runs below it have no end token, so all candidates have equal length and the printed "score" is simply the log-probability divided by $30^{0.75}$, a number the reader cannot interpret. Only exercise 3 exercises it, by treating a newline as the end. Separately, the exponent is $\beta$ in the text and `alpha` in the code, with a sentence to explain the mismatch. Fix: run the second beam example with the newline as `eos_id` and $\beta \in \{0, 0.75\}$, so that the reader sees short candidates win without normalization; print the mean log-probability per token as the score; name the argument `beta`.
- **DEC-5** (minor, language). "Beam Search". Quote: "$x_{\leq t_0}$ be the given prefix of $t_0$ tokens, and suppose that the two most". The toy example over $\{A, \ldots, E\}$ carries a subscripted prefix through two displayed equations. Dropping the prefix ("at the first step, suppose $A$ and $C$ are the most probable") loses nothing and halves the notation.
- **DEC-6** (moderate, thought). "Cutoffs on a Trained Model". Quote: "probability. Min-$p$ is strict where the model is sure and permissive where". The stored output reads: after "The Time Traveller" top-$p$ keeps 154 tokens and min-$p$ 15; after "The Time" both keep 2. So top-$p$ adapts to the model's confidence at least as much as min-$p$ does, and the sentence credits min-$p$ alone with a property the experiment shows for both. What the output does show, and the text does not say, is the difference between them where the model is unsure: top-$p$ admits ten times as many tokens, reaching far into the tail (its cut falls near probability $10^{-3}$ on the plot), while min-$p$ stops at 15. Fix: "Both rules tighten when the model is sure. They differ when it is not: to collect 90% of the mass top-$p$ must admit 154 tokens, most of them with probability below one percent, while min-$p$ keeps the 15 that are within a factor of twenty of the best."
- **DEC-7** (minor, explanation). "Cutoffs on a Trained Model". Quote: "flattens into a long tail of tokens with tiny probabilities, the tail that". On the logarithmic axes of the plot the curve does the opposite of flattening: it declines steadily and then falls off steeply past rank 500. "Flattens" describes a linear plot the reader is not looking at. Fix: "falls by a factor of a hundred over the first hundred ranks and continues down through a tail of several hundred tokens, each with probability below one in a thousand".
- **DEC-8** (moderate, thought). "Strategies Compared on One Prefix". Quote: "in between: they keep the variety of sampling and cut the tail on which pure". One 50-token sample per strategy cannot show this, and the stored samples do not: top-$k$ produces "belligent cheermine", top-$p$ "a kind of civeds". The text concedes that the model "cannot make any of these continuations good", then draws the conclusion anyway. Fix: move the claim to the evaluation subsection and support it there. Add the pure sampler and the top-$k$ and top-$p$ samplers to the table that already compares greedy, beam and min-$p$, with one more column that measures what truncation is for, such as the mean log-probability of the sampled tokens or the fraction of generated words that occur in the novel. Then "truncated samplers sit in between" becomes a row of numbers.
- **DEC-9** (minor, pedagogy). "A Unified Sampler". Quote: "Every draw respects its cutoff: tokens 3 and 4 never appear, and token 2". Fifteen random draws per rule to confirm that a cutoff cuts. The arithmetic in the paragraph before the cell ("since the cumulative probability first reaches 0.8 at the third (0.85)") already teaches the rules; printing the kept set for each rule would check the code deterministically in one line.
- **DEC-10** (minor, explanation). Opening. Quote: "reuse them in :numref:`sec_machine_translation`.". The section named as the consumer of beam search now sits in the Attic part. Point instead to wherever the Advanced or Language Models part first decodes with an end token, or drop the reference.
- **DEC-11** (minor, correctness). "Sampling". Quote: "renormalize, and sample :cite:`Radford.Wu.Child.ea.2019`. **Top-$p$** (or". Top-$k$ sampling is cited to the GPT-2 paper, which used it; it is usually credited to Fan, Lewis and Dauphin (2018). Worth checking the attribution.
- **DEC-12** (minor, thought). "The Cost of Generation". Quote: "the quadratic part is pure waste. The hidden state already summarizes the". The section chose a stateless `step_fn` so that the decoders work with any model, and then calls the consequence "pure waste" for the only model it has. Say why the interface was chosen (a later model has no fixed-size state to carry) so that the waste reads as a deliberate trade and the closing point about constant cost per token lands as the advantage of recurrence it is.

## Exercises

A good set with real variety. Exercise 2 (construct probabilities for which beam search misses the best sequence; smallest width that guarantees optimality) tests the algorithm's limits on paper. Exercise 4 (which cutoff is invariant to temperature) is short and sharp. Exercise 5 (ban every token containing "e") is fun and leads to a real point about subword constraints. Exercise 8 (a stateful `step_fn`) makes the reader implement the claim of the cost subsection. Exercise 7 (a taxonomy of failures, from CS224n) is open but well scaffolded. Exercise 3 carries the demonstration of length normalization that the text omits (DEC-4).
