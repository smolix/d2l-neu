# Language Models

Source: `chapter_recurrent-neural-networks/language-model.md`. About 3,900 words of prose (one short MXNet note aside, all shared), 950 words of exercises, one schematic figure, two plots, 23 code cells.

## Verdict

The most substantial section of the chapter and, paragraph by paragraph, some of its best writing: the experiments are sharp, the numbers are quoted at the precision the outputs support, and perplexity is introduced with a motivation a reader will remember. The treatment of perplexity and bits per byte is the cleanest I know in a textbook at this level. The weakness is order. Two smoothing methods are derived before the reader has a metric that shows why smoothing is needed or whether it worked, Zipf's law arrives after the sparsity it explains, and the Kneser-Ney recursion gets no number until three subsections later. Reordering would turn a good section into an excellent one without adding a word.

## What works

- **The opening.** "to recognize speech" against "to wreck a nice beach": a problem where the plausibility of text is the only thing that can decide, stated before any notation.
- **The trigram table speaks.** "followed by "traveller" in about half of them and by "machine" in about a third, so the model has learned that the novel's protagonist is the Time Traveller." (Stored: 47/87 and 27/87.)
- **Memorization measured.** The longest copied run turns "the trigram text reads like Wells" into a diagnosis: "It has memorized its training data rather than generalized from it."
- **Kneser-Ney's third idea on the book's own corpus.** ""traveller" occurs 49 times but after only two different words ("time" 48 times, "real" once), while "said" occurs 76 times after 48 different words." This beats the standard "San Francisco" example because the reader has just seen "the time traveller" in the table.
- **Why an average is needed.** "any model gives Tolstoy's *War and Peace* a far smaller probability than Saint-Exupéry's *The Little Prince*, whatever its quality."
- **Perplexity.** "A model that gives every observed token probability $1/4$ has perplexity 4", the phrase "effective branching factor", and the three reference points (1, infinity, $|\mathcal{V}|$).
- **The surprise.** "the trigram model, which wrote by far the most convincing text, has by far the *worst* held-out perplexity." October marked this "keep". It is the best sentence in the section and the reason the reader will distrust samples from now on.
- **Bits per byte.** "Judged by perplexity, the three Laplace-smoothed models differ by more than two orders of magnitude. Judged by bits per byte, they lie within half a bit of each other, and the ordering reverses." (Stored: 6.4, 281.6, 2618.1 against 2.68, 2.58, 2.21.) The compression reading ("a fraction $\textrm{bpb}/8$ of its raw size") and the three magnitudes that close the subsection (Shannon's one bit per character, below one for strong neural models, 1.6 to 2.7 here) give the reader a scale.

## Structural findings

- **Order of smoothing, Zipf and perplexity** (LM-S1, major). The current sequence is: counting, sampling, count sparsity, Laplace smoothing, Kneser-Ney, Zipf's law, perplexity, the evaluation of Laplace and Kneser-Ney, bits per byte, data pipeline. So the reader derives absolute discounting, interpolation and continuation counts (about 500 words and a dense function) without a way to tell whether the result is any good, then reads a section on word frequencies, and only then sees the table that makes Kneser-Ney matter (2,618 against 302). Zipf's law is introduced as the cause of sparsity ("How fast the count tables go sparse depends on how word frequencies are distributed") after sparsity and both of its remedies, and has to say "as we measured above". Proposed order: (1) counting and sampling; (2) count sparsity together with Zipf's law, as cause and measurement; (3) perplexity, motivated by the need to compare models and by the observation that one unseen trigram gives infinite perplexity; (4) Laplace smoothing, evaluated at once (the trigram is worst); (5) Kneser-Ney as the repair, evaluated at once (the trigram is best); (6) bits per byte; (7) the data pipeline. Every method then answers a failure the reader has just seen in a number, which is MacKay's method.
- **Four jobs in one section.** Count models, smoothing, evaluation metrics, and the minibatch pipeline. At 3,900 words it is within the ceiling but it is two ideas (how to estimate by counting, how to measure a language model) plus plumbing. If the chapter is ever rebalanced, "Perplexity and Bits per Byte" could stand as its own short section; it is what the rest of the book reuses.
- **Kneser-Ney is the right amount of classical material**, because `rnn-implementation.md` uses it as the baseline the RNN has to beat. The Bayesian paragraph is not (LM-5).

## Passage-level findings

- **LM-1** (minor, thought). Opening. Quote: "removes the difficulty". The chain rule does not remove the difficulty of assigning probabilities to $10^{30}$ sequences; it moves it into conditionals whose contexts grow without bound, as the first sentence of the next subsection says ("the number of distinct contexts grows without bound"). Fix: "turns the problem into one we know: predicting the next token."
- **LM-2** (minor, language). "Perplexity". Quote: "$$\frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T -\log P(x_t \mid x_{t-1}, \ldots, x_1),$$". The conditioning context is written $x_1, \ldots, x_{t-1}$ in the factorization at the top of the section and $x_{t-1}, \ldots, x_1$ from here on; `rnn.md` keeps the reversed order and `decoding.md` introduces $x_{<t}$. One convention for the chapter (I would adopt $x_{<t}$ from the first use of the chain rule in `sequence.md`).
- **LM-3** (minor, pedagogy). "Markov Models and Counting". Quote: "conditional probability (the constant `alpha` is explained below), and". The class implements smoothing before the reader knows that anything needs smoothing. Under the proposed order `prob` would be added by `add_to_class` where Laplace smoothing is introduced.
- **LM-4** (moderate, thought). "Markov Models and Counting" and "Bits per Byte". Quotes: "vocab_size = len(set(words_train + words_val))" and "model comes out best only because its vocabulary was built from the". The word vocabulary is built from training and held-out text together. This is disclosed 300 lines after the code, and only for the bits-per-byte table, where the best row of the comparison is then declared unfair in the next sentence. It also flatters every word-level perplexity in the earlier table. The guide's rule applies: an experiment that needs its winner explained away should be changed. Fix: build the word vocabulary from the training text with an `<unk>` entry (the class for it was defined one section earlier), report the word row with that handicap visible, and say in one sentence what the unknown token costs. The conclusion ("This is one more reason modern models use subword tokenization") then follows from the table instead of from a caveat.
- **LM-5** (moderate, pedagogy). "Kneser--Ney Smoothing". Quote: "approximates inference in a hierarchical Pitman--Yor process, a Bayesian". The paragraph introduces the hierarchical Pitman-Yor process and the sequence memoizer, neither of which the reader can understand from the sentence or will meet again, and points forward to a subsection on power laws that has not happened yet. It fails all three tests of the guide's teachable core. Fix: move to the chapter's Further Reading with the two citations.
- **LM-6** (moderate, pedagogy). "Kneser--Ney Smoothing", after :eqref:`eq_kneser_ney`. Quote: "The function below implements :eqref:`eq_kneser_ney`. It counts the". The recursion is followed by three good sentences of interpretation but no number, and then by a 22-line function whose key line is `stats[k] = c, N, S, c1 / max(c1 + 2 * c2, 1)`. Every other formula in the section is followed by a small computed instance. Fix: after the function, print the discount $D$ for the trigram order and evaluate $P_\textrm{KN}(\textrm{traveller} \mid \textrm{the time})$ and $P_\textrm{KN}(w \mid \textrm{the time})$ for one unseen $w$, next to the Laplace values. Two lines of output would show the freed mass going somewhere sensible.
- **LM-7** (minor, explanation). "Kneser--Ney Smoothing". Quote: "precede $(u, w)$ in training, and $N(u)$ and $S(u)$ by the corresponding". "The corresponding sums" leaves the reader to work out what is summed. Fix: "and $N(u)$ and $S(u)$ by the total and the number of nonzero continuation counts in context $u$."
- **LM-8** (minor, thought). "Perplexity". Quote: "uniform guesses, and no choice of $\alpha$ rescues it (the exercises ask". A claim supported only by an exercise. One clause with the result would do ("the best $\alpha$ still leaves the trigram behind the bigram").
- **LM-9** (minor, thought). "Word Frequency and Zipf's Law". Quote: "text are missing from the table, as we measured above. A model that estimates the probability of each context". The subsection restates the sparsity result it was supposed to explain. Under the proposed order the two merge and this sentence disappears.
- **LM-10** (minor, language). "Partitioning Sequences". Quote: "pipeline of :numref:`sec_text-sequence` instead, which later chapters use". An option documented for the benefit of other chapters. Move to a code comment.
- **LM-11** (minor, explanation). "Partitioning Sequences". Quote: "The first `num_train` windows are used for training. The last of them". The paragraph that explains the gap between training and validation windows is correct but reads as bookkeeping; :numref:`fig_lang_model_data` could carry it (mark the dropped windows), and the prose could shrink to "we drop the `num_steps` windows that would let a validation token appear as a training target".

## Exercises

A strong and well-graded set. Exercise 7 (normalization across lengths, adapted from Jurafsky and Martin) is the best: it makes the reader discover that a model without an end token is not a distribution over sequences of all lengths, a point the text nowhere makes. Exercise 5 (bits per byte from perplexity) is the right check of the central definition. Exercise 3 (the $\alpha$ sweep) carries the claim of LM-8. Exercise 2c is hard to parse ("What does the gap say about the coverage of (b) as an estimate of how often a query on new text finds its 5-gram in the table?"); rewrite it as two plain questions.
