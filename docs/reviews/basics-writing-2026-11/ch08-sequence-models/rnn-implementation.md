# Implementing RNNs

Source: `chapter_recurrent-neural-networks/rnn-implementation.md`. About 4,400 words of prose in the source, of which the PyTorch reader sees about 3,800 (19 tab blocks carry framework notes), 1,050 words of exercises, 86 code cells across four tabs (about 30 for one reader), four plots.

## Verdict

The weakest section of the chapter, though sentence by sentence it is as cleanly written as the others. It is honest to a fault: it reports that the RNN ties a trigram, that its embeddings barely train, and that greedy text loops. But it tries to be an implementation walk-through, a training tutorial, a diagnosis of an optimizer pathology, a model comparison, a first look at generation, and a framework benchmark, and the reader comes out with no single thing learned. Its two central claims about training are not shown: that gradients explode without clipping, and that the hidden state carries useful context. The most important fix is to decide what the section's experiment is for (I suggest: show that the state uses context a trigram cannot, and that training needs clipping) and cut what does not serve it.

## What works

- **"The model fits in three equations"** and the statement of what stays fixed from step to step ("it returns $|\mathcal{V}|$ logits per sequence and a new state of the same shape as the old one"). A reader knows the shape of the code before reading it.
- **The initial perplexity as a check.** "The logits of the untrained model are then close to zero, its next-token distribution is close to uniform, and its perplexity is close to $|\mathcal{V}|$", and the stored output prints 1027. A prediction, then its confirmation.
- **The argument for clipping.** "Gradient descent works because, over a short step, the loss is close to its linear approximation ... When $\|\mathbf{g}\|$ spikes, the step leaves the region where that approximation holds", and "Shrinking $\eta$ until even the rare spike is safe would slow every step to protect against a few." This is why clipping exists, in four sentences, with the admission that "its effect on convergence is hard to analyze".
- **Reading the overfit.** "with 222,915 parameters for 50,000 training tokens, more than four parameters per token, the model has started to memorize its training text."
- **Re-teaching bits per byte at the moment of need.** "A perplexity of about 90 may look poor next to the perplexity of about 6 of the character-level trigram ... The two numbers cannot be compared".
- **A fair baseline.** The RNN is compared with Kneser-Ney on the same tokens of the same text, and the result is reported as it is: "The RNN alone comes within a few hundredths of a bit per byte of the Kneser–Ney trigram". Stored outputs agree in all four frameworks (2.11 or 2.12 against 2.09, mixture 2.03). Few textbooks would print that.
- **The mixture.** Interpolating two language models and observing that it beats both is a real idea with history behind it, in one short paragraph.

## Structural findings

- **Too many jobs** (IMP-S1). In order: scratch cell, embedding, output layer, clipping, data, plain model, embedding diagnosis, wider model, Kneser-Ney comparison, mixture, generation with temperature, concise implementation, untrained generation, retraining, timing comparison. The guide's test ("could anything be removed without losing a teachable point") removes: the one-hot check (IMP-4), the shape helpers (IMP-3), most of "Embeddings under SGD" (IMP-13), the temperature half of "Text Generation" (IMP-16), the untrained sample (IMP-17), and the timing subsection (IMP-18). That is roughly 900 words and a dozen cells per framework.
- **The payoff is missing** (IMP-S2). The section opens by asking "whether a hidden state predicts *The Time Machine* better than the counts". The answer is "about as well", which is fine, but nothing shows the hidden state doing what a window cannot. The evidence is one parenthesis away: "(The validation loss on the board averages over all 32 positions of a window, and the first of them see only one or a few tokens of context.)" Plot the validation loss against the position in the window. If the loss keeps falling past position 3, the reader sees the state using context that a trigram does not have, which is the claim of `rnn.md`. If it does not, the reader learns something true about this model. Either way it is the experiment the section needs, and it costs one cell.
- **Clipping is needed but not shown to be needed** (IMP-7).
- **The embedding detour and the wide model** (IMP-13). The section discovers that its own configuration (unit-scale embeddings, plain SGD, a loss averaged over 32,768 positions) leaves the embedding table nearly frozen, then works around it with a 512-dimensional embedding feeding a 128-unit cell, and twice tells the reader that Adam, from a chapter they have not read, is the real fix. This undercuts `rnn.md` ("A trainable table lets the model choose the vector of each token") and the promise of `language-model.md` that learned representations are where neural models win.
- **Order relative to `bptt.md`.** Clipping, truncation and detaching are each introduced here with a forward reference and explained again there. See the chapter file.
- **Tabs.** The PyTorch reader meets notes on two cuDNN bias vectors, transposed weight storage and device synchronization. These are true and belong in code comments (guide §9).

## Passage-level findings

- **IMP-1** (minor, language). Opening and throughout. Quote: "The code for these equations keeps one contract.". "Contract" appears six times ("keeps one contract", "implement this contract", "The contract asks for", "keeps the contract of", "Because the contract is unchanged", "keeps one contract"). It is a reasonable software word used as a refrain. Say "interface" once and then describe the thing: "each step takes a token and a state and returns logits and a state".
- **IMP-2** (minor, explanation). "The Recurrent Cell". Quote: "small, the recurrent weight has spectral radius about $\sigma\sqrt{h}". "Spectral radius" is defined in the next section. Here it arrives with a formula from random matrix theory and no gloss. Fix: "its largest eigenvalue has magnitude about $\sigma\sqrt{h} \approx 0.11$, so, as in the scalar example of :numref:`sec_rnn` with $w_\textrm{hh} = 0.11$, the untrained cell forgets its state within a few steps."
- **IMP-3** (minor, pedagogy). "The Recurrent Cell". Quote: "helpers below check lengths and shapes; later sections use them as well.". Two `#@save` helpers and a run on a tensor of ones to confirm that a loop over 100 steps returns 100 states. The shapes could be printed in one line.
- **IMP-4** (moderate, pedagogy). "The Embedding Table". Quote: "To confirm the equivalence claimed above, we check on a toy five-token". The equivalence of a one-hot product and a row lookup was stated in `rnn.md` ("Selecting a row is the same as multiplying a one-hot vector"), is stated again here in two paragraphs beginning "Older treatments bridged the gap", is checked here by a cell in four tabs, and is exercise 5 of `rnn.md`. Three tellings and two checks. Fix: one sentence here citing :numref:`sec_rnn`; no cell.
- **IMP-5** (moderate, explanation). "The Embedding Table" and "The Output Layer". Quotes: "def __init__(self, rnn, vocab_size, lr=0.01, dropout=0, emb_dropout=0):" and "`RNNLMScratch` already accepts the two dropout rates, which default to". The class signature and `forward` contain two dropout rates and two `F.dropout` calls that no sentence mentions until 250 lines later. A reader following the code line by line meets unexplained arguments in the first model. Fix: either say when the class is introduced "the two dropout rates are zero for now; we use them below", or add dropout by subclassing when it is needed.
- **IMP-6** (minor, pedagogy). "The Output Layer". Quote: "H = d2l.stack(list(rnn_outputs), 0)  # (num_steps, batch_size, num_hiddens)". `forward` stacks the list of states into a tensor along axis 0, and `output_layer` at once turns it back into a list and stacks along axis 1. The round trip exists so that `predict` and the concise subclass can reuse `output_layer`; to the reader it looks like a mistake. Fix: stack once and transpose, with a comment.
- **IMP-7** (major, thought). "Gradient Clipping". Quote: "gradient norm starts near 0.1, and within the first twenty or so updates it". The sentence goes on: "jumps severalfold from one update to the next, in most runs past 1 and in some past 10." None of this is on the page. No cell records a gradient norm; exercise 1 asks the reader to. The next section then opens "The RNN language model of :numref:`sec_rnn-scratch` trained only because we clipped its gradients", a claim that also rests on exercise 1. The guide says an experiment must show what the prose says it shows; here there is no experiment. Fix: record the gradient norm before clipping at every update of the plain model's first epoch and plot it on a log axis with the threshold drawn in. One short cell, and the most persuasive figure the section could have. If time allows, add one unclipped run to the loss plot.
- **IMP-8** (moderate, explanation). "A Plain Model". Quote: "ten epochs of minibatch SGD at learning rate 4, clipping gradients to norm". Every model in the book so far has trained with a learning rate between 0.01 and about 1. A rate of 4 is not explained. It matters for understanding clipping too: with $\theta = 1$ a clipped update has length exactly 4, so on those updates the method is normalized gradient descent. Fix: one sentence. "The loss is an average over 32,768 token positions and the gradient is correspondingly small (its norm starts near 0.1), so the learning rate is large; when clipping acts, the update has length $\eta\theta = 4$."
- **IMP-9** (minor, language). "Gradient Clipping". Quote: "$\mathbf{x}$ to $\mathbf{x} - \eta\mathbf{g}$, a distance". The parameters are called $\mathbf{x}$ in a chapter where $x_t$ is a token and $\mathbf{X}_t$ an input. Use $\mathbf{w}$ or $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ (and then rename the clipping threshold).
- **IMP-10** (minor, language). "Gradient Clipping", PyTorch tab. Quote: "does not force a synchronization with Python.". Plumbing in prose, echoed by the comment `# no `if` on the host`. A first-time reader does not know what is being synchronized. Move to a code comment or drop.
- **IMP-11** (moderate, thought). "Training". Quote: "resetting it. The carried state must then be detached from the computation". This paragraph (windows truncate the gradient, a variant carries the state, the state must be detached or the graph grows) is repeated nearly sentence for sentence under "Truncated Backpropagation Through Time" in the next section, which also has the figure. Fix: keep two sentences here ("every window starts from a zero state, so no gradient and no information crosses a window boundary; :numref:`sec_bptt` discusses what this gives up") and leave the variant to `bptt.md`.
- **IMP-12** (minor, pedagogy). "Training". Quote: "demonstrations but gives a model too little text (an exercise asks you to". The pipeline's default (10,000 windows, about 15% of the novella) is described as unsuitable in the first experiment that uses it. Change the default in `language-model.md` to the split used here and delete the apology.
- **IMP-13** (major, pedagogy). "Embeddings under SGD" and "A Wider Embedding with Dropout". Quotes: "The rows barely move." and "embedding helps even if its rows stay close to random: with $d = 512$" and "as an exercise shows with Adam.". The diagnosis is careful and correct (stored: rows move about 4% of their length). But consider what the first-time reader takes away: the embedding table, introduced one section ago as the place where a neural model learns what tokens mean, is in practice a fixed random projection; the remedy shown is to make that projection four times wider than the state; and the remedy that works is in chapter 9. This is a research note about one configuration, and it leaves the book's first neural language model with the least typical architecture in the chapter ($d = 512$, $h = 128$). The guide says: "If an experiment needs a paragraph of confounders before its result can be read, it is a research experiment, not a teaching one: simplify it or drop it." Options, in order of preference. (a) Fix the configuration so the embeddings train under SGD: sum the loss over positions or scale the embedding learning rate, start the rows at a smaller scale, and keep $d$ near $h$. Then the section can show something a reader wants to see, for instance the nearest neighbours of a few tokens after training. (b) Use Adam here as an unexplained tool, with one sentence ("an optimizer that gives each parameter its own step size; :numref:`sec_adam` explains it"). The part introduction already says Basics uses optimizers "as tools". (c) Keep SGD and the wide model but cut the subsection to three sentences and present dropout as the main change. Any of these removes the two "an exercise shows" hand-offs to Adam.
- **IMP-14** (moderate, explanation). "Comparison with Kneser–Ney". Quote: "validation tokens from the 33rd on, `val[32:]`, so that the RNN, below, can". About 200 words and three cells of index bookkeeping (`val[32:]`, "validation window $j$ starts at `val[j]`, so its last target is `val[j + 32]`", `data.Y[start:stop, -1]`) stand between the reader and a three-line result. The idea is simple: score the same tokens with both models, giving the RNN a full window before each. Fix: put the alignment in one helper with a docstring, and say the idea in one sentence.
- **IMP-15** (minor, thought). "Comparison with Kneser–Ney". Quote: "Kneser–Ney trigram, and the mixture beats both by a clear margin, because". Two issues. The RNN's gap to the trigram (0.02 bits) is "a few hundredths" and the mixture's gain (0.06 bits) is "a clear margin"; the second is three times the first, not a different kind of thing. And "because the two models are confident on different tokens" is asserted. One line would show it: the fraction of tokens on which each model assigns the higher probability, or the correlation of their per-token log-probabilities.
- **IMP-16** (moderate, pedagogy). "Text Generation". Quote: "Otherwise it *samples* token $i$ with probability proportional to". Greedy decoding, temperature, the looping of greedy text and the invented words of sampled text are all introduced here, with outputs, and then introduced again two sections later in `decoding.md`, which runs the same temperature sweep on the same prefix. Fix: here, generate greedily only (the loop "I had a falling. I had a falling." is the right cliffhanger), end with the question of how to choose tokens better, and let `decoding.md` own temperature. `predict` loses a branch.
- **IMP-17** (minor, pedagogy). "Concise Implementation". Quote: "Before training, we let the untrained model continue a prefix. The". Twenty random tokens with replacement characters, to "check the wiring". Cut.
- **IMP-18** (moderate, pedagogy). "Scratch and Concise Implementations Compared". Quote: "The framework layer is two to three times faster. `nn.RNN` runs the whole". A subsection, a table and four tab-specific paragraphs on kernel launch overhead, cuDNN fusion, `tf.function` graphs and XLA unrolling. The four tabs reach different conclusions (TensorFlow: "which one finishes first varies from run to run"; stored TensorFlow times are 36.1 s and 37.7 s). This is the material the guide assigns to the Computation and Performance chapters. Fix: keep one shared sentence ("the framework layer runs the same recurrence in a single optimized call, so the rest of the book trains with it") and the perplexity check that the two implementations agree.
- **IMP-19** (minor, language). Summary. Quote: "move, and the plain model overfits, reaching 2.3 to 2.4 bits per byte. A wider". The summary is 180 words and replays the section's numbers. Three sentences would do: the model in three equations, clipping and truncation as the two training devices, the result against Kneser-Ney.

## Exercises

Well constructed, and several are better than the text they follow. Exercise 2 (usable history: reset the state every 32 tokens or carry it across 512) is the experiment that tests whether the state generalizes beyond its training horizon, and is the kind of thing the section itself should show (IMP-S2). Exercise 1 carries the evidence for clipping that the text asserts (IMP-7); part (c), ReLU with and without clipping, is a good probe of why $\tanh$ matters. Exercise 4 (Adam) carries the claim of IMP-13. Exercise 7 (weight tying) is well designed, including the trap about the initial perplexity. Exercise 3a ("adjust the number of epochs, hidden units, ... to lower the validation perplexity") is the generic tuning exercise and could go. Exercise 6 spends more words on how to split the data than on the question.
