# Working with Sequences

Source: `chapter_recurrent-neural-networks/sequence.md`. About 2,700 words of prose (nearly all shared across tabs), 500 words of exercises, one schematic figure, five plots.

## Verdict

Good textbook prose, and the cleanest statement of autoregressive modelling in the chapter: the chain-rule passage is at the level of Cover and Thomas. The experiment is honestly analysed, down to a closed-form prediction of the error that matches the printed numbers. The problem is that the experiment does not show the lesson the section draws from it. The headline is that errors accumulate when a model consumes its own predictions; what the sine wave shows is a model that averages, damps whatever is fed back, and is wrong at long horizons because the one-step loss never asked it to learn the curve. The text half-admits this and then states the accumulation lesson anyway. Fix that mismatch first.

## What works

- **The opening.** "Thank you for your" against "your thank for you" shows in six words that order matters, and the second paragraph says what the section will do in plain terms ("turns sequence modeling into a problem we already know how to solve, regression").
- **"From Conditionals to Sequences".** Meaning before formula, the formula, then "The factorization is exact: it holds for every distribution over sequences. Thus a model of the next entry is a model of the entire sequence.", then a three-word example with numbers (0.05, 0.01, 0.2, product $10^{-4}$), then the two uses (score, generate). This is the Cover and Thomas rhythm and the reviser should not touch it. October marked it "keep"; it is still the best paragraph of the section.
- **Reading the weights.** After the rollout fails, the text prints the four weights (stored output: 0.223, 0.234, 0.257, 0.260) and explains the failure from them: "the model has learned to predict the next value as the average of the last four." The variance argument ($0.04/4 = 0.01$) and the fixed point $b/(1-\sum_i w_i)$ are correct and satisfying.
- **The error budget.** The prediction "$0.05$, $0.05$, $0.065$, and $0.25$" from $1-\cos(0.01k)$ sits next to the measured 0.050, 0.055, 0.075, 0.270. A reader sees a formula earn its keep. The prose claim "four to six times the one-step error" survives re-execution (stored ratio 5.4).
- **Markov condition.** "Even large language models read a context window of fixed length, not everything that came before it" ties an old assumption to a system the reader knows.

## Structural findings

- **The experiment and its lesson diverge** (SEQ-1). This is the main finding.
- **No reason is given for wanting $P(x_1,\ldots,x_T)$** (SEQ-2). The section moves from prediction tasks to density estimation in one sentence.
- **The left-to-right paragraph sits under "Stationarity"** (SEQ-5), where it does not belong.
- The section does three things (define the two strategies, state the assumptions, run the experiment) and they hold together. Length is right.

## Passage-level findings

- **SEQ-1** (major, thought). "Error Growth with the Horizon" and Summary. Quotes: "in a rollout every prediction" and "so it can never correct its errors. On the noisy sine wave, the". The section's own analysis shows that the fitted model does not accumulate error: "The averaging model damps whatever is fed back into it, so its rollout drifts toward a constant instead of diverging." The $k$-step plot is not accumulation either: each 64-step forecast starts from four observed values and is "a copy of the series delayed by about 64 steps", so its error is the distance the sine travels, not compounded mistakes. The next paragraph then says "The underlying problem is general, though" and asserts the accumulation story for language models and simulators with no evidence on the page. A careful reader notices that the claim and the experiment have come apart. Fix, either of two ways. (a) Make the demonstrated lesson the headline: a model trained on a one-step loss learns only what one step rewards ("The model has not learned the curvature of the sine, which the one-step loss barely rewarded" is already the right sentence), and used many steps ahead it is as good as its dynamics, not as good as its one-step error. Rename the subsection "Forecast Error and Horizon" and rewrite the summary sentence to: "A model that predicts one step well can still be a poor model of the series: on the noisy sine the averaging model reaches a one-step error near the noise floor, and 64 steps ahead its error is four to six times larger." (b) Keep accumulation as the lesson and show it: add the second model that exercise 3 asks for (an MLP, or a linear model with a window long enough to continue the sine) and show a rollout whose error grows because early mistakes are fed back. Option (a) costs nothing and is true to the data.
- **SEQ-2** (moderate, pedagogy). "Sequential Data". Quote: "Before handling targets of any kind, we model the sequence itself:". The reader has just been told about sentiment labels, captions and translation, and is then told the section will estimate how probable a sequence is, with no reason. Why would anyone want that number? Fix: one or two sentences before the claim: a model of the sequence itself needs no labels (every position supplies its own target), it can generate, and the other tasks can be built on it. The speech example from the opening of `language-model.md` would serve here too.
- **SEQ-3** (minor, language). "Fixed Windows". Quote: "This is *autoregression* in its narrow sense, and $m$ is the". The section calls every model that regresses a signal on its past "autoregressive", then reserves "autoregression" for the window, and calls the state model "latent autoregression". The figure and the summary repeat the split. Later chapters call recurrent and transformer language models simply autoregressive. Fix: say once that both strategies are autoregressive, and name them by what differs: a window model and a state (latent) model.
- **SEQ-4** (moderate, correctness). "Sequential Data" and "Stationarity". Quotes: "We do not even assume that the entries share one distribution." and "Dynamics with this property are called *stationary*.". The first sentence drops the assumption that entries are identically distributed; three screens later the text requires the conditional rule to be the same at all times and calls that stationarity. The two are compatible (a fixed transition rule can produce changing marginals), and the text says "The values still change, sometimes dramatically", but it never connects the two statements, and a reader who knows the term will object: a stationary process is one whose joint distribution is invariant under shifts, and what is defined here is a time-homogeneous rule. The sine series itself is not stationary in the usual sense. Fix: "We assume the rule is the same at every step (the dynamics are *time-homogeneous*, often loosely called stationary). This does not make the entries identically distributed: a document still reads differently at its end, because the context the rule is applied to has changed."
- **SEQ-5** (minor, pedagogy). "Stationarity", last paragraph. Quote: "One choice above was implicit: we factored the sequence left to right.". A remark about the chain rule placed under a heading about stationarity, two subsections after the chain rule. Fix: move it to the end of "From Conditionals to Sequences".
- **SEQ-6** (minor, explanation). "Multistep Rollout". Quote: "(the 600 training examples need the first 604 observations)". The reader has to do index arithmetic (600 examples, window 4, hence 604, hence predict 605 to 609) before meeting the idea of a rollout. Fix: say "Suppose observations stop at some step $t$" and write the five lines with $t+1,\ldots,t+5$; mention 604 only where the code starts its loop.
- **SEQ-7** (moderate, pedagogy). "Multistep Rollout", plot. Quote: "legend=['1-step preds', 'multistep preds'], figsize=(6, 3))". The prose says "The rollout never follows the oscillation", but the plot does not show the series, only the one-step predictions as a stand-in. A reader looking at the rendered figure compares a dashed line with a noisy blue curve labelled "1-step preds" and has to infer that this is nearly the data. Fix: plot the observed series (thin, grey) with the rollout on top, and drop the one-step curve from this figure; it was shown in the previous one.
- **SEQ-8** (moderate, thought). "Error Growth with the Horizon", last paragraph. Quote: "Two remedies exist.". The first "remedy" is the decoding rule, which does not address the gap between one-step training and multistep use; it changes which token is fed back. The second (training on the model's own predictions) is stated as a fact, "narrows the gap", and exercise 7 then asks the reader to "Test the claim". A claim the book has not tested should not be stated flatly. Fix: "Two lines of work address this. The rule that picks each next value can avoid the choices that derail a rollout (:numref:`sec_decoding`). And a model can be trained on its own predictions as well as on observed histories; exercise ... tests whether that helps here."
- **SEQ-9** (moderate, pedagogy). "Multistep Rollout". Quote: "A linear model *could* continue a sine wave far better;". The most interesting fact in the section is handed to an exercise: a noise-free sine satisfies $x_t = 2\cos(0.01)\,x_{t-1} - x_{t-2}$, so two lags suffice and a linear model can continue it for ever. The reader has just watched a linear model fail and is told that it need not have, without being told why. Fix: give the identity in one sentence and leave to the exercise why noise pushes the fit toward averaging and how many lags are needed in practice.
- **SEQ-10** (minor, explanation). "Fixed Windows". Quote: "A window is simple and easy to parallelize.". "Parallelize" has no referent yet (parallel over what?). Fix: "Every prediction depends only on its own window, so all of them can be computed at once."

## Exercises

A good set, graded from a parameter sweep to scheduled sampling. Exercise 1b (how many lags suffice without noise) is the best and its answer belongs partly in the text (SEQ-9). Exercise 2 (momentum strategies) is the weakest: it asks for an "assumption about the return series" in the vocabulary of two subsections and has a thin connection to anything the section computed; either cut it or tie it to the stationarity of the sine experiment. Exercise 6 (regime switching) is a real test of the stationarity paragraph. Exercise 7 carries a claim of the main text (SEQ-8).
