# From Text to Tokens

Source: `chapter_recurrent-neural-networks/text-sequence.md`. About 3,350 words of prose (all shared across tabs; the only tabbed cells are the imports), 700 words of exercises, three schematic figures, one plot, 34 code cells.

## Verdict

A strong section with a real payoff: a tokenizer short enough to read, traced by hand on eight words, then shown to reproduce GPT-2's tokenizer token for token. Few textbooks give the reader that moment. The prose is plain and the examples (Greek at seven tokens a word, "12345678" split two ways) are well chosen. Its faults are of two kinds. The teaching code carries speed tricks and forward-declared arguments that make the central loop harder to read than the algorithm is. And the section is interrupted by a legacy character and word pipeline (`Vocab`, `build`) that is justified three times by "later sections use it" and reintroduces the unknown-token problem the reader has just seen solved. Fix the second first: it is what makes the section feel long.

## What works

- **The hand trace.** "hug pug pun bun hugs hug hug pug", 32 bytes, three merges, with the table showing counts and corpus length (32, 26, 22, 19; all correct) and the ties named and resolved. Then "Training on our toy corpus reproduces the hand trace" and the printed merges match. This is the smallest case solved completely, as the guide asks.
- **Two properties read off the trace.** "merges compose" and "the third merge already glued a word to the *space that follows it*". The second plants the problem that pre-tokenization solves forty lines later.
- **The prefix property.** "a BPE tokenizer with a smaller vocabulary is a *prefix* of a larger one trained on the same corpus", so "A single training run therefore sweeps the vocabulary size from 256 to 4,096." An idea that is both elegant and saves compute.
- **The GPT-2 check.** Loading GPT-2's rank table into the reader's own encoder and asserting equality on a paragraph with accents, digits and an emoji. "GPT-2's first four merges, learned from 40 gigabytes of web text, are " t", " a", "he", and "in", the same four pairs that `bpe` learned first from one novella." That sentence is the kind of fact a reader repeats to a colleague.
- **Fertility with numbers.** "A 1,024-token context window therefore holds about 140 Greek words against about 900 English ones." (1024/7.33 and 1024/1.14: both right.)
- **Special tokens as a safety property.** "encoding ordinary text must never produce a special token" is a better reason than most texts give.

## Structural findings

- **The classical pipeline interrupts the BPE story.** "Vocabularies and Special Tokens" opens with a `Vocab` class of forty lines for character and word tokens, placed after BPE has been built and trained. Its justification is that other sections need it: "which some later sections still use", "several later sections reuse it" (said earlier of the normalization step), "several later sections call it". Within this chapter `Vocab` is used once, in `language-model.md`, to sort words by frequency, which `collections.Counter.most_common` does in one line. The reader has just learned that byte-level BPE "eliminates" out-of-vocabulary tokens and is then taught `<unk>`. Fix: move "Characters and Words", `_tokenize`, `Vocab` and `build` together into one subsection before "Text as Bytes" (they are the "before" picture), say once that this is the pipeline older chapters of the book use, and keep it out of the BPE arc. Or move `Vocab` and `build` out of the chapter to the first section that needs them.
- **"Tokenizers in the Wild" is two things.** The rank table and the verification are the section's payoff and must stay. "Fertility, Digits, and Glitch Tokens" is a tour of consequences; fertility and digits earn their place because they support the opening claim that tokenization decides sequence length and affects arithmetic. The glitch-token paragraph is an anecdote with a one-line cell and could move to the Language Models part (October's suggestion for the whole of this material) at no loss here.
- **Special tokens are defined and never used in the chapter.** `<pad>`, `<bos>` and `<eos>` add three ids (1,024 becomes 1,027, a number the next four sections carry), none of which occurs in the training text. The one named consumer is a section in the Attic (TOK-10).
- **Zipf's law is absent here** although the opening paragraph's trade-off (vocabulary size against sequence length) is driven by it; it arrives in `language-model.md`. That is acceptable, but one forward sentence under "Characters and Words" ("the vocabulary keeps growing with the corpus" is where it would attach) would help.

## Passage-level findings

- **TOK-1** (minor, thought). Opening. Quote: "model's output layer, the length of every document it reads, and even which". "Which arithmetic problems it can solve" overstates what the section later shows, which is that misaligned digit chunks are "one reason why language models make arithmetic errors". Fix: "and even how hard arithmetic is for it".
- **TOK-2** (moderate, pedagogy). "Implementation", `_merge` and `train`. Quotes: "try:  # list.index scans for the next candidate at C speed" and "if w == 1:  # Counter.update counts at C speed". The one operation the whole tokenizer rests on (replace each adjacent pair by its merged id) is written as a `try`/`except` around `list.index` with three branches, for speed. The plain version is a six-line `while` loop that reads like the sentence that introduces it. In `train`, the `w == 1` branch exists only as an optimization. Code in this book is supposed to teach; here the reader must reverse-engineer an optimization to see a simple idea. Fix: show the plain loop in the text; keep the fast version in the saved library if build time needs it, or make the speed-up an exercise.
- **TOK-3** (minor, explanation). "Implementation". Quotes: "both defaults are harmless for now. We import `regex`, an extension of" and "self.byte_ids = list(range(256))                  # byte value -> id". The first class cell carries a regex constant, a `pattern`, three special tokens with properties, `_chunks`, and an identity table `byte_ids` whose purpose appears 400 lines later in `from_tiktoken`. The reader is asked to ignore half of the first cell. Fix: introduce the class with `merges` and `vocab` only; add `pattern` and `_chunks` under "Pre-Tokenization", the specials under "Special Tokens", and `byte_ids` where GPT-2's byte permutation is explained. `add_to_class` exists for this.
- **TOK-4** (minor, thought). "The Merge Rule". Quote: "across word boundaries. On a real corpus this becomes a problem, which we". The toy's `'hug '` is a word plus its trailing space. The later test for a boundary-crossing token (`b' ' in v.strip(b' ')`) would not count it, and the cure adopted later attaches the space to the front of the word instead. The real problem is tokens such as "of the ". Fix: say that the trailing space is harmless in itself and is the first step toward merges that span two words, which is what the measurement later finds (78 of them).
- **TOK-5** (minor, thought). "Encoding and Decoding". Quote: "table. **The tokenizer is defined by its merge ranks**, and training and". "Agree by construction" asserts what the starred exercise 6 asks the reader to argue and exercise 1 shows is not obvious (the rank rule and the leftmost rule differ). Fix: give the reason in a sentence (a merge learned at rank $r$ was applied to text already merged at all lower ranks, so replaying in rank order retraces training), or write "agree, as exercise ... shows".
- **TOK-6** (moderate, thought). "Training on The Time Machine". Quote: "therefore not much further along the curve: on English prose they reach". Two problems. The "therefore" does not follow: logarithmic growth extrapolated from the plot to 50,000 tokens would predict seven or more bytes per token, not four. And the plot itself reaches about 4.6 bytes per token at a vocabulary of 4,096, above the 4.06 measured for GPT-2 two subsections later, so the reader sees a 4,096-token vocabulary beat a 50,257-token one and is given the reason ("In-domain text flatters the larger vocabularies") only as an aside before the plot. Fix: measure the curve on held-out text (exercise 7 already sets up the split), or state plainly after the plot that its upper end reflects memorized words of this novella and that a general-purpose vocabulary needs its extra entries for text it was not tuned to.
- **TOK-7** (minor, language). "Verifying Our Implementation". Quote: "50 and 200 times its size, because it was trained on this text. A". Read literally, the tokenizer reaches only two thirds because it was trained on this text. Fix: "Although it is 50 to 200 times smaller, our tokenizer reaches two thirds of their compression, because it was trained on this text."
- **TOK-8** (moderate, explanation). "Verifying Our Implementation". Quote: "The two parents of each longer token are recovered". This sentence and the twenty-line `from_tiktoken` are the hardest passage in the section, and they serve a check, not an idea. A reader can accept "GPT-2 stores bytes and ranks, not pairs, so we reconstruct the pairs" without the inner loop. Fix: either add a three-line example (which two tokens merge into " the" and why the lowest-rank rule finds them), or present the method as a utility the reader may skip and keep the weight on the `assert`.
- **TOK-9** (minor, thought). "Text as Bytes". Quote: "English text takes about". The subsection ends "Everything that follows keeps the universality of bytes while shortening the sequences", a good hand-off, but the cost of bytes is never quantified for the running corpus. One number (bytes of the raw novella against its 32,775 words) would let the reader place bytes on the same scale as the characters-versus-words table.
- **TOK-10** (minor, explanation). "Special Tokens". Quote: "encoder-decoder models of :numref:`sec_machine_translation` will use all". The only stated use of the three special tokens is a section that now sits in the Attic part. A front-to-back reader will not reach it. Fix: name a use in this chapter (`<eos>` lets beam search and `generate` stop, :numref:`sec_decoding`) or in the Language Models part.
- **TOK-11** (minor, explanation). "Special Tokens". Quote: "a form of the *prompt injection* attacks on deployed language models. Our". A new term used as if known. One clause would do: "attacks in which text supplied by a user is read by the model as an instruction".

## Exercises

Among the best in the chapter. Exercise 1 (rank rule against leftmost rule on "abc") and exercise 6 (why rank replay reproduces training) form a pair that tests the one subtle idea of the section. Exercise 2 (cross-corpus compression) and exercise 7 (pre-tokenization ablation on held-out text) make the reader measure what the text asserts about in-domain compression. Exercise 4 (digit chunking and place value) is concrete and answerable. Exercise 3 is long for what it tests; its part (c) is the valuable one.
