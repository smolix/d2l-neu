# Matrices as Linear Maps

Source: `chapter_mdl-linear-algebra/mdl-linear-maps.md`, read to the slides marker. About 8,400 words of prose and mathematics, 13 distinct code cells, 10 exercises (about 1,700 words).

## Verdict

The first half of this section (columns, composition, column space and null space, rank) is clear, well paced and at the level of a good textbook: the opening makes the reader want to know whether a map can be undone, and two small matrices, one that can and one that cannot, carry the whole argument. The second half is correct but is organized around the logic of the proofs and not around the reader. Results are announced, postponed and "settled" two or three subsections later; the determinant gets an axiomatic development with cofactor expansion while its one use in deep learning, the change of variables for densities that the opening promises, never appears in the body; and a subsection on `einsum` sits between the determinant theorem and the determinant's limits. I found no false statement. I found three places where the argument has a hole in its order (uniqueness used before it is derived, existence asserted without saying it is not proved, two basis facts used silently). The single most important fix is to reorder the second half so that each claim is proved where it is made: the invertibility of a square matrix with independent columns needs only rank–nullity and belongs in "Inverses", after which the forward references disappear.

## What works

- **The opening.** A $256 \times 784$ layer "sends at least $784 - 256 = 528$ independent input directions to zero, so different images produce the same features before any nonlinearity sees them." Then the two matrices: $\mathbf{A}$ sends $(2,-1)$ to $(0,-5)$ and can be undone; $\mathbf{B}$ "sends both $(1, 1)$ and $(0, 3)$ to $(3, 6)$. Seeing the output $(3, 6)$, we cannot tell which of the two inputs we started from". The reader has the whole section in two examples before any definition.
- **"Columns as Images of the Axes"**: "A matrix acts on infinitely many inputs, yet it is a finite table of numbers." The derivation, the running example, the row view giving the same numbers, and the converse (every linear map is a matrix) in five lines. Nothing to change.
- **Composition explains the product.** "The product is row-times-column because that is what the composite of two linear maps computes. It is associative ... because composing functions is associative ... And it is not commutative, because composing two functions in the other order generally gives another function." This is the explanation most courses never give. The shear-then-rotate example is computed by following the axes, before any formula.
- **Column space and null space from one collision.** Two inputs collide, "By linearity their difference $(1, -2)$ goes to the difference of the outputs", and "The row view shows why: both rows ... are perpendicular to $(1, -2)$, so both hyperplane tests return zero." The link back to the previous section's row view is exact.
- **The row-rank proof** by the factorization $\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{G}\mathbf{H}$ is the short proof, and the sentence after it is the best in the section: "a matrix of rank $r$ first computes $r$ numbers from its input (the map $\mathbf{H}$) and then spreads them over the $m$ outputs (the map $\mathbf{G}$)."
- **Area before determinant.** Counting triangles ($12 - 7 = 5$), then base times height, then "The lengths cancel, and what remains is a polynomial in the four entries. No case distinction on signs was needed." The three properties are each glossed as a fact about volume, and the $2 \times 2$ expansion recovers $ad - bc$.
- **Multiplicativity**: the tiling intuition first, then the three-line proof through uniqueness. This is the right proof.
- **The Hilbert-matrix cell** separates residual from error, which most treatments blur, and **"Limits of the Determinant"** has two sharp examples ($0.1\,\mathbf{I}_{400}$ with determinant $10^{-400}$; the shear with determinant 1 that is $10^{-8}$ from singular) and a surprise in the output: "Yet `matrix_rank` reports rank one for both."
- **The last paragraph** connects the running matrix to the next two sections with numbers the reader can check: eigenvalues $2 \pm i$ with product 5, singular values 3.618 and 1.382 with product 5 (verified).
- **Einstein notation**: "The attention scores show the limit of this classical rule: the indices $b$ and $h$ also appear in both factors, but they are kept, not summed." A precise remark that explains why `einsum` needs the arrow.
- All worked numbers and all stored outputs agree with the prose (I recomputed the triangles, $\mathbf{A}^2$, the null vectors and row relations of $\mathbf{C}$, the $169^\circ$, the cofactor expansion, the rank threshold $4\cdot10^{-8}$, and the exercise numbers).

## Structural findings

1. **A chain of promises in the second half.** Count of forward references to a later subsection of the same section for a proof or a definition: nine. "This follows from a general fact about square matrices, shown in" (Orthogonal Matrices, pointing to Inverses); "shows that the sign of the determinant tells the two kinds apart" (same subsection, pointing to Multiplicativity); "which ... defines as a signed area" and "The theorem of ... shows that the hypothesis" (Inverses, pointing two and five subsections ahead); "so once we know that $\mathbf{Q}$ has some right inverse" (Inverses); "we state this here and prove it in" (Inverses); "it is proved in" (Area of a Parallelogram); "We show below" and "that we use later" (The Determinant in General); and finally "The proof also settles the two claims left open in" (Invertibility and the Determinant). The fact $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^\top = \mathbf{I}$ for a square orthogonal matrix is promised in one subsection, half-proved in a second and completed in a third, 740 lines after it was stated. The cause is one decision: the theorem "independent columns imply invertible" is filed under the determinant, although its proof (step (iii) to (ii) of the last proposition) uses only rank–nullity and the null space. See MAP-9.

2. **The determinant's argument is interrupted, and its ending is in another section.** The order is: Area, General, Multiplicativity, Invertibility, then a `##` section "Tensors and Einstein Summation", then a `##` section "Limits of the Determinant", which is at once the end of the determinant story and the closing of the whole section. `einsum` has nothing to do with either neighbour. Proposed order: move the Einstein-notation material to follow "Composition" (the index formula :eqref:`eq_mdl-composition` is already there, and the trace can be defined with it), or make it the last `##` before the Summary; make "Limits of the Determinant" the last `###` of "The Determinant". See MAP-17.

3. **The determinant is developed past its use, and its use is missing.** The opening says that invertibility decides "how a probability density changes under a change of variables". The body never returns to it; the only appearance is the last-but-one exercise (planar flows). A deep learning reader needs four things about determinants: the volume factor, multiplicativity, the triangular rule (the reason autoregressive and coupling flows are cheap), and the log-determinant (which the last cell computes with `slogdet` without saying what it is for). The section instead spends about 900 words on the permutation formula as a uniqueness proof, cofactor expansion, and a $3 \times 3$ hand computation. The part introduction promises results "developed far enough to be useful and no further". Recommendation: keep the three properties, the swap rule, the $2 \times 2$ expansion, the triangular rule, the transpose rule and multiplicativity; state the permutation formula and say candidly what is not proved; cut cofactor expansion to two sentences; and add one paragraph, after multiplicativity, on the change of variables: a linear map $\mathbf{z} \mapsto \mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}$ divides a density by $|\det\mathbf{A}|$, flows therefore need $\log|\det|$ of each layer, and layers are designed with triangular Jacobians so that this is a sum of $n$ logarithms (with a reference to the change-of-variables formula in the calculus chapter and to the flows of the dynamics chapter).

4. **Many cells confirm arithmetic the paragraph above has just done.** Nine of the thirteen cells compute a $2 \times 2$ or $3 \times 3$ product, inverse or determinant whose value is printed in the prose immediately before, and are read off with "The output lists ...", "We confirm the three images", "Up to rounding, the values are ...". They are harmless one at a time, and they obey the rule that code computes and does not draw, but they teach nothing the hand computation has not taught, and they set a rhythm (claim, cell, "as computed") that makes the middle of the section drag. The cells that earn their place are the ones whose output the reader could not predict: the rank of $\mathbf{C}$ with its null vectors, the Hilbert system, the attention scores, and the limits cell. Deferred (code changes): merge the confirmations of each `##` into one cell, or drop the weakest (columns of $\mathbf{A}$, the three images under $\mathbf{B}$, the $2 \times 2$ inverse).

5. **Length.** 8,400 words is nearly twice the guide's ceiling. With finding 3 (about 500 words net), the removal of duplicated statements (MAP-11, MAP-13, MAP-16, about 300 words) and the recap at the head of "Limits" (about 80 words), the section comes to about 7,500 with a stronger argument. I would not split it: rank, invertibility and determinant are one question, as the opening says.

6. **Missing for a lookup reader.** The trace is defined in a subordinate clause inside the `einsum` section (MAP-17). "Singular" is used before it is defined (MAP-10). Elimination is mentioned as what one does by hand and is taught nowhere in the chapter (MAP-8).

**MAP-14** (moderate, correctness: order and an unproved claim not marked as such, sure). "The Determinant in General".
Anchor: `Uniqueness comes in a sharper form that we use later: *every* function $f$ of`

## For the chapter review

- The section depends on the previous one for span, independence, basis, dimension, projection and the row view, and uses them correctly. It sets up the next two with care: the commuting-with-rotation exercise and the last paragraph prepare $2 \pm i$; the shear prepares the defective case; `matrix_rank` and the Hilbert matrix prepare singular values and the condition number, both of which are used here before they are defined (with references).
- The chapter's weakest stretch in my half is the determinant: roughly 3,000 words, more than any other topic in the two sections, for the object deep learning uses least. Its good parts (area, the three properties, multiplicativity, limits) should be kept, and the change of variables added.
- Pattern across both sections: closings in the form "We can now ..."; the same fact told in the subsection that poses it, the one that proves it, and the summary; cells that re-run arithmetic done by hand. The second pattern is a consequence of proving things late.
- Pattern specific to this section and worth checking in the eigenvalue and SVD sections: forward references inside a section used as a substitute for ordering ("we state this here and prove it in", "settles the two claims left open").
- No error of mathematics in either section. The correctness findings are gaps of order or of unstated assumptions (GEO-8, GEO-16, MAP-6, MAP-14, MAP-15).
- The label prefixes of this section's exercises and several cells still read `geometry-linear-algebraic-ops`, a trace of the split from the first section. This is invisible to readers and noted only for whoever renames things.
