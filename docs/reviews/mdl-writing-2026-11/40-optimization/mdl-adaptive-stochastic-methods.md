# Adaptive Methods

Source: `chapter_mdl-optimization/mdl-adaptive-stochastic-methods.md` (read to the slides marker, line 1257). About 6,500 words of prose and mathematics in the body, 2,000 more in eleven exercises; nine code cells, all NumPy.

## Verdict

This section has the best idea of the two I read and executes most of it very well. It does not retell the optimizers; it asks what dividing by a gradient scale *is*, proves the answer as a symmetry (Adam's iterates do not change when each coordinate's gradient is rescaled), and then spends its strongest pages on where the symmetry ends: a rotation by $45^\circ$, a three-gradient convex sequence, weight decay passed through the division, and the fixed points of AdamW. Those four passages are above ordinary textbook level, and the rotated-valley analysis (the run ends in a two-cycle with $f = 250\,\eta^2$) and the fixed-point proposition are things a reader will not find in the standard texts. I recomputed the numbers quoted without a cell (40, 48 and 54 steps; the minimum $6 \times 10^{-5}$ near $t = 2{,}600$; the final $f = 2.5$; the 620 steps after retuning; the peak $6.6$ at $t = 12$; the Zipf ratio $0.64$; the periodic values of the counterexample) and all are right. I found no error in the three proofs. The most important thing to fix is the clarity of thought at three points where the experiment and the claim do not line up: the headline "48 steps" compares optimally tuned gradient descent and heavy ball with an Adam whose step size is hand-picked and whose count depends on the starting distance, not only on the absence of $\kappa$; the warmup subsection opens by blaming the unestimated preconditioner and then shows by its own experiment that the preconditioner is not the cause; and the structured preconditioners are promised as the answer to the rotated valley and never applied to it.

## What works

- **The opening** (lines 4 to 38) starts from what the reader does (trains with AdamW), states the problem in one image ("the stiffest coordinate sets the step and the others crawl"), and gives three numbers for one valley. Its question has a second half, "and when that choice fails", which the section honours.
- **"A Diagonal Quadratic."** Twenty lines take the reader from "nothing forces the two coordinates to share a step" to Newton for a diagonal Hessian to the two obstacles (curvature unknown, gradients noisy) and so to "The available estimate is the size of the gradient." This is the motivation chapter 9 gives in one sentence, done properly, and it is honest about its premise ("When the coordinates have comparable magnitudes").
- **The scale-invariance proposition and its use.** A four-line induction, then the consequence that Adam on the $1000{:}1$ valley "follows, step for step, the path of Adam on the round bowl", then the observation that "Only the stopping test depends on the ratio". The cell that follows confirms the prediction to rounding error ($|x_1 - x_2| \le 5.6 \times 10^{-17}$). Predict, run, confirm: the book's own method at its best.
- **The rotated valley.** The explanation of why a diagonal cannot help ("A per-coordinate scale is one number per coordinate, so it cannot divide the stiff and the flat component of the same coordinate by different amounts") is exact and plain, and the account of what the run then does (effective step grows to the stability limit, the stiff mode starts to oscillate, the update becomes a sign step, the loss parks at $250\,\eta^2$) is the kind of worked dynamics MacKay would have enjoyed writing. The bold sentence that states the conclusion is earned.
- **The counterexample made visible.** "The mechanism is visible in one period": three displacements, $-1.03\,\eta$, $+0.74\,\eta$, $+0.98\,\eta$, against SGD's $-\eta$, $+0.25\,\eta$, $+0.25\,\eta$. A reader understands in six lines what the paper's induction hides. The closing judgment is right and should stay: "It does not predict failure on ordinary training problems."
- **"The gain therefore comes from concentration of the gradient mass, not from sparsity as such."** A precise correction of the usual folklore, backed by two numbers ($1.00$ for uniform one-hot gradients, $0.64$ for Zipf).
- **Coupled and decoupled decay, both regimes.** The noise experiment predicts three of its four numbers, and where the fourth misses ($0.03$ measured, $0.018$ predicted) the text says why. The fixed-point proposition then shows the other side, with a one-dimensional example whose four values ($2.4$, $3.0$, $1.5$, $1.0$) can be checked by hand.
- **Sentences worth protecting:** "a plateau met late is crossed with almost no step left"; "the curvature ratio recovered from gradient sizes alone, with no Hessian"; "Uncorrected Adam takes its largest steps when its preconditioner rests on the fewest gradients" (in the figure caption).
- **The last paragraph's candour:** "why these methods work as well as they do on deep networks is an open question."

## Structural findings

**S1. The headline comparison is confounded.** The opening and the table under "Aligned and Rotated Valleys" set gradient descent at its optimal step (6,160) and heavy ball at its optimal step and momentum (296) against Adam with $\beta_1 = 0$ at $\eta = 0.1$ (48). Nothing says how $0.1$ was chosen. It matters: with the same code, $\eta = 0.3$ gives 14 steps and $\eta = 1$ gives 1 step (the first Adam step is $\eta\,\mathrm{sign}(\mathbf{g})$, which from $(1, 1)$ lands on the minimizer), while $\eta = 0.03$ gives 166. Adam's count is set by the starting distance in units of $\eta$, since its first steps have length about $\eta$ per coordinate whatever the curvature; started at $(100, 100)$ the same optimizer needs 4,145 steps (I ran it), 86 times as many, while gradient descent needs 8,462, about a third more. So "six times fewer than the tuned heavy ball's 296" ranks a tuned method against an untuned one on a quantity that depends on the start. The true and sufficient claim is the one the text makes later: "The ratio costs a few steps of a geometric contraction, not a factor $\kappa$." Direction (prose only): in the opening and under the table, say that $\eta = 0.1$ is one tenth of the starting distance, that the count scales with distance$/\eta$ and not with $\kappa$, and drop "six times fewer than". The comparison that carries the point is 40, 48, 54 steps for ratios $1$, $10^3$, $10^5$, which the text already has and no cell shows; it deserves the cell.

**S2. The table shows a number the next sentence withdraws.** The same table runs Adam with $\beta_1 = 0.9$ at $\eta = 0.01$, a different step from the $\beta_1 = 0$ row, with no reason given, and reports 7,908 steps on the rotated valley, "more than gradient descent". The next sentence says that retuning brings it "down to about 620 steps" (true: 622 at $\eta = 1$, 620 at $\eta = 3$) and assigns the tuning to an exercise. A reader's first impression is that Adam loses to gradient descent after a rotation; the corrected impression is that momentum, not scaling, rescues it by a factor of ten over gradient descent. Direction: lead with the tuned number in the prose and say why the table's default is shown ("at the conventional $\eta = 0.01$ ... ; at its best step, about 620").

**S3. The warmup subsection argues against its own opening.** The section opening says "The first steps are taken before the scale has been estimated", and "Warmup" opens "The preconditioner must act before it has data." The experiment then shows that replacing $\hat{\mathbf{v}}_t$ by the exact variance changes nothing ("agrees with it to about $0.01$ at every $t$") and concludes that the inflation "comes from the momentum average ... and not from the preconditioner". The last paragraph adds that the long warmups of practice rest on "a second mechanism that this noise model does not contain" and sends the reader to chapter 9. So the subsection sets up one cause, refutes it, and defers the real one. As a piece of reasoning it is honest; as teaching it leaves the reader unsure what warmup is for. It also leaves two loose ends: (i) under "Adam and Its Bias Correction" the text promises that "The warmup subsection below returns to these first steps" (the downward bias of $\sqrt{\hat v}$), and the subsection never mentions that bias; (ii) chapter 9 cites this subsection for the claim that Adam's "estimates are unreliable in the first steps" (`lr-scheduler.md:544`), which is the claim the experiment here does not support. A further point the text half makes: with equal scales on all coordinates, an RMS over coordinates cannot see what the preconditioner does at $t = 1$, because $g/|g|$ and $g/\sigma$ have the same root mean square. The exercise with unequal scales is the experiment that would show it. Direction: reframe the subsection around its finding. Open with the question ("Adam's first update has size $\eta$ in every coordinate. Is that large, and if so, why?"), give the answer (four times the stationary size, for about ten steps, because one gradient is not yet an average), state that this accounts for tens of steps and not the thousands used in practice, and name the curvature mechanism in two sentences here instead of only pointing away. Fix the sentence in the bias-correction subsection and the pointer in chapter 9.

**S4. The promise to the rotated valley is not kept.** "The structured preconditioners at the end of the section address this limit" and, at the start of that part, "The rotated valley defeated every diagonal preconditioner". What follows is the Kronecker identity and a taxonomy of K-FAC, Shampoo and Muon for weight matrices. Nothing is applied to the rotated valley, and Kronecker structure is not what a two-variable rotated quadratic lacks. The reader who was shown a failure is owed its repair: a full-matrix preconditioner $\mathbf{P}_t = (\sum_\tau \mathbf{g}_\tau\mathbf{g}_\tau^\top)^{1/2}$ on the rotated valley takes five lines and would show the step count returning to that of the aligned valley, because the method is rotation-equivariant. Under the decision that experiments wait, the prose fix is to weaken the promise ("...address this limit for weight matrices, by replacing the diagonal with factors that mix coordinates") and to state the equivariance in one sentence where the exercise on rotation is cited.

**S5. The same ground as chapter 9, for about a quarter of the section.** `chapter_optimization/adam.md` derives AdaGrad, RMSProp and Adam in the same order, gives the same bias-correction argument with the same number ("after ten steps $\mathbf{v}_t$ holds about $1\%$"), the same uncorrected first-step factor ($3.2$), and the same display comparing the coupled shrinkage $\eta\lambda/(\sqrt{\hat v_{t,i}} + \epsilon)$ with $\eta\lambda$, followed by nearly the same sentence ("a parameter with large gradients ... is barely decayed, while a parameter with small gradients is decayed strongly"). Here the reader meets all of it again under "AdaGrad", "RMSProp", "Adam and Its Bias Correction" and "Coupled and Decoupled Decay". The appendix must define the three updates, because its propositions are about them and because some readers start here. It need not re-derive the bias correction or re-explain the decay distortion at the same level. What is new here (the metric and regret views, the peak of $6.6$ at $t = 12$, the scale invariance, rotation, the counterexample worked by hand, the fixed points, Kronecker factors) is exactly what chapter 9 points to (`adam.md:116`, `:254`, `:1235`, `:1298`; `muon.md:141`, `:925`). Direction: keep the three update equations with one sentence each, make chapter 9 the home of the bias-correction computation or this section, not both, and reduce "Coupled and Decoupled Decay" to the equation, the experiment and its reading.

**S6. The last part is a survey.** "K-FAC, Shampoo and Muon" defines three methods in 350 words, with no example and no check, and ends on a cost comparison. Guide 12.2 excludes surveys at the end of sections, and `muon.md` in chapter 9 already teaches Muon from scratch and points here for "the whole ladder, from diagonal through Kronecker to spectral preconditioners". The identity $(\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^\top)^{-1/4}\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{G}^\top\mathbf{G})^{-1/4} = \mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}^\top$ is the one piece of mathematics here and is worth keeping; the three-method form $\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{l}}^{-a}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{r}}^{-a}$ is a good organizing line. The rest can shrink by half. The subsection label is still `subsec_mdl-preconditioning-ladder`, and the word "ladder" no longer occurs in the text that chapter 9 says derives one.

**S7. Opening, closing and summary restate the results three times.** The opening already tells the outcome of the rotated valley and of the counterexample ("drives it to the worst feasible point") and lists the "three further consequences"; the last body paragraph ("We can now derive ..., prove ..., read off ..., and apply ...") lists them again; the Summary lists them a third time, with 6,160 and 48. The number 48 appears seven times before the exercises. Direction: keep the opening's first two paragraphs and the question; cut its third and fourth paragraphs to two sentences that name the movements without their results; delete the "We can now" sentence (the same formula ends the previous section and `mdl-convexity.md`); keep the open-question sentences, which are the true ending.

**S8. Length.** One section, not two, but the centre of gravity is the first two parts (about 3,700 words). The decay part (1,100) is proportionate. Warmup (450) is thin and inconclusive (S3). Structured preconditioners (850) is half survey (S6). Removing the duplication of S5 and S7 and half of S6 would save about 900 words with no loss.

## Passage-level findings

**ADA-1** (moderate, thought). Opening. Anchor: "needs 48."
See S1. Add where the step size comes from and what the count depends on. Draft: "An optimizer that gives each coordinate its own step size, estimated only from the gradients it has seen, needs 48, and would need about as many if the ratio were $10^5$."

**ADA-2** (minor, language). Opening. Anchor: "Dividing by a gradient scale has three further consequences".
An announcement followed by three clipped sentences, one of which begins with "And". The paragraph also holds the notation line ("Throughout, $t$ counts optimizer steps ..."), which belongs with the first equation. See S7.

**ADA-3** (minor, explanation). Under "A Diagonal Quadratic". Anchor: "When the coordinates have".
The premise that carries the whole section ("When the coordinates have comparable magnitudes, a coordinate with large gradients therefore has large curvature") is stated once and not revisited. A reader should be told what happens when it fails: dividing by $|g_i| = h_i|x_i|$ gives a step of size $\eta$, not the Newton step $x_i$, so the method normalizes the *distance travelled per step* and not the curvature. The scale-invariance subsection shows this implicitly. One sentence here would connect them and would prepare S1's point.

**ADA-4** (minor, pedagogy). Under "AdaGrad". Anchor: "A global decaying step, $\eta/\sqrt{t}$, is the classical choice".
The previous subsection motivated per-coordinate steps by curvature. This one opens with a different motivation (coordinates "used at very different rates") without saying that it is a second, independent reason. The previous section's classical schedule was $c/k$, with $1/\sqrt{K}$ as a constant step, so "the classical choice" needs half a clause ("in online and nonsmooth convex optimization"). The rare-word example itself is good.

**ADA-5** (moderate, thought). Under "AdaGrad", last paragraph. Anchor: "The squares of $1/\sqrt{t}$ sum to $\sum_t 1/t = \infty$, so AdaGrad's decay is".
The paragraph sets out to say that AdaGrad's steps shrink too much on long runs. On the way it shows that $1/\sqrt{t}$ violates the square-summable Robbins-Monro condition, which means the steps shrink too *slowly* for that theorem. The two remarks pull in opposite directions and the reader is left to sort them out; the previous section had also just explained that the square-summable condition is not needed on a quadratic. Direction: drop the Robbins-Monro detour. "Under persistent noise $s_{t,i} \approx \sigma^2 t$, so the effective step is about $\eta/(\sigma\sqrt{t})$: AdaGrad carries its own decay schedule, tied to the step count and not to progress. That is the right rate for $\sqrt{T}$ regret on convex problems. On long nonconvex runs it is a drawback: ..."

**ADA-6** (minor, language). Under "AdaGrad". Anchor: "*The regret view.* AdaGrad was designed for".
The regret view runs 450 words and is needed (the counterexample is stated in terms of regret). It reads as an insertion because the definition of online convex optimization arrives as a run-in paragraph in the middle of a subsection about a step-size rule. Consider a `####` heading, or move the definition of regret to "A Convex Counterexample to Adam", where it is used, and keep here only the comparison of the two bounds.

**ADA-7** (minor, explanation). Under "Adam and Its Bias Correction". Anchor: "The warmup subsection below returns to these first".
A promise not kept (S3). Either say in "Warmup" what the downward bias of $\sqrt{\hat v_1} = |g_1|$ does (it is the reason the first step is exactly $\eta$ and not $\eta\,g/\sigma$), or delete the sentence.

**ADA-8** (minor, language; notation). Under "Scale Invariance". Anchor: "Suppose two problems have gradients related by".

The hypothesis also deserves its plain-language reading before the proof: "the second problem is the first with coordinate $i$ measured in units $c_i$ times smaller".

**ADA-9** (minor, explanation). Under "Scale Invariance". Anchor: "on, and the step is much smaller than $\eta$".
Ambiguous. The displacement $\eta\,x_t/\sqrt{\hat v_t}$ is smaller than $\eta$ because $|x_t| < \sqrt{\hat v_t}$; the relative step $\eta/\sqrt{\hat v_t}$ grows (from $0.1$ to $0.33$). The next sentence switches to the contraction factor, which is the second of these. Write "so the iterate moves by less than $\eta$ and contracts geometrically, by the factor $1 - \eta/\sqrt{\hat v_t}$, which is $0.9$ at $t = 1$ and $0.67$ at $t = 48$".

**ADA-17** (minor, correctness; not fully checked). Under "A Convex Counterexample to Adam". Anchor: "The authors note that the argument".
The proposition matches Theorem 1 of Reddi, Kale and Kumar as I know it ($C > 2$, $\beta_1 = 0$, $\beta_2 = 1/(1 + C^2)$, $\alpha < \sqrt{1 - \beta_2}$, $x_{3t+1} = 1$), and the per-period numbers are right. **ADA-19** (minor, language). Under "Fixed Points". Anchor: "The answer reverses the".

**ADA-20** (minor, correctness of statement; confident). Under "Fixed Points". The proposition defines a fixed point as a point where "the update vanishes ... once the moment estimates have settled", for exact gradients. **ADA-21** (minor, explanation). Under "Fixed Points". Anchor: "(Running both methods with a cosine-decayed step reproduces".
A result reported in a parenthesis with no cell. Either point to exercise 9, which does this in two dimensions, or drop the sentence; the four values follow from the proposition.

**ADA-22** (moderate, thought). Under "Warmup". Anchors: "The preconditioner must act before it has data" and "The fourfold inflation of the first steps".
See S3. Also, "inflation" is measured against the stationary size $0.23\,\eta$ of a *pure-noise* gradient. For a coordinate with a consistent gradient the stationary update is $\eta$, and the first step is not inflated at all. Say that the comparison is for coordinates whose gradient is noise, which is the case the experiment models and the case where a step of full size $\eta$ is wasted.

**ADA-23** (minor, explanation). Under "Warmup". Anchor: "The long warmups used in practice, thousands of steps, rest on a second".
The reason most readers come to this subsection for is given in two sentences and a pointer to chapter 9, with no label inside the appendix for the curvature fact it relies on (the stability ceiling $2/\lambda_{\max}$ and its drift, which "The Edge of Stability" in `mdl-gradient-based-optimization` treats). **ADA-24** (minor, correctness; confident). Under "K-FAC, Shampoo and Muon". Anchor: "equals the Gauss–Newton matrix, the positive semidefinite part".
"The positive semidefinite part of the Hessian" has a standard meaning (keep the nonnegative eigenvalues) that the Gauss-Newton matrix does not have. The Gauss-Newton matrix is the Hessian with the term containing second derivatives of the network dropped, which is positive semidefinite for a convex loss. Write "a positive semidefinite approximation of the Hessian, which drops the term containing the second derivatives of the network".

**ADA-25** (minor, explanation). Under "Kronecker Factors". Anchor: "The rotated valley defeated every diagonal preconditioner".
The paragraph claims "Every method of this section has the form $\mathbf{x}_{t+1} = \mathbf{x}_t - \eta\,\mathbf{P}_t^{-1}\mathbf{g}_t$", which omits momentum and the decay term the section has just spent two subsections on. "Every preconditioner of this section" is what is meant. In the same part, "The approximation is exact when inputs and output gradients are independent" is correct as a sufficient condition; "which they are not in a trained network" is asserted without a reference or a number and could carry a citation to the K-FAC paper.

**ADA-26** (minor, notation). $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ is the gradient covariance in the previous section and the chapter's notation table, and the matrix of singular values here; $\sigma$ is a "gradient scale" in the decay cell, a noise standard deviation in the warmup cell and in the previous section; $n$ and `m` are rebound across cells (`n = 10_000`, then `20_000`, then `m = n = 4096`, where `m` had been the moment array). Use $\mathbf{S}$ for the singular values (the section already writes $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{in}}$, so perhaps $\mathbf{D}$), and avoid reusing `m`.

**ADA-27** (minor, language). Closing paragraph. Anchors: "We can now derive a coordinate's step from its gradient history" and "All of these results assume curvature aligned with the coordinates".
See S7 for the first. The second is too broad: the fixed-point proposition and the counterexample assume nothing about alignment. Write "The positive results assume ...".
