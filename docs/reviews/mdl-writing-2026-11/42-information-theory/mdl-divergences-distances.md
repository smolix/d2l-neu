# Divergences and Distances Between Distributions

Source: `chapter_mdl-information-theory/mdl-divergences-distances.md`. About 11,100 words of prose and mathematics outside code up to the exercises (13,000 with the 16 code cells), 2,700 words of exercises, six pre-generated figures, two tables, ten propositions. By part: opening 1,100 words; "Divergences and Metrics" 340; "f-Divergences" 4,050; "Test Functions and Transport" 4,800; "Scores" 2,100; "Training Objectives and Their Divergences" 660.

## Verdict

This is careful, correct mathematics with one of the best openings in the part, and it is too much for one section. I recomputed every worked number, checked all ten propositions and proofs, compared every claim about an output with the stored PyTorch outputs, and reran the stochastic cells 200 times to test the stated tolerances. I found no mathematical error, only loose hypotheses at four places (DD-5, DD-14, DD-16, DD-20) and one false claim about notation (DD-22). The table of five comparisons on two point masses, the identity $D_f(\delta_0\|\delta_d) = f(0) + f'(\infty)$ that explains three of its rows in one line, and the crossing argument for $W_1$ on the line would not be out of place in MacKay. Against that: the section is three sections (ratios; test functions and transport; scores), the reader meets ten propositions with no indication of which two the main text depends on, the disjoint-support argument is told four times, and the last 900 words (kernel Stein discrepancy) serve nothing else in the book. The prose is clean and even, with almost no voice. The single most important fix is structural: split into two sections along the existing `##` boundary, move the score material to the score-matching section of the dynamics chapter (or keep it as a third short section), and cut about a quarter (kernel Stein discrepancy, most of Sinkhorn, $\alpha$-divergences, two verification cells).

## What works

- **The opening problem.** "A model at $d = 0.01$ is nearly right and a model at $d = 100$ is far off, so a useful loss should tell them apart." Then a table in which three of five rows do not move. The reader feels the defect before any definition. The October review asked to keep this, and the current version is better than the one it saw: the table now comes first and is computed.
- **The table is explained, not just shown.** "$D_f(\delta_0 \| \delta_d) = f(0) + f'(\infty) \qquad \textrm{for every } d \neq 0$" turns three rows of the table into one formula, evaluated for five generators in the next sentence.
- **The f-divergence is obtained by asking what a proof used.** "Second, Gibbs' proof used only two facts about $u \log u$: it is convex, and it is zero at $u = 1$." This is the right way to generalize, and the proof then names its own key step ("The step that makes the argument work is that the ratio averages to one under $Q$").
- **Predict, then compute.** The forward fit is predicted from the proposition ($-0.8$ and $\sqrt{3.72}$), the reverse-KL values from $\log(1/0.7)$ and $\log(1/0.3)$, the critic experiment from the closed form $\chi^2(2c - c^2)$, the MMD estimates from a Gaussian integral, the KSD from $1/\sqrt3$. Every cell prints a number the prose has already derived. This is the method the Basics overview asked for, and here it is carried through.
- **"A half-trained critic ($c = \tfrac12$) reports $0.76$, which is $75\%$ of the divergence, and an overconfident one ($c = 2$) reports zero."** A memorable way to see that adversarial estimates are biased low.
- **The operational meaning of TV**: "the best possible test beats coin flipping by $\textrm{TV}/2$."
- **MMD built from the smallest class up**: linear functions see only means ("It cannot see a change of spread; for $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ against $\mathcal{N}(0, 4)$ it reports $0$"), then $(x, x^2)$ gives $9$, then the kernel. Four steps, each with the same pair of Gaussians. The "panel of auditors" is the one image in the section and it works.
- **The proof of $W_1$ on the line.** "any plan must carry at least $|F_P(t) - F_Q(t)|$ of mass across $t$" is the whole idea, stated before the proposition. The four-step proof that follows is the best proof in the section: each step is one identity, and the non-routine step is named.
- **The non-uniqueness remark** after the $W_1$ cell (every rightward plan costs $3.35 - 1.65$) anticipates exactly what a reader who inspects `res.x` will wonder.
- **The Fisher blind spot.** KL settles at $0.339$ nats while the Fisher divergence falls from $0.1$ to $4 \times 10^{-6}$. One loop, three lines of output, a real limitation of score matching that the diffusion chapter uses.
- **Tolerances are honest.** The stochastic cells are unseeded and the prose quotes spreads ("about $9$, give or take $3$", "within about $\pm 0.1$"). In 200 reruns every stated tolerance held: quadratic MMD ranged 6.2 to 12.1, RBF 0.069 to 0.123, shifted pair 0.024 to 0.081, KSD against the wrong model 0.45 to 0.68, the witness's sign change 0.09 to 0.41.
- **The closing table** pairs each training objective with its divergence, what it needs, and its value on the opening's point masses. It is the page a reader arriving from the main text most wants.

## Structural findings

**S1. The section is three sections.** The four "choices" announced in the opening are three different problems. Ratio methods answer "which function of $p/q$?", test-function and transport methods answer "what can be computed from samples and sees geometry?", and score methods answer a different question about a different kind of model ("An energy-based model specifies a density only up to its normalizing constant"). The `##` headings already mark the seams. Proposed division, with all existing labels kept (they have inbound references, listed in S2):

1. *f-Divergences* (about 5,500 words): the opening table, shortened; divergence against metric, folded into the opening; template; generators; local quadratic; forward and reverse KL; total variation, Pinsker and data processing (these are statements about f-divergences and belong here, see S4); the f-GAN bound last, since its second caveat ("An optimal critic that is constant on the model's support supplies no gradient") is the natural handover.
2. *Test Functions and Transport* (about 3,500 words): IPMs, MMD, $W_1$ and Kantorovich–Rubinstein, a short Sinkhorn paragraph, then the objectives table and the taxonomy figure.
3. *Scores*: the Fisher divergence, its blind spot, and Stein's identity as the integration by parts of score matching (about 1,200 words after the cuts below). The only users are `sec_mdl-score-matching-diffusion-flow`, `mdl-fokker-planck-probability-flow.md` and `chapter_diffusion-models/denoising.md`. This fits better as the opening of the score-matching section in the dynamics chapter than as the tail of a section on GAN losses. If it stays in this chapter, make it its own short section.

**S2. What a deep learning reader needs from it, judged by who cites it.** Inbound references from outside the section:

So the main text needs: the f-divergence definition with its conventions on disjoint supports; the Fenchel proof of the f-GAN bound; forward against reverse KL; TV as a testing advantage, Pinsker, and data processing; the MMD closed form; and $W_1$ with its dual and its CDF form. It does not need the $\alpha$-divergence family, Sinkhorn iterations, Stein's identity as a test, or the kernel Stein discrepancy. The GAN chapter's `objectives.md` restates each needed result in a sentence and cites the proof here, which is the right division of labour; the appendix should therefore concentrate on proofs and worked numbers and can drop what nothing uses.

**S3. Nothing in the section points to the main text by label.** The body names "the original GAN", "WGAN", "MMD-GANs", "diffusion models", "variational inference" and "energy-based model" in prose, but contains no reference to `chap_gans`, `sec_gan_objectives`, `sec_gan_convergence`, `chap_diffusion` or any other main-text label. The only outward references go to other appendix sections. A reader who starts here cannot find where a WGAN critic is trained or where the Fisher blind spot bites; a reader who arrives from `objectives.md` cannot find the way back. Add one labelled pointer per use: at the f-GAN bound and the WGAN paragraph to `sec_gan_objectives`, at the Lipschitz constraint to `sec_gan_convergence`, at the forward and reverse KL to the distillation and regularized-RL sections, at the Fisher divergence to the denoising section.

**S4. Total variation, Pinsker and data processing are in the wrong part, and data processing cannot be found.** TV is defined twice: as a row of the generator table (with its value $0.4$ on the running pair) and 450 lines later as a supremum over events. Pinsker relates two f-divergences, and the data-processing inequality is a statement about all of them, yet both sit under "Test Functions and Transport", inside a subsection titled "Total Variation and Pinsker's Inequality". `relativistic.md` cites data processing as "the data-processing inequality of :numref:`sec_mdl-tv-pinsker`", and `mdl-mutual-information.md` and `mdl-mi-estimation.md` cite it and the f-GAN bound through the label of the whole 15,000-word section. Give data processing its own subsection and label in the f-divergence section, and let the IPM subsection begin with one sentence ("TV is a supremum over events, that is, over indicator functions").

**S5. The disjoint-support argument is told four times.** (1) The opening: "A loss that does not change with the generator's parameters has zero gradient". (2) After the conventions: "Every f-divergence is thus a constant on disjoint supports. It carries no information about how far apart the supports are". (3) The second caveat of the f-GAN bound: "An optimal critic that is constant on the model's support supplies no gradient to the generator". (4) The opening of optimal transport, which begins "Return to the point masses" and re-derives all three constants and the zero gradient in a paragraph. The second telling is the proof and the third adds the critic's view. The first can lose its GAN sentence (DD-1) and the fourth should be one clause with a reference (DD-23). `objectives.md` tells it a fifth time, with a labelled reference; that one is fine.

**S6. No result is ranked.** Ten propositions arrive in the same typography and the same tone. Nothing tells the reader that the f-GAN bound and Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality are the two results on which adversarial training rests, that Pinsker is the workhorse inequality in later proofs, or that the forward-KL moment-matching proposition is a routine calculation. The writing guide's first point on voice ("The authors judge") applies here more than anywhere in the chapter. Two sentences in the opening, and a clause before each proposition saying what it is for, would do it.

**S7. The scores part swaps the roles of $P$ and $Q$** without saying so (DD-28). This is the one place where a careful reader will be misled.

**S8. Code.** All 16 cells compute something the prose reads; the five plots are data plots of computed results; no cell draws an illustration. Two cells only verify what has just been proved or defined (DD-29, DD-31). The imports cell exists in four framework tabs although, as the text says, "The numerical examples use NumPy and SciPy"; the frameworks are loaded only for `d2l.plot` and `d2l.show_heatmaps`, and `math`, `nn`, `F`, `autograd`, `optax` and the rest are unused (DD-36). Tabs do not fragment the prose anywhere.

**S9. Overlap with Basics.** None to speak of. `chapter_preliminaries` has no divergences; the section builds on `sec_mdl-information_theory` (KL, Gibbs, the Gaussian KL formula, the running pair) and on the conjugate and duality subsections of the optimization chapter, and says so. The level is steady except for three jumps above the stated reader: "For general measures ... $P_\perp$" (DD-6), absolute continuity cited to Folland in the $W_1$ proof (DD-25), and the RKHS aside.

## Passage-level findings

**DD-1** (moderate, explanation). Anchor: "adversarial network (GAN) at its best discriminator, which is", section opening.
"The Jensen--Shannon row fixes the loss of the original generative adversarial network (GAN) at its best discriminator, which is $2\,\textrm{JS} - \log 4$ and therefore zero in every column." The sentence is in the third paragraph of the section and uses "GAN", "discriminator" and "fixes the loss" before any of them means anything here; "which" could refer to the discriminator; and the value zero is not the point, constancy is. A reader starting in the appendix is lost, and a reader from the GAN chapter does not need it. Replace by: "A model trained on any of these three would receive the same loss at $d = 0.01$ as at $d = 100$, hence no gradient in $d$. This is the situation of the original GAN, whose loss is the Jensen--Shannon row up to constants (:numref:`sec_mdl-f-gan-dual`)."

**DD-2** (moderate, language). Anchors: "generator cannot learn which way to move." (opening) and "equals the divergence at the optimal critic, while the generator that" (under "The Fenchel Conjugate and the f-GAN Bound").
"Generator" has two meanings in this section: the convex function $f$ (about 45 uses, defined) and the network that produces samples (opening, f-GAN paragraph, the caveats, the optimal-transport opening; never defined). The worst spot is the paragraph on the original GAN, where "twice the Jensen--Shannon generator" is followed ten lines later by "supplies no gradient to the generator". The GAN chapter noticed the clash and resolved it the other way: "We call $f$ the *divergence function*; the f-GAN literature calls it the generator, a word this chapter keeps for the network." Choose one convention for both chapters. The cheapest fix here is to keep "generator" for $f$ and write "the model" or "the sampling network" for the network, with one sentence at the f-GAN bound noting that the GAN literature calls that network the generator.

**DD-3** (minor, language). Anchor: "contain no logarithm and are dimensionless, and transport distances carry the", opening.
The paragraph on units is five sentences on a point that needs one ("KL, reverse KL and JS are in nats; the others have no unit, except transport distances, which are in the units of $x$"). The opening runs 1,100 words, a table, two import cells and a compute cell before the first heading. Shorten the units and notation paragraphs, and move the sentence "How accurately any of them can be estimated from a finite sample stays open" to the closing, where it is already said.

**DD-4** (minor, pedagogy). Anchor: "Before building any of the five comparisons, we fix the least that a", under "Divergences and Metrics".
A top-level `##` section of five paragraphs. The content (definition, the $0.318$ against $0.807$ example, squared distances failing the triangle inequality) is good and short. Make it the first subsection of the f-divergence part, or fold it into the opening after the table. The Gaussian KL formula is displayed again with a three-sentence reading of its terms that `sec_mdl-information_theory` already gave; cite the equation there instead.

**DD-5** (minor, correctness; sure). Anchor: "two properties. Let $f : (0, \infty) \to \mathbb{R}$ be convex with", under "The f-Divergence Template".
The generator is defined on $(0, \infty)$, but $P \ll Q$ allows $p(x) = 0 < q(x)$, so the definition and the Jensen step of the non-negativity proof evaluate $f$ at $u = 0$. The value $f(0) := \lim_{u \downarrow 0} f(u)$ is introduced only a page later, among the conventions for the case without $P \ll Q$. Move that one convention into the definition ("with $f(0)$ defined as the limit from the right, possibly $+\infty$").

**DD-6** (moderate, explanation). Anchor: "For general measures the same rule reads: the part $P_\perp$ of $P$ that sits", under "The f-Divergence Template".
One sentence of measure theory (singular part, total mass on $\mathcal{X}$) for a reader with "some probability". It adds nothing to the two cases just given. Delete it. In the same paragraph, $f'(\infty)$ is defined as $\lim f(t)/t$ but written as a derivative, and it is later used as a derivative ("it equals $f'(0)$ on one and $f'(\infty)$ on the other"). Say once that for a convex $f$ the slope $f'(t)$ and the ratio $f(t)/t$ have the same limit, which is why the limit is written $f'(\infty)$. The paragraph is otherwise one of the most useful in the section: say so by leading with its purpose ("Generative models begin training with disjoint supports, so we must say what $D_f$ means there") instead of placing that sentence third.

**DD-7** (minor, language). Anchor: "*Total variation* uses $f(u) = \tfrac{1}{2}|u - 1|$, which unwinds to", under "Common Generators".
"Unwinds" three times in one subsection ("which unwinds to", "Pearson's divergence unwinds the same way", "The squared Hellinger divergence unwinds to"). Write "reduces to" or "is".

**DD-8** (moderate, pedagogy). Anchor: "The table's last row is a family within the family :cite:`Amari.2016`. For", under "Common Generators".
The $\alpha$-divergence paragraph comes after the cell and its reading, as an afterthought, and nothing in the section, the chapter or the book uses it. The interesting fact (forward and reverse KL are the two ends of one family, Hellinger the midpoint) is already the subject of exercise 1(b) and 1(d). Remove the paragraph and the table row from the text and let the exercise introduce the generator, or keep one sentence.

**DD-9** (moderate, thought). Anchor: "The same expansion connects the family to the Fisher information", under "The Local Quadratic".
This paragraph asserts $\chi^2(q_{\boldsymbol\theta}\|q_{\boldsymbol\theta + \mathbf h}) \approx \mathbf h^\top \mathbf I(\boldsymbol\theta)\mathbf h$ without derivation, concludes the Hessian of every smooth $D_f$, and cites the natural gradient. The same content is told again in the further-reading section under "Information Geometry", there with the symbol $\mathbf{F}$ for the Fisher information. Keep it in one place. Since natural gradient is the deep learning use, I would keep two sentences here with the one-line derivation ($u - 1 \approx \mathbf h^\top \nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\log q_{\boldsymbol\theta}$, so $\mathbb{E}_Q[(u-1)^2] \approx \mathbf h^\top \mathbf I \mathbf h$) and reduce the further-reading entry to Chentsov's uniqueness statement. Use one symbol. The preceding paragraph's conclusion deserves to stay as it is: "They disagree only about distant distributions, which is the regime early in training, when the model is far from the data."

**DD-10** (minor, explanation). Anchor: "the *reverse* KL from the approximate posterior to the true one, which is why", under "Forward and Reverse KL".
"which is why variational posteriors tend to be too narrow" is asserted before the experiment that shows it. Move the clause after the cell's reading, where $\sigma = 0.603$ against the target's $1.929$ makes the point. Also give the names a deep learning reader has met: the section says "zero-avoiding" and "zero-forcing" but never "mode-covering" and "mode-seeking", which are the words used in the papers the LM and RL chapters cite when they refer to this subsection.

**DD-11** (minor, pedagogy). Anchor: "**Proposition (forward KL fits moments).** *Let $P$ have a density with finite", under "Forward and Reverse KL".
Ten lines of proof for a calculation the reader did in `sec_mdl-maximum_likelihood` (the Gaussian maximum-likelihood estimate, with $P$ in place of the empirical distribution). The proof is correct and not elegant: completing the square, then a sign analysis of a derivative. Replace by two sentences: forward KL equals a constant minus $\mathbb{E}_P[\log q]$, so its minimizer is the maximum-likelihood Gaussian for data distributed as $P$, which matches mean and variance. The general exponential-family statement that follows is then the natural remark.

**DD-12** (moderate, explanation). Anchor: "case $f(u) = u\log u - (u+1)\log(u+1)$, which equals", under "The Fenchel Conjugate and the f-GAN Bound".
The paragraph that connects the bound to the original GAN omits the one formula the reader would recognize. It gives $f$, says "Its critic is the log of a discriminator, $T = \log D$", and jumps to the value at the optimum. The reader must work out alone that $f^*(t) = -\log(1 - e^t)$ and that the bound is therefore $\mathbb{E}_P[\log D] + \mathbb{E}_Q[\log(1 - D)]$. Also, this $f$ has $f(1) = -\log 4$, which contradicts the hypothesis $f(1) = 0$ of the proposition stated half a page earlier; "up to ... a constant" covers it only for a reader who is already sure. Write the three steps in one display: the conjugate, the substitution $T = \log D$, the familiar objective; then "The constant $f(1) = -\log 4$ shifts the value and nothing else."

**DD-13** (minor, explanation). Anchor: "conjugate taken over $u > 0$ alone is smaller, so using the conjugate over", under "The Fenchel Conjugate and the f-GAN Bound".
A remark on the domain of the conjugate interrupts the setup of the experiment. It answers an objection the reader has not raised, and exercise 3(a) treats it fully. Reduce to a parenthesis ("taking the supremum over all real $u$; problem ... (a) shows that this loses nothing at $T^\star$").

**DD-14** (minor, correctness; sure). Anchor: "support (it equals $f'(0)$ on one and $f'(\infty)$ on the other). An optimal", under "The Fenchel Conjugate and the f-GAN Bound".
The proposition assumes $P \ll Q$ and a differentiable $f$; the second caveat applies its optimal critic to disjoint supports, where the assumption fails, and uses $f'(\infty)$ as a derivative (see DD-6). The conclusion is right. Say "Formally extend $T^\star = f'(p/q)$ to disjoint supports:" so that the reader sees it is an extension, and note which value sits on which support ($f'(\infty)$ on the data, $f'(0)$ on the model's samples).

**DD-15** (moderate, pedagogy). Anchor: "**Proposition (data processing for f-divergences).** *For any channel", under "Total Variation and Pinsker's Inequality".
See S4. The proposition is introduced as a generalization of a step in the previous proof ("The merging step is a special case of the next proposition") and never motivated on its own, although two later sections and the GAN chapter rely on it. Give it a subsection with a label, open with the question ("Can a feature extractor, a quantizer, or any other processing make two distributions easier to tell apart?"), and state the deep learning reading after the proof: a divergence measured on features is a lower bound on the divergence between the inputs.

**DD-16** (minor, correctness; sure). Anchor: "**Proof.** *Step 1: two outcomes.* For $a, b \in (0, 1)$ let", under "Total Variation and Pinsker's Inequality".
Step 1 proves the coin inequality for $a, b \in (0, 1)$. Step 2 applies it to $a = P(A^\star)$, $b = Q(A^\star)$, which can be $0$ or $1$ (if $P = Q$ then $A^\star$ is empty; if $Q$ gives $A^\star$ no mass then $b = 0$). The boundary cases are trivial (KL infinite, or by continuity with $0\log 0 = 0$) but the proof as written does not cover them. Add one clause. The proposition is stated for countable sets while the data-processing proposition that is said to contain its merging step is stated for finite sets; this is harmless because the proof uses the log-sum inequality directly, but align the two statements.

**DD-17** (minor, thought). Anchor: "The following experiment asks whether the inequality is ever violated and how", under "Total Variation and Pinsker's Inequality".
An experiment cannot ask whether a proved inequality "is ever violated". The 10,000 random pairs teach one thing, visible in the scatter plot: the bound is loose for most pairs and the gap grows with KL. The coins teach the other: the constant cannot be improved. Say that these are the two questions ("How tight is the bound for typical pairs, and is the constant $\tfrac12$ the best possible?"). The attribution in the reading ("This sharp constant is due to Csiszár ... not to Pinsker's original argument") is a historical aside in the middle of reading an output; Kullback and Kemperman obtained the constant independently in the same years, so either give all three or drop the sentence.

**DD-18** (moderate, explanation). Anchor: "bounded functions it cannot in general, because a rich enough class separates", under "Integral Probability Metrics and MMD".
"For all bounded functions it cannot in general, because a rich enough class separates any two finite samples." This is the reason MMD and $W_1$ exist, and it is stated abstractly. Make it concrete: "Two samples of $1{,}000$ points from the same continuous distribution share no point, so the indicator of the first sample has mean $1$ on it and $0$ on the other. The empirical TV is $1$ whatever the distributions are. A class of test functions must be small or smooth enough that sample averages track population averages."

**DD-19** (minor, language). Anchor: "The class $\mathcal{G}$ is a panel of auditors, and the IPM reports the", under "Integral Probability Metrics and MMD".
The function being maximized has five names in the section: critic ($T$), test function ($g$), auditor, witness, and "tester" for the person. They are all introduced properly, but nowhere is it said that the f-GAN critic and the IPM test function play the same role, and in the WGAN paragraph "a neural critic plays the role of $g$". Add one sentence at the IPM definition: "The critic of the f-GAN bound was such a function too; the difference is that there the second expectation is of $f^*(T)$, here of $g$ itself." Keep the auditors.

**DD-20** (moderate, correctness; fairly sure it is loose, not wrong). Anchor: "quickly. For such *characteristic* kernels, $\textrm{MMD}(P, Q) = 0$ only if", under "Integral Probability Metrics and MMD".
"So the RBF kernel compares moments of every order, with weights that decrease quickly. For such *characteristic* kernels, $\textrm{MMD}(P, Q) = 0$ only if $P = Q$". "Such" has no antecedent: no property has been stated that makes a kernel characteristic, and "compares moments of every order" does not imply the conclusion, since moments do not determine a distribution in general (the features here are damped monomials, which is why it works, but the text does not say that). Write it as a definition and a cited fact: "A kernel for which $\textrm{MMD}(P, Q) = 0$ only if $P = Q$ is called *characteristic*. The RBF kernel is characteristic :cite:..., so its MMD is a metric."

**DD-21** (minor, correctness; sure). Anchor: "$Q$ on $\mathbb{R}^n$ (or any complete separable metric space) with finite", under "Optimal Transport and the Wasserstein Distance".
Dimension conventions drift. MMD is developed for scalar $x$ only (the RBF kernel is written with $(x - x')^2$, the features $\varphi_n$ are one-dimensional) without saying so; $W_1$ is defined with $\|x - y\|$ on $\mathbb{R}^n$; the Stein kernel is derived in one dimension and then displayed "In $d$ dimensions"; the Fisher divergence uses bold $\mathbf{x}$. State once, at the start of the test-function part, that the worked examples are on the line and that in $\mathbb{R}^n$ squared differences become squared norms, and use one letter for the dimension.

**DD-22** (moderate, correctness; sure). Anchor: "The section uses a dozen symbols, each with one meaning; the chapter's full", opening.
The claim is false for several symbols. $d$ is the separation of the point masses, the binary KL divergence $d(a\|b)$ in Pinsker's proof, part of $d_{\mathrm H}$, the dimension ("In $d$ dimensions"), and a polynomial degree in exercise 7. $\delta$ is a point mass, the bound $\max_x|u(x) - 1|$ of the local-quadratic proposition, the coin offset in the Pinsker cell, a mean shift in exercise 5 and a Kronecker delta in exercise 7. $n$ is a dimension and a sample size; the closing uses $N$ for the sample size. $\mathbf{K}$ is the Gibbs kernel of Sinkhorn and, in the first cell, a kernel matrix; $\kappa$ is a channel and `kappa` in the KSD cell is the Stein kernel. Most collisions are far enough apart to be harmless; the ones worth fixing are $d(a\|b)$ (write $D_{\textrm{KL}}(\textrm{Ber}(a)\|\textrm{Ber}(b))$ or reuse the binary-KL notation of the earlier sections), $\delta$ in the local-quadratic proposition (use $\eta$ or $r$), and the dimension.
