# How GANs Are Taught, 2024–2026: A Survey of University Courses

Research compiled 2026-08-02 for the ground-up rewrite of the d2l GAN chapter
(`chapter_generative-adversarial-networks/`).

**Evidence conventions.** Every factual claim below carries a URL. Where a claim
is an inference rather than a direct quotation, it is marked "**inferred from
X**". Where materials were paywalled, JS-gated, or simply absent, that is
stated explicitly rather than papered over — a course whose GAN content we
could not see is recorded as *unverified*, not as *absent*.

**Method.** Primary sources were fetched directly. Slide decks were downloaded
and run through `pdftotext -layout` rather than summarised by a fetch model, so
the topic sequences quoted for Stanford CS236, CS231n, Berkeley CS294-158 and
MIT 6.S978 are read off the actual slides. Regional long-tail coverage (CMU,
Europe, Asia, US long tail + MOOCs) was gathered by parallel sub-agents whose
findings are integrated below with their own source URLs.

---

## Part A — Per-course findings

### A1. Stanford CS236 · Deep Generative Models (Ermon)

- Public course site: <https://deepgenerativemodels.github.io/>
- Syllabus: <https://deepgenerativemodels.github.io/syllabus.html>
- Notes (GAN chapter): <https://deepgenerativemodels.github.io/notes/gan/>
- Slides: `cs236_lecture9.pdf`, `cs236_lecture10.pdf` under
  <https://deepgenerativemodels.github.io/assets/slides/>

**Latest publicly posted iteration: Fall 2023.** The public site header reads
"Fall 2023"; the syllabus dates run Sep 27 – Dec 06, 2023. The course is still
taught (a professional-education mirror, XCS236, runs through Stanford Online:
<https://online.stanford.edu/courses/xcs236-deep-generative-models>), but the
2024/2025 lecture materials are behind Canvas. Treat the Fall-2023 deck as the
most recent *visible* iteration. Stanford ExploreCourses is a JS-only SPA and
could not be scraped to confirm 2025–26 offerings — **unverified**.

**GAN placement and budget: lectures 9–10 of 18** — i.e. two of eighteen
lectures, ~11% of the course. Order: autoregressive → maximum likelihood → VAEs
→ normalizing flows → **GANs** → energy-based models → score-based models →
evaluation → score-based diffusion → discrete latent variables → discrete
diffusion. So GANs sit **after** all the likelihood-based families and
**before** everything score/diffusion.

**The framing is the MLE→likelihood-free pivot, and it is explicit.** Lecture 9
opens by recapping that autoregressive models, VAEs and flows "are all trained
by minimizing KL divergence $D_{KL}(p_{data}\|p_\theta)$, or equivalently
maximizing likelihoods", then asks: "Is the likelihood a good indicator of the
quality of samples generated by the model?" It answers with **three worked
cases** that are the pedagogical heart of the lecture:

1. *Optimal model* — best likelihood and best samples coincide.
2. *Great likelihood, terrible samples.* The mixture
   $p_\theta(x) = 0.01\,p_{data}(x) + 0.99\,p_{noise}(x)$, where 99% of samples
   are noise, yet
   $\mathbb{E}_{p_{data}}[\log p_\theta] \ge \mathbb{E}_{p_{data}}[\log p_{data}] - \log 100$.
   Because $\log p_{data}$ grows linearly in dimension $n$ while $\log 100$ is
   constant, in high dimension the likelihoods are *indistinguishable*.
3. *Great samples, terrible likelihood* — memorise the training set; samples are
   perfect, test likelihood is zero.

Conclusion on the slide: "it might be useful to disentangle likelihoods and
sample quality. Likelihood-free learning consider[s] alternative training
objectives that do not depend directly on a likelihood function." This is the
single best motivating argument found in any course surveyed, and it is
reproduced almost verbatim in the derived Cornell and (in spirit) CMU 11-785
material.

**Lecture 9 topic sequence** (read from the slides):
two-sample tests (null $H_0: P=Q$, test statistic, "likelihood-free since it
does not involve the densities") → learn the test statistic with a classifier →
discriminator objective
$\max_{D_\phi} \mathbb{E}_{p_{data}}[\log D_\phi(x)] + \mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}[\log(1-D_\phi(x))]$
→ **optimal discriminator** $D^*(x) = p_{data}/(p_{data}+p_\theta)$ → generator
as implicit latent-variable model, "similar to a flow model, but mapping
$G_\theta$ need not be invertible" → substitute $D^*$ and derive
$V(G,D^*) = 2 D_{JSD}[p_{data}, p_G] - \log 4$ → JSD properties (symmetric,
$\sqrt{D_{JSD}}$ is a metric) → pros/cons ("Cons: very difficult to train in
practice") → the training algorithm → **optimization challenges**
("Theorem (informal): if generator updates are made in function space and the
discriminator is optimal at every step, then the generator is guaranteed to
converge… Unrealistic assumptions!"; "No robust stopping criteria in practice
(unlike MLE)") → **mode collapse** (mixture-of-Gaussians toy: "the generator
distribution keeps oscillating between different modes") → "Fixes to mode
collapse are mostly empirically driven", pointing at
<https://github.com/soumith/ganhacks> → the Christie's $432,500 GAN artwork.

**Lecture 10 topic sequence:** recap → the GAN Zoo
(<https://github.com/hindupuravinash/the-gan-zoo>) → **f-divergences**
($D_f(p,q)=\mathbb{E}_q[f(p/q)]$, $f$ convex lsc with $f(1)=0$; non-negativity
by Jensen) → **Fenchel conjugate and strong duality** ($f^{**}=f$ for convex
lsc $f$) → **f-GAN variational divergence minimization**,
$\min_\theta\max_\phi \mathbb{E}_{p_{data}}[T_\phi(x)] - \mathbb{E}_{p_{G}}[f^*(T_\phi(x))]$
→ **why f-divergences fail on disjoint supports** (the $p=\delta_0$ vs
$q_\theta=\delta_\theta$ example: $D_{KL}\in\{0,\infty\}$,
$D_{JS}\in\{0,\log 2\}$) → **Wasserstein distance**
$D_w(p,q)=\inf_{\gamma\in\Pi}\mathbb{E}_\gamma\|x-y\|_1$, with $D_w(p,q_\theta)=|\theta|$
→ **Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality**, WGAN objective, "Lipschitzness … enforced
through weight clipping or gradient penalty" → **BiGAN** (encoder $E$, joint
two-sample test on $(z,G(z))$ vs $(E(x),x)$) → **CycleGAN** with the explicit
cycle-consistency loss → AlignFlow (Grover et al.: if $G$ is a flow, $F=G^{-1}$
and cycle consistency is exact) → StarGAN.

**What CS236's GAN lectures do NOT contain** (verified by full-text grep):
no non-saturating loss (!), no DCGAN architecture guidance, no spectral
normalization, no StyleGAN, no FID/Inception Score (evaluation is deferred to a
separate lecture), no precision/recall, no Mescheder convergence analysis, no
relativistic GANs, no R3GAN.

**Homeworks.** Three homeworks (15% each) + midterm (15%) + project (40%). The
assignment content is not public; the syllabus lists only release/due dates.
**Unverified** whether HW2 or HW3 includes a GAN implementation.

**Prerequisites the GAN lectures assume:** convex analysis (convexity, lower
semicontinuity, Fenchel conjugation, strong duality) and optimal transport
(couplings, Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality). No RKHS/MMD.

### A2. Stanford CS231n · Deep Learning for Computer Vision — the clearest before/after evidence

- 2025 schedule: <https://cs231n.stanford.edu/2025/schedule.html>
- L13 slides: <https://cs231n.stanford.edu/slides/2025/lecture_13.pdf>
- L14 slides: <https://cs231n.stanford.edu/slides/2025/lecture_14.pdf>
- 2025 A3: <https://cs231n.github.io/assignments2025/assignment3/>
- 2019 A3: <https://cs231n.github.io/assignments2019/assignment3/>

**Latest iteration: Spring 2025** (the "10th Anniversary" edition; slides are
dated May 15 / May 20, 2025).

**This is the single most quantitatively legible datapoint in the whole
survey**, because the slide decks can be counted:

- L13 "Generative Models 1" (116 slides): taxonomy (tractable density →
  approximate density → implicit density), autoregressive models, VAEs. Its
  closing slide reads "Next Time: Generative Models (part 2) — Generative
  Adversarial Networks, Diffusion Models", i.e. **GANs and diffusion share a
  single 80-minute lecture**.
- L14 "Generative Models 2" (123 slides): **GANs occupy slides 8–35 (28
  slides); diffusion occupies slides 36–123 (88 slides).** So in Stanford's
  flagship vision course, GANs get roughly a quarter of one lecture — about
  20–25 minutes — versus ~70 minutes for rectified flow / CFG / latent
  diffusion / DiT / video diffusion / distillation.

**What survives in those 28 slides** (read from the deck): the minimax
objective; $D(x)=P(x\text{ is real})$; "imagine fixing G / imagine fixing D";
alternating gradient updates with the explicit warning "**We are not minimizing
any overall loss! No training curves to look at!**"; the **saturation problem
and the non-saturating fix** ("At start of training… $D(G(z))$ close to 0.
Problem: Gradients for G are close to 0. Solution: … train generator to
minimize $-\log D(G(z))$"); "Why is this a good objective?" with the JSD result
stated and "**(Proof omitted)**"; DC-GAN; **StyleGAN** with the AdaIN formula
$\mathrm{AdaIN}(x,w,b)_i = w_i (x_i-\mu(x))/\sigma(x) + b_i$; latent-space
interpolation $z_t = t z_0 + (1-t) z_1$ (citing Alias-Free GAN); and a summary
slide whose "Cons" column is:

> "No loss curve to look at / Unstable training / Hard to scale to big models +
> data" — annotated "**These were the go-to generative models from ~2016 –
> 2021**".

That past-tense annotation is the clearest single statement of how a top-tier
2025 course positions GANs.

**The SP24 GAN lecture is nonetheless the most encyclopaedic single GAN lecture
found anywhere.** Its own outline slide:

> Bayes-optimal Discriminator; Jensen-Shannon Divergence; Mode Collapse;
> **Avoiding Saturation** · GAN Progression: DC GAN · Improved Training of GANs
> (Salimans et al, 2016) · WGAN, WGAN-GP, Progressive GAN, SN-GAN, SAGAN ·
> BigGAN, BigGAN-Deep, StyleGAN, StyleGAN-v2, VIB-GAN · Creative Conditional

> Transport, Implicit Likelihood Models, Moment Matching** · Other uses of
> Adversarial Loss: Transfer Learning, Fairness · **GANs and Imitation
> Learning**

Notable specifics read from the slides:

- **A superb motivating slide**: it shows a literal `glob.glob('*.jpg')` +
  `np.random.randint` "sampler", then argues why that isn't a generative model
  ("smoothly interpolate across the training samples… digits with unseen
  strokes, faces with unseen poses").
- **Evaluation is taught inside the GAN lecture** (unlike CS236): Parzen-window
  / KDE estimates *and why they are unreliable* (citing Theis, van den Oord &
  Bethge), then Inception Score (with the "low entropy $p(y|x)$, high entropy
  $p(y)$" argument), then FID with the explicit critique that "Inception Score
  doesn't sufficiently measure diversity: a list of 1000 images (one of each
  class) can obtain perfect Inception Score" — and FID's definition as
  mean/covariance of Inception-v3 `pool3` features.
- **Mode-covering vs mode-seeking** as a first-class concept, with a
  KL / reverse-KL / JSD comparison slide and the compression-vs-perceptual
  tradeoff spelled out.
- DCGAN's actual recipe, transcribed: no pooling, transposed convs up,
  strided convs down, ReLU in G / LeakyReLU(0.2) in D, tanh out of G, BatchNorm
  except at G's output and D's input, Adam lr 2e-4 β₁=0.5 batch 128.
- Salimans et al. 2016 tricks: minibatch discrimination, feature matching,
  historical averaging, virtual batch normalization, one-sided label smoothing,
  semi-supervised discriminator.
- WGAN → **WGAN-GP** with the memorable editorial "**Since then no-one uses
  batchnorm in D anymore**".
- **SN-GAN** ("connecting Lipschitzness of discriminator to spectral norm of
  each layer", plus the projection discriminator), **SAGAN** ("applies spectral
  normalization to both the generator and discriminator … counter-intuitive to
  popular belief that you only have to mathematically condition the
  discriminator", hinge loss), **BigGAN** (batch size, cross-replica BN,
  orthonormal regularization, truncation trick), **StyleGAN/StyleGAN-2**
  (AdaIN, noise injection, style mixing), **VIB-GAN** (instance noise →
  variational information bottleneck on D).
- pix2pix (slides credited to Phillip Isola), GauGAN, Everybody Dance Now,
  Video2Video, "Learning to paint (GANs + RL)".
- InfoGAN / BiGAN / BigBiGAN for representation learning.
- Optimal transport (25 slide mentions), Sinkhorn, **moment matching / MMD**
  (26 mentions), and **GAIL / imitation learning** (~30 mentions).

**Not present:** relativistic GANs, R3GAN, Mescheder convergence analysis,
precision-recall metrics (verified by grep).

**Homework 3 (Spring 2024) — the best GAN assignment found in the survey.**
Deliverable: a PDF built by dropping auto-generated figures into a provided
LaTeX template, submitted to Gradescope with the Colab notebook.

- **Q1 "Warmup" [20 pt] — 1-D toy data.** (a) Train the **original minimax**
  objective; (b) train the **non-saturating** objective
  $L^{(G)} = -\mathbb{E}_z \log D(G(z))$. Both use a 3-layer, 128-hidden-unit
  MLP for G and D with LeakyReLU(0.2). Deliverables for each: samples at epoch
  1, final samples, and the loss curve. *This is a direct, minimal, visual A/B
  of the saturating vs non-saturating loss — the cleanest teaching device
  found.*
- **Q2 "GANs on CIFAR-10" [35 pt].** Implement **WGAN-GP** with the SN-GAN
  CIFAR-10 ResNet architecture (page 17 of Miyato et al.), $z\in\mathbb{R}^{128}$,
  with `DepthToSpace`/`SpaceToDepth` up/down-sampling instead of transposed
  convs, ResBlockUp in G, no BatchNorm in D, global sum pooling. Hyperparameters
  are fully specified: Adam $\alpha=2\times10^{-4}$, $\beta_1=0$, $\beta_2=0.9$,
  $\lambda=10$, $n_{critic}=5$, batch 256, ≥25,000 steps, LR linearly annealed
  to 0. Deliverables: **Inception Score** (a utility is provided), **FID**
  (bonus 5 pt), discriminator loss curve, 100 samples.
- **Q3 "Quantization with GANs" [40 pt] — VQGAN.** (a) Vanilla **VQGAN** on
  CIFAR-10: VQ-VAE from HW2 plus a **patch-based discriminator** (8×8 patches),
  reporting discriminator losses, L2 losses, **LPIPS** losses and
  reconstructions; (b) **ViT-VQGAN** [15 pt].

That last question is the crux of the modern framing: **40% of Berkeley's GAN
homework is not about generating images with a GAN at all — it is about using an
adversarial loss as a perceptual term inside a tokenizer.**

### A5. MIT 6.S978 · Deep Generative Models (Kaiming He, Fall 2024)

- Site: <https://mit-6s978.github.io/>
- Schedule: <https://mit-6s978.github.io/schedule.html>
- GAN lecture slides: <https://mit-6s978.github.io/assets/pdfs/lec4_gan.pdf>

**Latest iteration: Fall 2024.** Graduate seminar: instructor lectures + guest
lectures + student paper seminars (3 papers per session, 20 min + 10 min QA),
bi-weekly problem sets (30%), final project (40%) with a **blog-format** report
"designed to be accessible to general audiences". Prereqs: 6.S898/6.7960 (DL)
plus a CV or NLP course. The problem sets themselves are not publicly linked —
**unverified**.

**GANs get exactly one of five instructor lectures**: L1 Introduction, L2 VAE,
L3 Autoregressive, **L4 GAN (Oct 3, 2024)**, L5 Energy-based / Score matching /
Diffusion. So GANs are the third of four model families, taught **before**
diffusion — but with two seminar sessions of GAN papers attached.

**The lecture is in three acts, and the third act is the important one:**

> Overview: • Generative Adversarial Networks (GAN) • Wasserstein GAN (W-GAN) •
> **Adversary as a Loss Function**

*Act 1 — GAN.* The motivation is **not** "likelihood ≠ sample quality" (that is
CS236's framing) but a critique of the reconstruction loss: "What's the
implication of a 'reconstruction' loss? Elements (e.g. pixels) are independently
distributed; each element follows a simple distribution
(Gaussian/Bernoulli/...). **Assumptions are too strict for high-dim variables.
Can we measure the distribution difference in another way?**" Then: "Generative
Adversarial Networks: representing distribution difference by a neural network."
There is a beautiful **side-by-side VAE/GAN block diagram** — VAE:
$p_{data}\to$ encoder $\to p_z \to$ decoder $\to p_g$ with a reconstruction
loss; GAN: $p_z\to$ generator $\to p_g$, compared against $p_{data}$ by a
discriminator. The min-max process is contrasted with "**EM's max-max
process**". D-step and G-step are each developed with "push to 1 / push to 0"
annotations; the non-saturating trick appears as "**a 'flip' trick**" with
"weak grad / strong grad" annotations for early training. Then the three
theoretical results (optimal $D$; $D_{JS}$; global optimum at $p_g=p_{data}$),
with JSD properties (symmetric, **bounded in $[0,\log 2]$** vs unbounded KL,
"more stable"). A **running MNIST example** shows D loss / G loss /
loss_fake / loss_real curves annotated with which player minimises or maximises
what, plus samples at epochs 1, 20, 50, 300, 500. Problems: hard to reach
equilibrium, vanishing gradients, mode collapse (citing Arjovsky & Bottou and
Metz's unrolled GANs). Demo pointer: **GAN Lab**,
<https://poloclub.github.io/ganlab/>.

*Act 2 — W-GAN.* Opens with a Reddit user's three-audience summary that is
worth stealing outright:

> For mathematicians: Wasserstein distance, instead of JS divergence.
> For engineers: remove logarithms; clip weights.
> For laymen: **art critic, instead of forgery expert**.

Then: JSD is constant ($\log 2$) on disjoint supports, hence no gradient; a
"can we have a measure like this?" slide; a **shovelful-by-shovelful earth-mover
animation** in 1-D (credited to Lilian Weng's "From GAN to WGAN"); 1-Wasserstein
in 1-D discrete, then 1-D continuous via CDFs, then high-dimensional via
couplings $\gamma(x,y)$; Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality; Lipschitz continuity as
a bounded gradient; the WGAN objective with weight clipping to $[-0.01, 0.01]$;
and a direct **W-GAN vs original GAN objective diff** ("clip weights" / "remove
logarithms"). Ends with one slide each on **LSGAN and EBGAN**.

*Act 3 — "Adversary as a Loss Function."* This is the modern re-framing, and no
other course states it as crisply:

> • GAN essentially defines an **adversarial loss function**
> • Input to networks is not necessarily random/noise
> • Beyond L2/L1: adversarial loss encourages output to look "realistic"
> • Combined with L2/L1: reconstruction loss largely stabilizes training

Then: encoder→generator→discriminator diagrams with "reconstruction loss +
adversarial loss"; the discriminator relabelled "**a parameterized loss
function, trained alternately**"; **SRGAN** ("worse PSNR, but better visual
quality"); **Context Encoders**; **pix2pix**; **CycleGAN**; and finally
**VQ-VAE → VQ-GAN**:

> To be precise: VQ-GAN = VQ-VAE + Adv Loss + Perceptual Loss. w/o VQ, it's VAE
> + Adv Loss + Perceptual Loss. **Both are the de facto tokenizers in image
> generation** — w/ VQ: e.g. Autoregressive Models; w/o VQ: e.g. Diffusion
> Models. **Commercial models (e.g. Stable Diffusion, Sora) use these
> tokenizers.**

Main references listed: just two — Goodfellow et al. 2014 and Arjovsky et al.
2017.

**Seminar reading lists (this is where the frontier shows up).** Two sessions
of GAN papers follow the lecture:

- **Week 6, Tue 10/08/2024 — "Reading: GAN 1":** StyleGAN-T (ICML 2023);
  "Scaling up GANs for Text-to-Image Synthesis" (GigaGAN, CVPR 2023);
  **"Distilling Diffusion Models into Conditional GANs" (Kang et al., ECCV
  2024)**; optional: Z. Tu, "Learning Generative Models via Discriminative
  Approaches" (CVPR 2007).
- **Week 6, Thu 10/10/2024 — "Reading: GAN 2":** **"N. Huang et al., The GAN is
  dead; long live the GAN! A Modern GAN Baseline"** (i.e. **R3GAN**), linked to
  <https://openreview.net/pdf/9197a4074900f763675c3c896d7c4ca3402a3c55.pdf>;
  "Diffusion-GAN: Training GANs with Diffusion" (ICLR 2023); "GANs Settle
  Scores!".

**This is the only course in the entire survey that has R3GAN on its syllabus**
(see synthesis §4). Note the schedule mislabels it "ICML 2024"; it is NeurIPS
2024 / arXiv 2501.05441.

## Part B — Synthesis

### B1. The common core: what every serious 2024+ course still teaches

Across every course whose GAN materials were actually inspectable, the
irreducible core is remarkably narrow and stable — five items:

1. **The implicit / likelihood-free generator.** $z \sim p(z)$, $x = G_\theta(z)$,
   no tractable density. Always contrasted against the likelihood-based families
   already taught (autoregressive, VAE, flows). Stanford CS236 phrases it as
   "similar to a flow model, but $G_\theta$ need not be invertible"; Berkeley as
   "What if all we care about is sampling?"; Kaiming He as "representing
   distribution difference by a neural network".
2. **The two-player minimax objective**
   $\min_G \max_D \mathbb{E}_{p_{data}}[\log D(x)] + \mathbb{E}_{p_z}[\log(1-D(G(z)))]$,
   with the alternating-SGD training loop. Universal.
3. **The optimal discriminator $D^*(x) = p_{data}/(p_{data}+p_G)$ and the JSD
   result** $V(G, D^*) = 2 D_{JS}(p_{data}\|p_G) - \log 4$, with global optimum
   at $p_G = p_{data}$. Present in CS236, MIT 6.S978, Berkeley, CMU 11-785, ETH,
   Tübingen, TUM, PKU. Notably, **CS231n now states the JSD result with
   "(Proof omitted)"** and CMU 10-423 omits it entirely — the derivation is the
   first thing to go when a course compresses.
4. **The saturation problem and the non-saturating loss**
   $L^{(G)} = -\mathbb{E}_z \log D(G(z))$. Universal *except* CS236, which
   omits it (an outlier worth noting). Kaiming He calls it "a 'flip' trick";
   CS231n gives it three build-up slides; Berkeley makes it a graded homework
   A/B against the saturating version.
5. **The pathologies**: no single loss to monitor / no stopping criterion,
   training instability, and **mode collapse** — almost always illustrated with
   a **2-D mixture-of-Gaussians toy** (CS236, Berkeley citing Metz et al.,
   Tübingen with a 1-D Gaussian, MIT with GAN Lab).

Two more are *near*-core, present in most but not all:

6. **WGAN** — the disjoint-support argument ($D_{JS} = \log 2$ with no gradient),
   Wasserstein/Earth-Mover distance, Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality,
   Lipschitz enforcement by weight clipping or gradient penalty. Present in
   CS236, Berkeley, MIT, CMU 16-726, TUM, Tübingen, Fleuret/UNIGE, PKU, KAIST,
   IIT-M, Coursera. Absent from CS231n, CMU 10-423, ETH Machine Perception,
   UvA 2024.
7. **FID (and IS as its flawed predecessor)** — Berkeley, CMU 16-726, TUM,
   Tübingen, Coursera. Absent from CS236's GAN lectures (deferred to a separate
   evaluation lecture), CS231n, and PKU.

**DCGAN, CycleGAN and pix2pix** are the near-universal *applications*; StyleGAN
appears as a named milestone in roughly half the courses, but only TUM, CMU
16-726 and ETH-MP go into the architecture.

### B2. What has been dropped since ~2020, and how GANs are now framed

**The shrinkage is real, and it is measurable in four same-institution pairs:**

| Course | ~2019/2020 | 2024/2025 |
|---|---|---|
| Berkeley CS294-158 | 2 GAN lectures (L5, L6); **no diffusion** | 1 GAN lecture (L5); diffusion is L6 |
| Stanford CS231n | GANs = Assignment 3 Q5 (MNIST: vanilla → LSGAN → DCGAN) | **No GAN assignment**; Q3 is DDPM. GANs = 28 slides of one lecture |
| Georgia Tech CS 7643 | (SP24) standalone "Generative Models Part I: GANs", before diffusion | (F25) "GANs **and** Self-Supervised Learning", merged, **after** VAE and diffusion |
| fast.ai | Lesson 12: DarkNet + GANs incl. WGAN | **Zero** GAN lessons; multi-lesson diffusion build-up |
| Toronto CSC412 | (older) GAN content | **No GAN lecture**; diffusion in wk 12 |
| UMich EECS 498 | A6 = VAE + GAN on MNIST (W2022) | Course discontinued; successor DeepRob has none |
| KAIST AI602 | (F23) generative-models lecture | (S25) foundation models only; no GAN/VAE/diffusion |
| Stanford CS236G | A whole GAN-only course (W2021) | **Discontinued** |
| PKU Deep Generative Models | 4 GAN lectures (S2022) | **Discontinued after 2022** |

**What specifically got dropped:**

- **The GAN-zoo architecture tour.** BigGAN, SAGAN, Progressive GAN, SN-GAN,
  VIB-GAN, BiGAN/BigBiGAN, InfoGAN, StarGAN. Berkeley SP24 and TUM still carry
  them; almost nobody else does.
- **The training-tricks bestiary** (minibatch discrimination, feature matching,
  historical averaging, virtual batch norm, one-sided label smoothing). Only
  Berkeley SP24 retains the full Salimans-et-al. list.
- **f-GANs / Fenchel duality.** Now essentially only Stanford CS236 and its
  Cornell derivative. Every European and Asian course surveyed omits it.
- **From-scratch GAN homeworks in generalist DL/CV courses.** CS231n, UMich,
  fast.ai all removed theirs. Where a GAN assignment survives, it is in a
  specialist course (Berkeley CS294-158, CMU 16-726, KAIST, Toronto CSC413).
- **The derivation itself, in compressed courses** — CS231n's "(Proof omitted)";
  CMU 10-423's objective-without-derivation.

**How GANs' place is now framed — three distinct postures, all present in the
2024+ corpus:**

1. **Historical milestone.** CS231n's summary slide is the archetype: "These
   were the go-to generative models from ~2016 – 2021", with cons listed as
   unstable / no loss curve / hard to scale. Georgia Tech's merged-and-last
   lecture and U. Tokyo's one-session-in-ten encode the same judgement
   structurally.
2. **A necessary rung on the divergence ladder.** The theory-first courses
   (CS236, Cornell, ETH-MP, CMU 11-785, Tübingen) keep GANs because they are
   where the course teaches *"a divergence you cannot compute can be estimated
   by a learned critic"* — the same move that later powers score matching,
   contrastive learning, and density-ratio estimation. GANs are load-bearing
   pedagogy even if nobody trains one.
3. **A still-live engineering tool: the adversarial loss.** This is the
   fastest-growing framing and the most important one for a 2026 rewrite.
   Kaiming He devotes a third of his GAN lecture to it ("Adversary as a Loss
   Function": the discriminator is "a parameterized loss function, trained
   alternately"; SRGAN, Context Encoders, pix2pix, CycleGAN; and then **VQ-GAN =
   VQ-VAE + Adv Loss + Perceptual Loss**, "the de facto tokenizers in image
   generation… Commercial models (e.g. Stable Diffusion, Sora) use these
   tokenizers"). Berkeley makes this **40% of the GAN homework** (VQGAN /
   ViT-VQGAN with a patch discriminator and LPIPS). And MIT's seminar list
   assigns **"Distilling Diffusion Models into Conditional GANs"** (Kang et al.,
   ECCV 2024) — adversarial losses as the mechanism for few-step diffusion
   distillation.

The compact summary: **GANs have stopped being taught as *the* way to generate
images and are now taught as (a) the origin of the learned-divergence idea and
(b) the source of an adversarial loss term that is still in every production
image tokenizer.**

### B3. Rare-but-excellent ideas worth stealing

Ranked by how much a d2l chapter would gain:

1. **Kaiming He's three-act structure** (MIT 6.S978 L4): *GAN → W-GAN →
   Adversary as a Loss Function*, ending at VQ-GAN and "Stable Diffusion and
   Sora use these tokenizers". This single arc solves the "why teach GANs in
   2026?" problem better than anything else found: it starts from theory and
   lands on something the reader is definitely using today.
2. **Berkeley HW3 Q1: the minimax-vs-non-saturating A/B on 1-D data.** Same
   3-layer 128-unit MLP, same toy target; train once with
   $\log(1-D(G(z)))$ and once with $-\log D(G(z))$; plot samples at epoch 1,
   final samples, and loss curves for both. It converts an abstract gradient
   argument into two pictures. Cheap to run on CPU — ideal for a notebook.
3. **CS236's three-case argument that likelihood ≠ sample quality**, especially
   Case 2: the mixture $0.01 p_{data} + 0.99 p_{noise}$ whose log-likelihood is
   within $\log 100$ of optimal — a *constant* — while $\log p_{data}$ grows
   like $n$. Three lines of algebra that justify the whole chapter's existence.
4. **CMU 16-726 HW3's GAN-vs-diffusion head-to-head on identical data.**
   Students train a DCGAN and a DDPM on the *same* grumpy-cat images and write a
   comparison. Perfectly matched to a book that has both chapters.
5. **Kaiming He's VAE/GAN block-diagram diptych** — the same $p_{data}$,
   $p_z$, $p_g$ boxes rearranged, with "reconstruction loss" replaced by
   "discriminator". One figure that makes GANs feel inevitable given VAEs.
6. **The "art critic vs forgery expert" gloss on WGAN**, plus the
   three-audience summary ("for mathematicians / for engineers / for laymen").
   Also He's shovelful-by-shovelful 1-D earth-mover animation before any
   Kantorovich–Rubinstein algebra.
7. **GAN Lab** (<https://poloclub.github.io/ganlab/>) — the discriminator
   heatmap + generator-manifold-as-warped-grid + gradient-arrow visualisation.
   Used by both Berkeley and MIT. A static reproduction of those three panels on
   a 2-D toy would be an excellent d2l figure.
8. **Berkeley's `glob.glob('*.jpg')` "sampler"** — show the trivial
   resample-the-training-set generator first, then argue what's missing
   (interpolation, unseen strokes/poses). Best available motivation for *why a
   model at all*.
9. **Berkeley's Inception-Score refutation**: "a list of 1000 images (one of
   each class) can obtain perfect Inception Score". One sentence that teaches
   why FID replaced IS.
10. **CMU 16-726's evaluation lecture caveats**: the aliased-resizing FID pitfall
    (Parmar CVPR 2022) and the ImageNet-class dependence of FID
    (Kynkäänniemi ICLR 2023). Almost nobody teaches that FID is *itself*
    fragile.
11. **CMU 11-785's "DILLAF loss"** ("Does It Look Like A Face") as the
    throwaway name for the learned critic — a memorable device for the
    "hand-designed loss → learned loss" transition.
12. **Tübingen's five named mode-collapse mitigations** presented as a menu
    (minibatch discrimination, unrolling, experience replay, multiple GANs,
    WGAN/GP) rather than as a bag of tricks.
13. **CMU 16-726 HW5's GAN inversion** ($z^* = \arg\min_z L(G(z),x)$ over
    $z$ / $w$ / $w^+$) — teaches that an implicit model *has* no encoder and
    what you do about it, connecting straight to CS236's BiGAN material.

### B4. Has anyone picked up R3GAN / relativistic GANs?

**Almost nobody — with exactly one confirmed exception.**

**MIT 6.S978 (Kaiming He, Fall 2024) assigns R3GAN as a student-seminar paper.**
The schedule's Week 6, Thursday 10/10/2024 "Reading: GAN 2" list is:

> 1. N. Huang et al., *The GAN is dead; long live the GAN! A Modern GAN
>    Baseline* — <https://openreview.net/pdf/9197a4074900f763675c3c896d7c4ca3402a3c55.pdf>
> 2. Z. Wang et al., *Diffusion-GAN: Training GANs with Diffusion*, ICLR 2023
> 3. S. Asokan et al., *GANs Settle Scores!*

(<https://mit-6s978.github.io/schedule.html>; the page mislabels the venue as
"ICML 2024" — it is NeurIPS 2024 / arXiv 2501.05441.) Note that this is a
*seminar reading*, presented by students, **not** instructor lecture content:
He's own L4 slides contain no relativistic-loss material and list only two main
references (Goodfellow 2014, Arjovsky 2017).

Everywhere else the answer is no, verified by full-text grep of the slide decks
where available:

- Stanford CS236 L9/L10, Berkeley CS294-158 L5, CS231n 2025 L14: **zero**
  occurrences of "relativistic" or "R3GAN".
- CMU, ETH, TUM, Tübingen, EPFL/UNIGE, PKU, KAIST, Coursera, Hugging Face:
  none found by the regional surveys.
- **The sharpest irony: Volodymyr Kuleshov is an R3GAN co-author and teaches
  Cornell CS 6785, and there is no public evidence his own course has adopted
  it.** His Spring 2024 materials are Canvas-gated, so this is *unverified*
  rather than confirmed-absent; but the public `dgm-website` lecture list
  (lectures 9–10) is structurally unchanged from the pre-2024 f-GAN / BiGAN /
  CycleGAN syllabus.

**Interpretation.** The teaching canon lags the literature by roughly 3–5 years,
and R3GAN (Jan 2025 arXiv posting) has had one academic year to propagate. It is
also arriving *into a declining topic*, which slows adoption further: the
courses most likely to add a modern GAN baseline are exactly the ones cutting
GAN time. **This is an opportunity for the d2l chapter**: a textbook that teaches
the regularized relativistic loss (RpGAN + $R_1$ + $R_2$) as *the* modern
default, with a clean ResNet generator and no StyleGAN tricks, would be ahead of
every course surveyed. The neighbouring frontier papers that *are* being
assigned (Diffusion-GAN, GANs-Settle-Scores, Distilling-Diffusion-into-GANs)
point the same way: adversarial objectives are being re-derived in the
score/diffusion language.

### B5. Prerequisites assumed

Three distinct tiers emerged:

**Tier 1 — the survey tier** (CS231n, MIT 6.S191, CMU 10-423, EPFL CS-433,
Cambridge 4F10, Hugging Face, UvA 2024). Assumes only: expectation,
cross-entropy / binary logistic regression, SGD, conv nets. The JSD result is
stated, not derived. **No convex duality, no optimal transport, no RKHS.**

**Tier 2 — the standard graduate tier** (MIT 6.S978, Berkeley CS294-158,
Tübingen, TUM, CMU 11-785, KAIST, PKU, UNIGE/Fleuret, IIT-M). Adds:
KL divergence and its asymmetry, Jensen's inequality, the JSD derivation, and —
for the WGAN section — **couplings/joint distributions with fixed marginals,
Lipschitz continuity, and Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality stated (not proved)**.
Optimal transport is introduced *just in time*, in one or two slides, usually via
a 1-D earth-mover picture; no measure theory. Kaiming He's version explicitly
routes around the math ("for engineers: remove logarithms, clip weights").

**Tier 3 — the divergence-theory tier** (Stanford CS236, Cornell CS 6785, and
Gatsby/Gretton). Genuinely assumes **convex analysis**: convexity, lower
semicontinuity, the **Fenchel conjugate** $f^*(t) = \sup_u (ut - f(u))$,
biconjugation $f^{**} \le f$ with equality under convexity+lsc, i.e. **strong
duality** — all of which CS236 states and uses on slide 8 of Lecture 10 to
derive f-GAN. Gretton's lineage additionally assumes **RKHS / kernel mean
embeddings** to present GANs as MMD/IPM minimisation — this is the *only* place
RKHS machinery appears in the corpus, and it is guest-lecture rather than
regular-module material.

**Statistical framing prerequisites** worth noting: CS236 (and Cornell) build
the whole lecture on **two-sample hypothesis testing** ($H_0: P=Q$, test
statistic, likelihood-free), which is a genuinely different entry point from the
"minimax game" framing everyone else uses, and requires the reader to know what
a test statistic is.

**Implication for d2l.** Tier 2 is the right target: derive the optimal
discriminator and JSD honestly; introduce Wasserstein via a 1-D picture and
state Kantorovich–Rubinstein; treat f-GAN/Fenchel duality as optional material
(and note that d2l's own math appendix — ch. 25 Optimization has convex-duality
machinery, ch. 27 Information Theory has the divergences — makes it cheap to
*reference* rather than re-derive).

## Part C — Practical implications for the d2l GAN chapter

Not requested, but falls directly out of the evidence:

1. **Lead with the likelihood-free argument, not the counterfeiter story.**
   CS236's three cases (optimal / good-likelihood-bad-samples /
   good-samples-bad-likelihood) are the strongest opening found and connect the
   chapter to the book's existing MLE material.
2. **Teach the non-saturating loss as the default and demonstrate why**, with
   the Berkeley 1-D A/B. Do not repeat CS236's omission.
3. **Two teaching datasets, both CPU-cheap**: a 2-D mixture-of-Gaussians toy
   (for mode collapse and the discriminator heatmap) and one small image set.
   The corpus uses MNIST (CS231n historical, MIT running example), CIFAR-10
   (Berkeley), and custom cats (CMU). The existing d2l Pokemon dataset is
   defensible; a 2-D toy is *mandatory* given how universally it is used.
4. **Do teach FID, and teach that FID is fragile** (Parmar 2022, Kynkäänniemi
   2023) — almost no course does, and it is a differentiator.
5. **Land the chapter on "adversary as a loss function"**: SRGAN → pix2pix →
   VQ-GAN → diffusion distillation. This is where the field actually is, it is
   what Kaiming He and Berkeley now teach, and it gives the chapter a live
   reason to exist next to the diffusion chapter.
6. **Consider R3GAN as the chapter's "modern baseline"** — no course teaches it
   yet, the paper is explicitly written as a pedagogical simplification
   ("a roadmap of simplification and modernization"), and it would let the
   chapter present *one* well-behaved recipe instead of a bag of tricks.
7. **Cross-link, don't duplicate**: WGAN's Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality and
   f-GAN's Fenchel conjugate both have homes in the math appendix; the courses
   that do this well (CS236) spend one slide each because the machinery is
   assumed.
